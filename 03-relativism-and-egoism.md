# Chapter 3 — Relativism and Egoism

Two thoughts arrive early in almost everyone's moral life, usually in late adolescence and often in the same week. The first: *who's to say?* Morals differ from culture to culture, so perhaps right and wrong are just local customs dressed up as universal truths. The second: *everybody's really just looking out for number one.* Behind every kind act there is a payoff, so perhaps morality is self-interest in disguise. Both thoughts feel tough-minded, and both seem to dissolve ethics before it starts. Both are also very old: Herodotus, Protagoras, Thrasymachus and Callicles had them in the fifth century BCE, and Hobbes gave the second its most powerful modern form. This chapter takes them seriously. It separates the several different claims that travel under the names "relativism" and "egoism," tests the arguments for each, and shows why most philosophers think the crude versions fail and the refined versions teach something worth keeping. Previous: [Chapter 2 — Moral Reasoning](02-moral-reasoning.md). Next: [Chapter 4 — Divine Command and Natural Law](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md).

## The experiment of Darius

The founding document of moral relativism is a story about funerals. Herodotus, the fifth-century Greek historian who traveled the eastern Mediterranean collecting customs the way other people collect coins, tells it while explaining why the Persian king Cambyses must have been insane: Cambyses mocked the religious rites of the Egyptians, and only a madman, Herodotus thinks, would do that.

> For if one should propose to all men a choice, bidding them select the best customs from all the customs that there are, each race of men, after examining them all, would select those of his own people; thus all think that their own customs are by far the best: and so it is not likely that any but a madman would make a jest of such things.
>
> — Herodotus, *The History* III.38 (tr. G. C. Macaulay)

Then comes the proof, an early piece of experimental anthropology:

> Dareios in the course of his reign summoned those of the Hellenes who were present in his land, and asked them for what price they would consent to eat up their fathers when they died; and they answered that for no price would they do so. After this Dareios summoned those Indians who are called Callatians, who eat their parents, and asked them in presence of the Hellenes, who understood what they said by help of an interpreter, for what payment they would consent to consume with fire the bodies of their fathers when they died; and they cried out aloud and bade him keep silence from such words. Thus then these things are established by usage, and I think that Pindar spoke rightly in his verse, when he said that "of all things law is king."
>
> — Herodotus, *The History* III.38 (tr. G. C. Macaulay)

The Greek word Pindar used, *nomos*, means law, custom and convention all at once. "Nomos is king" became a slogan. Herodotus delighted in collecting cases. His Egyptians, he reports, do nearly everything backwards:

> for among them the women frequent the market and carry on trade, while the men remain at home and weave; and whereas others weave pushing the woof upwards, the Egyptians push it downwards: the men carry their burdens upon their heads and the women upon their shoulders: the women make water standing up and the men crouching down
>
> — Herodotus, *The History* II.35 (tr. G. C. Macaulay)

Notice three things before drawing conclusions. First, the examples mix kinds of norm: which way you weave is pure convention, while treatment of the dead involves respect, piety and the duties of children. Second, the Greeks and the Callatians *agree* that the dead father must be honored; they disagree about what honoring him requires. Third, Herodotus draws a practical moral, not a metaethical one. He does not say that nothing is really right. He says that mocking other people's sacred customs is the act of a madman, and that claim, as will become clear, is not relative to anything.

## Nomos and physis: the Greek debate

Herodotus's contemporaries turned his observations into a philosophical controversy, framed by the contrast between *nomos* (convention) and *physis* (nature). Is justice natural, binding everyone like the law of gravity, or is it a human artifact, varying from city to city like the coinage? The Sophists, itinerant teachers who sold training in persuasion, took up the conventionalist side in several different ways.

**Protagoras** (c. 490–420 BCE) is the patron saint of relativism. Only a few sentences of his survive, and the most famous is reported by Plato:

> Man, he says, is the measure of all things, of the existence of things that are, and of the non-existence of things that are not
>
> — Plato, *Theaetetus* 152a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Plato's Socrates illustrates with the wind: one person finds it chilly, another warm. Is the wind in itself cold? Protagoras answers that it is cold *for* the one and not cold *for* the other, and there is no further fact. Later in the dialogue Socrates imagines Protagoras rising from the dead to defend himself, and the defense applies the doctrine to ethics and politics:

> for whatever appears to a state to be just and fair, so long as it is regarded as such, is just and fair to it; but the teacher of wisdom causes the good to take the place of the evil, both in appearance and in reality.
>
> — Plato, *Theaetetus* 167c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

This is a startlingly modern position: whatever Athens takes to be just *is* just for Athens. But Protagoras still wants to be a well-paid teacher, so he adds that some beliefs are *better* than others, even if none is truer. Socrates pounces: even Protagoreans admit that a city can be wrong about what is *expedient* for it, yet on justice they insist that "the truth is that which is agreed on at the time of the agreement, and as long as the agreement lasts" (172b), and this, he adds, is the view of many who do not accept the rest of Protagoras. The pattern persists today: plenty of people who think science discovers facts think ethics only records agreements.

Not every Sophist drew tolerant conclusions. In Plato's *Gorgias*, the young politician **Callicles** uses the same contrast between nature and convention to reach the opposite verdict. Conventional justice, he argues, is a trick of the weak:

> The reason, as I conceive, is that the makers of laws are the majority who are weak; and they make laws and distribute praises and censures with a view to themselves and to their own interests; and they terrify the stronger sort of men, and those who are able to get the better of them, in order that they may not get the better of them; and they say, that dishonesty is shameful and unjust; meaning, by the word injustice, the desire of a man to have more than his neighbours; for knowing their own inferiority, I suspect that they are too glad of equality.
>
> — Plato, *Gorgias* 483b–c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Nature, by contrast, teaches something else:

> nature herself intimates that it is just for the better to have more than the worse, the more powerful than the weaker; and in many ways she shows, among men as well as among animals, and indeed among whole cities and races, that justice consists in the superior ruling over and having more than the inferior.
>
> — Plato, *Gorgias* 483d (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Callicles then quotes the very same line of Pindar that Herodotus quoted, "law is the king of all," and reads it as a celebration of might. The irony is instructive. The same observation, that customs vary, was used by Herodotus to recommend respect for others and by Callicles to recommend contempt for the rules. The observation alone settles nothing; everything depends on what you add to it. Callicles is not a relativist at all. He believes there is a natural justice, and that it favors the strong. (Nietzsche's version of this thought appears in [Chapter 15](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md).)

Six centuries later the Pyrrhonian skeptic **Sextus Empiricus** turned diversity into a method. His "tenth mode" sets customs, laws, myths and doctrines against each other (some Ethiopians tattoo newborns; the Tauri sacrificed strangers to Artemis) and concludes not that each custom is right for its people but that we cannot tell which is right at all:

> Since, however, such anomaly of things is shown by this Trope also, we shall not be able to say what objects are by nature, but only what each thing appears to be like, according to this or that school, or this or that law, or this or that custom, or according to each of the other conditions.
>
> — Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* I.163 (tr. Mary Mills Patrick)

So antiquity drew four different conclusions from the same facts of diversity: tolerance (Herodotus), relativism about justice (Protagoras), suspension of judgment (Sextus) and the natural right of the strong (Callicles). Whenever someone says "cultures disagree, *therefore* …", the "therefore" needs a further premise, and that premise decides where you end up. ([Chapter 6](06-hellenistic-ethics.md) returns to the skeptics, who thought suspension of judgment would bring peace of mind.)

## Cultural relativism, stated carefully

Modern cultural relativism grew out of nineteenth- and twentieth-century anthropology. The American sociologist William Graham Sumner gave it a classic statement in *Folkways* (1906). Sumner coined the term **ethnocentrism**:

> Each group thinks its own folkways the only right ones, and if it observes that other groups have other folkways, these excite its scorn.
>
> — William Graham Sumner, *Folkways* §15 (1906)

and drew a sweeping conclusion about what "right" means:

> The notion of right is in the folkways. It is not outside of them, of independent origin, and brought to them to test them. In the folkways, whatever is, is right.
>
> — William Graham Sumner, *Folkways* §31 (1906)

Later he put it more bluntly still, in a chapter title: "the mores can make anything right." The anthropologist Ruth Benedict made a similar case in "Anthropology and the Abnormal" (1934), arguing that what counts as normal, and as good, is fixed by each culture's selection from the "great arc" of possible human behavior, so that "morally good" is simply a name for habits a society happens to approve.

It is worth being precise, because the word "relativism" covers at least four distinct claims:

1. **Descriptive relativism (the diversity thesis).** Different societies hold different moral beliefs. This is an empirical claim, and in some form it is plainly true.
2. **The dependency thesis.** What a society believes about morality is shaped by its environment, history, economy and religion. Also empirical, and also largely true.
3. **Metaethical relativism.** The *truth* of moral claims is relative to a society's code. "Infanticide is wrong" is true-for-us and false-for-them, and there is no further, non-relative fact about which code is better. This is a philosophical claim about the nature of moral truth.
4. **Normative relativism (the tolerance thesis).** It is wrong to judge, condemn or interfere with the practices of other cultures.

A fifth view is often confused with these: **subjectivism**, the view that moral truth is relative to each *individual's* attitudes. Subjectivism faces most of the same objections as cultural relativism, plus some of its own (it implies that no two people ever really disagree about morality, since each only reports their own feelings). It is discussed further in [Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md).

The first two theses are claims for anthropology. The third and fourth are claims for philosophy. Most of the confusion in popular debates comes from treating (1) as if it proved (3), and (3) as if it implied (4).

## The cultural differences argument

James Rachels, in a much-reprinted textbook chapter, called the central inference **the cultural differences argument**:

1. Different cultures have different moral codes.
2. Therefore, there is no objective truth in morality. Right and wrong are only matters of opinion, and opinions vary from culture to culture.

Put this way, the argument is invalid. The premise is about what people *believe*; the conclusion is about what is *true*. Belief and truth come apart everywhere else. Some cultures have believed the earth is flat and others that it is round; it does not follow that there is no fact about the shape of the earth. Some cultures attributed disease to witchcraft and others to microbes; it does not follow that there is no fact about the cause of disease. The bare fact of disagreement shows only that at least one side is wrong, or that the question is harder than it looks.

Rachels is careful to say what he is *not* claiming. Showing that an argument is invalid does not show that its conclusion is false. There might be no objective moral truth for some other reason. The point is narrower: diversity alone does not establish relativism. To get from (1) to (3) you need a further premise, such as "if there were objective moral truths, careful inquirers would converge on them." That premise may be defensible (J. L. Mackie's argument from relativity, discussed in [Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md) and in [Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md), tries to defend something like it), but it is now a premise about the best *explanation* of disagreement, and it can be argued about.

## What would follow if relativism were true

A second strategy tests metaethical relativism by its consequences. If the truth of moral claims were fixed by each society's code, several things would follow that almost no one, including most people who call themselves relativists, can accept.

**We could not say that another society's practices are worse than ours.** We could not say that slavery in the antebellum American South was wrong, only that it was wrong-by-our-code and right-by-theirs. We could not say that the Taliban's ban on girls' education is unjust, only that it differs. Relativists often present this as a gain in humility. But it extends to every practice, including genocide, torture and the persecution of minorities, so it is less humility than abdication.

**We could settle moral questions by consulting a poll.** If right means "approved by my society," moral reasoning of the kind Chapter 2 described would be pointless except as a forecast of public opinion.

**Moral reform would be a contradiction.** The abolitionist and the suffragist said, in effect, "our society's code is wrong." On the relativist's definition that sentence is false at the moment it is uttered. Reformers are wrong until they win, and then become right without changing their minds. Relativism can describe moral change, but it cannot call the abolition of slavery **progress** rather than a change in fashion.

**"Society" is indeterminate.** A Muslim feminist in Paris belongs to several overlapping moral communities: French, Muslim, feminist, her profession, her generation. When their codes conflict, which one makes things right for her? Any answer looks arbitrary, and if she may choose, relativism has collapsed into subjectivism.

**The tolerance thesis undercuts itself.** "Every culture ought to tolerate other cultures" is a universal moral claim. If relativism is true, it holds only for cultures whose codes contain it, and a culture whose code says "conquer the unbelievers" acts rightly, by its own lights, in conquering. Tolerance cannot be *derived* from relativism. If you think tolerance is good for everyone, you are not a relativist about tolerance. Bernard Williams (*Morality*, 1972) called the inference from relativism to tolerance "the anthropologist's heresy" and judged it possibly the most absurd view ever advanced in moral philosophy.

Herodotus never made this mistake. He did not say that mocking customs is wrong-for-Persians. He said it is madness, full stop.

## How deep does disagreement go?

The third strategy questions the premise itself. Return to the Greeks and Callatians: both were honoring their dead fathers. They differed about what honor requires, probably because of different beliefs about the body and the dead. A disagreement in practice can rest on agreement in **values** combined with disagreement in **facts** or circumstances.

Rachels gives a harder example, drawn from older ethnographies (themselves disputed) reporting infanticide among Inuit communities in times of scarcity. On his reading, the practice did not reflect a lower valuation of children but a nomadic life at the edge of survival, in which a mother could carry and nurse only one infant at a time and the alternative might be the death of the family. A practice that looks like indifference to life may express the very value we hold, under circumstances we have never faced.

Some values, moreover, seem **necessary for any society to exist at all**. A group in which no one cared for infants would die out; one in which lying was the norm could not coordinate anything; one with no restraint on killing its own members would not hold together. Empirical work supports this. A 2019 study by Oliver Scott Curry and colleagues coded ethnographies from sixty societies and found that seven cooperative behaviors (helping kin, helping your group, reciprocating, bravery, deference to superiors, fair division and respect for prior possession) were rated morally good wherever they were mentioned, and never as bad.

None of this shows that there is *no* deep disagreement. Cultures differ about animals, about individual versus community, about sexuality, authority, honor and purity, and some of these differences may be bedrock. But humanity is not a set of moral islands with nothing in common. Both the disagreement and the core of agreement need explaining.

## What relativism gets right

**Ethnocentrism is a real error.** Sumner and Herodotus were right that every group tends to treat its own ways as the only right ones. The history of colonialism is full of missionaries and administrators who mistook their own etiquette for the moral law. Where a difference really is conventional (greetings, dress, funeral rites), respect is the right response.

**Some moral truths are relative to circumstances.** "Drive on the left" is true in Britain and false in France, because one non-relative principle (do not endanger others) yields different requirements under different conventions. The objectivist can say that what is right depends on the situation without saying it depends on what people in the situation believe.

**Sophisticated relativisms survive.** Gilbert Harman ("Moral Relativism Defended," 1975) argued that judgments of the form "he ought not to have done it" presuppose a tacit agreement between speaker and agent, so they make no sense about a hit-man who shares none of our agreements, though we can still call him evil. David Wong (*Natural Moralities*, 2006) defends **pluralistic relativism**: human nature and the needs of cooperation rule out many moralities but leave more than one adequate morality standing. Williams ("The Truth in Relativism," 1975) proposed a **relativism of distance**: judgments about ways of life that are not real options for us, such as those of remote past societies, may lose their point. These are serious positions, and far more modest than "the mores can make anything right."

## From "who's to say?" to "what's in it for me?"

The relativist doubts that morality has *authority*; the egoist doubts that it has any *motive* behind it other than self-interest. Thrasymachus, in Book I of Plato's *Republic*, combines the two: justice is a convention imposed by the powerful, and anyone who sees through it will pursue his own advantage:

> justice and the just are in reality another's good; that is to say, the interest of the ruler and stronger, and the loss of the subject and servant; and injustice the opposite
>
> — Plato, *Republic* I, 343c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

His evidence sounds as if it came from a modern tax office:

> when there is an income-tax, the just man will pay more and the unjust less on the same amount of income; and when there is anything to be received the one gains nothing and the other much.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* I, 343d (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Chapter 1 followed Glaucon's sharper version of this challenge, the ring of Gyges. Here the job is to separate the two egoisms that Thrasymachus runs together:

- **Psychological egoism** is a *descriptive* theory of motivation: every human action is ultimately motivated by self-interest. People *cannot* act otherwise.
- **Ethical egoism** is a *normative* theory: each person *ought* to do whatever best promotes their own interest.

The first says what we do; the second says what we should do. They are logically independent. If psychological egoism were true, ethical egoism would be idle (there is no point telling people to do what they cannot help doing) and every other ethical theory would be impossible to follow. If psychological egoism is false, ethical egoism becomes a real option that has to be argued for.

## Psychological egoism: Hobbes and the maxim-makers

The great modern psychological egoist, on the traditional reading, is Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679). (Some scholars, such as Bernard Gert, argue that Hobbes held only that self-interest is the strongest motive, not the only one; but the traditional reading is the one his critics, including Butler, attacked.) *Leviathan* begins with a mechanical psychology in which human beings are driven by appetites and aversions, and moral words simply record them:

> But whatsoever is the object of any mans Appetite or Desire; that is it, which he for his part calleth Good: And the object of his Hate, and Aversion, evill; And of his contempt, Vile, and Inconsiderable. For these words of Good, evill, and Contemptible, are ever used with relation to the person that useth them: There being nothing simply and absolutely so
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* ch. 6 (1651)

(Hobbes kept his idiosyncratic spelling; so does this course.) This is a relativism about "good," and Hobbes pairs it with an egoism about action:

> For it is a voluntary act: and of the voluntary acts of every man, the object is some Good To Himselfe.
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* ch. 14 (1651)

What about the obvious counterexamples, the sympathetic emotions? Hobbes redescribes them. Pity, on his account, is really fear for yourself:

> Griefe, for the calamity of another is PITTY; and ariseth from the imagination that the like calamity may befall himselfe; and therefore is called also COMPASSION, and in the phrase of this present time a FELLOW-FEELING
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* ch. 6 (1651)

John Aubrey tells how a clergyman saw Hobbes give money to a beggar and asked whether he would have done so if Christ had not commanded it. Hobbes replied that the old man's misery pained him, so the alms relieved them both. (A similar, probably apocryphal story has Abraham Lincoln rescuing piglets from a mud-hole "for his own peace of mind.") The stories capture the egoist's favorite move: find a benefit to the agent behind every generous act and declare it the real motive.

The French aristocrat François de La Rochefoucauld turned the move into an art form. His *Maxims* (1665) open with an epigraph:

> Our virtues are most frequently but vices disguised.
>
> — La Rochefoucauld, *Maxims*, epigraph (tr. Willis Bund and Friswell)

> Interest speaks all sorts of tongues and plays all sorts of characters; even that of disinterestedness.
>
> — La Rochefoucauld, *Maxims* 39 (tr. Willis Bund and Friswell)

Anyone who has watched a charity gala knows that the maxims are often true. The question is whether they are *always* true, as the theory requires. Defenders usually offer three arguments:

1. **The argument from desire.** Whenever you act voluntarily, you do what you most want to do. So you always act to satisfy your own desires. So you are always selfish.
2. **The argument from satisfaction.** People who help others feel good about it. The good feeling is what they were really after.
3. **The argument from hidden motives.** Look closely at any apparently altruistic act and you will find a self-interested motive: reputation, reciprocity, avoiding guilt, a ticket to heaven.

Each, on examination, either fails or proves something too trivial to matter.

## Butler's reply

The decisive critique came from Joseph Butler (1692–1752), an Anglican bishop who preached to lawyers at the Rolls Chapel in London and published fifteen of his sermons in 1726. Butler's sermons are among the best moral psychology in English, and they are aimed squarely at Hobbes.

Butler's central distinction is between **self-love**, a general desire for one's own happiness, and the **particular affections**, desires for specific things in the world: food, revenge, a friend's welfare, the esteem of others.

> The principle we call self-love never seeks anything external for the sake of the thing, but only as a means of happiness or good: particular affections rest in the external things themselves.
>
> — Joseph Butler, Sermon XI, "Upon the Love of Our Neighbour" (1726)

Hunger aims at food, not at the pleasure of eating. Curiosity aims at knowing, not at the satisfaction of having found out. The pleasure comes *when* a particular desire is satisfied, and it comes *because* we already wanted the thing for its own sake. Butler states the point with a memorable image:

> That all particular appetites and passions are towards external things themselves, distinct from the pleasure arising from them, is manifested from hence; that there could not be this pleasure, were it not for that prior suitableness between the object and the passion: there could be no enjoyment or delight from one thing more than another, from eating food more than from swallowing a stone, if there were not an affection or appetite to one thing more than another.
>
> — Joseph Butler, Sermon XI, "Upon the Love of Our Neighbour" (1726)

This dismantles the **argument from satisfaction**. Yes, the person who helps a friend feels good afterward. But the good feeling depends on the prior desire that the friend be helped. Someone who did not care about the friend would feel nothing on helping him, just as someone with no appetite for food would get no more pleasure from a meal than from a stone. The pleasure is the *result* of a non-selfish desire being satisfied; it cannot be the *explanation* of that desire.

It also dismantles the **argument from desire**. Yes, every voluntary act satisfies some desire of yours; that is what makes it voluntary. But "selfish" does not mean "acting on your own desire." It means acting on a desire *whose object is your own good*. The desire that your friend recover is your desire, but it is a desire *about* your friend. Butler sees that the egoist has simply redefined the word:

> But then this is not the language of mankind; or if it were, we should want words to express the difference between the principle of an action, proceeding from cool consideration that it will be to my own advantage; and an action, suppose of revenge or of friendship, by which a man runs upon certain ruin, to do evil or good to another.
>
> — Joseph Butler, Sermon XI, "Upon the Love of Our Neighbour" (1726)

Notice the example: *revenge*. Butler's point is not that people are nice. Malice is as disinterested as benevolence. A man who ruins himself to destroy his enemy is not acting from self-love, any more than a man who ruins himself to save his friend. Both are moved by a particular affection whose object is another person. If psychological egoism cannot even explain spite, something has gone wrong with it.

Butler also turned his attention directly to Hobbes's definition of pity. If pity were fear for oneself, he observed in a note to Sermon V, then the compassionate person and the cowardly person would be the same character:

> Hobbes defines *pity*, *imagination*, *or fiction of future calamity to ourselves*, *proceeding from the sense* (he means sight or knowledge) *of another man's calamity*. Thus fear and compassion would be the same idea, and a fearful and a compassionate man the same character, which every one immediately sees are totally different.
>
> — Joseph Butler, Sermon V, "Upon Compassion," note (1726)

And Butler adds something surprising. Not only is self-love not the whole of human nature; too much of it is self-defeating. Happiness consists in the satisfaction of particular affections, so someone who cared only about their own happiness, and about nothing in the world for its own sake, would have nothing to be happy *about*:

> Immoderate self-love does very ill consult its own interest: and, how much soever a paradox it may appear, it is certainly true that even from self-love we should endeavour to get over all inordinate regard to and consideration of ourselves.
>
> — Joseph Butler, Sermon XI, "Upon the Love of Our Neighbour" (1726)

This is now called the **paradox of hedonism**: those who aim directly at their own pleasure tend to get less of it than those who lose themselves in projects, people and causes. Mill would later report the same discovery from his own mental crisis (see [Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)).

## Hume, Feinberg and the experiments

David Hume, who disagreed with Butler about religion and much else, agreed with him here. An appendix to his *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, "Of Self-Love," attacks the theory that all benevolence is disguised self-love:

> The most obvious objection to the selfish hypothesis is, that, as it is contrary to common feeling and our most unprejudiced notions, there is required the highest stretch of philosophy to establish so extraordinary a paradox.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, Appendix II

Egoists claim to see beneath appearances, as physics explains the visible world with invisible atoms. Hume replies that in the study of the passions the simplest explanation is usually right, because hidden calculations cannot produce strong feelings:

> but a man that grieves for a valuable friend, who needed his patronage and protection; how can we suppose, that his passionate tenderness arises from some metaphysical regards to a self-interest, which has no foundation or reality? We may as well imagine that minute wheels and springs, like those of a watch, give motion to a loaded waggon, as account for the origin of passion from such abstruse reflections.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, Appendix II

He adds that animals show kindness "nor is there, in this case, the least suspicion of disguise or artifice," and makes a shrewd practical point: even if all affection were "self-love, twisted and moulded," the difference between the person whose self-love takes the form of concern for others and the person who cares for nothing beyond himself would remain exactly as real as before.

Joel Feinberg's essay "Psychological Egoism" (1958) added a methodological point. Ask the egoist what behavior would count as evidence *against* the theory. If every sacrifice, including dying for a stranger, is reinterpreted as secretly self-interested (the soldier wanted to avoid shame, the saint wanted heaven, the anonymous donor wanted to feel virtuous), then the theory is being protected from the facts, not tested against them. A theory compatible with every possible observation says nothing about the world. "Everyone acts selfishly" has become "everyone acts on their own motives," which is true, trivial and no reason for cynicism. That answers the **argument from hidden motives**: self-interest sometimes hides behind generosity, but the only way to get from "sometimes" to "always" is to make the theory unfalsifiable.

Finally, the question has been put to experiment. Over several decades the psychologist C. Daniel Batson tested the **empathy-altruism hypothesis**, that empathic concern produces a genuinely other-directed motive. Each egoistic rival makes a prediction: if people help only to relieve their own distress, they should help less when escape is easy; if only to avoid disapproval, they should help less when no one will know. In study after study, people feeling high empathy kept helping even when escape was easy and no one would know. Batson concluded, cautiously, that genuine altruism exists, though not that it is common or strong. (Chapter 1 described his parallel work on moral hypocrisy: self-interest is alive and well too.)

The verdict of most philosophers and psychologists is that **psychological egoism, in any form strong enough to be interesting, is false.** Human motivation is mixed. Self-interest is powerful and often disguised, but it is one motive among others, alongside benevolence, malice, curiosity, loyalty, duty and love.

## Ethical egoism

If people *can* act for the sake of others, the question becomes whether they *should*. Ethical egoism says that each person ought to promote their own interest, and should help others only when, and because, doing so serves it. Stated fairly, it does not recommend rudeness or short-sighted greed: a sensible egoist keeps promises, cultivates friends and obeys the law, because these generally pay. Its best-known modern defender, the novelist Ayn Rand (*The Virtue of Selfishness*, 1964), argued that a person's own life is their highest value, that altruism, understood as a demand for self-sacrifice, is degrading, and that a society of rational egoists trading value for value would be just and prosperous.

Three arguments are common. The **argument from respect** (Rand's main one) says that altruism treats individuals as resources to be sacrificed. The **argument from efficiency** says that each person knows their own needs best, so everyone is better off if everyone looks after themselves; Adam Smith's remark that we get our dinner from the butcher's self-interest, not his benevolence, is often cited, though Smith was no egoist (see [Chapter 8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md)). The **argument from common sense** says that ordinary duties such as honesty and promise-keeping can be derived from enlightened self-interest. Notice that the second and third are not really egoist: they recommend egoism *because* it benefits everyone or reproduces common morality. If self-interest made everyone worse off, they would recommend abandoning it. They are arguments for something like utilitarianism, using egoism as a strategy.

### Hobbes and the Foole

Hobbes's political philosophy is a study of what rational egoists would do. Without a government to enforce agreements, each has reason to fear the others and strike first, and the result is a war of every man against every man:

> In such condition, there is no place for Industry; because the fruit thereof is uncertain; and consequently no Culture of the Earth; no Navigation, nor use of the commodities that may be imported by Sea; no commodious Building; no Instruments of moving, and removing such things as require much force; no Knowledge of the face of the Earth; no account of Time; no Arts; no Letters; no Society; and which is worst of all, continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short.
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* ch. 13 (1651)

(If you doubt it, Hobbes adds, ask yourself why you lock your doors at night, even in a country with laws and police.) This is the logic of what game theorists now call the **prisoner's dilemma**: each party does better by defecting whatever the other does, yet both do worse if both defect than if both cooperate. Hobbes's solution, a contract establishing a sovereign who punishes defectors, is the subject of [Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md).

Once the contract exists, should an egoist keep agreements he can get away with breaking? Hobbes gives the question to a character he calls the Foole, after the fool of Psalm 14 who says in his heart that there is no God:

> The Foole hath sayd in his heart, there is no such thing as Justice; and sometimes also with his tongue; seriously alleaging, that every mans conservation, and contentment, being committed to his own care, there could be no reason, why every man might not do what he thought conduced thereunto; and therefore also to make, or not make; keep, or not keep Covenants, was not against Reason, when it conduced to ones benefit.
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* ch. 15 (1651)

The Foole is Gyges and Hume's sensible knave in seventeenth-century dress. Hobbes's reply is egoist through and through:

> He therefore that breaketh his Covenant, and consequently declareth that he thinks he may with reason do so, cannot be received into any Society, that unite themselves for Peace and defence, but by the errour of them that receive him
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* ch. 15 (1651)

Even if the Foole gets away with it, he was relying on luck, and a policy that relies on other people's errors is not rational. This is a strong argument against short-sighted cheating. Whether it answers the cheater who is very good at not getting caught, the person with the ring, is less clear.

### Arguments against ethical egoism

**It cannot resolve conflicts of interest.** Kurt Baier (*The Moral Point of View*, 1958) noted that one job of morality is to settle disputes. If A and B compete for one job, egoism tells A that he ought to get it by any prudent means, including sabotaging B, and tells B the same about A. It cannot serve as a *public* standard for resolving disagreements. Some egoists reply that morality is about each person's own life, not about resolving conflicts. Whether that is a reply or a change of subject is for you to decide.

**Arbitrariness.** Rachels thought this the deepest objection. A theory that counts some people's interests more than others' needs a relevant difference to justify it; racism and sexism are wrong partly because they draw a moral line where there is none. Egoism draws the line between oneself and everyone else. What is the relevant difference? That I am *me*, as each egoist says? Every other person can say exactly the same.

**Incoherence.** G. E. Moore argued in *Principia Ethica* (1903) that egoism is self-contradictory. If it is *good* that a person has something, everyone else has as much reason to promote it as that person does; so egoism, in saying each person's good is the sole thing that person should aim at, says that each person's good is *the* good:

> What Egoism holds, therefore, is that each man's happiness is the sole good--that a number of different things are each of them the only good thing there is--an absolute contradiction! No more complete and thorough refutation of any theory could be desired.
>
> — G. E. Moore, *Principia Ethica* §59 (1903)

Most later philosophers think Moore moved too fast. The egoist need not claim that his happiness is good "absolutely," only that *he* has reason to pursue it. Reasons can be **agent-relative**: that something benefits a person can be a reason for that person without being a reason for anyone else. Nearly every ethical theory allows some agent-relative reasons (special obligations to one's own children, for example), and once they are allowed Moore's contradiction disappears.

### Sidgwick's stalemate

Henry Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics* (1874) treated egoism more fairly than any work before or since. Sidgwick thought the axioms of practical reason supported utilitarianism, but he could not show that the egoist was irrational:

> It would be contrary to Common Sense to deny that the distinction between any one individual and any other is real and fundamental, and that consequently "I" am concerned with the quality of my existence as an individual in a sense, fundamentally important, in which I am not concerned with the quality of the existence of other individuals: and this being so, I do not see how it can be proved that this distinction is not to be taken as fundamental in determining the ultimate end of rational action for an individual.
>
> — Henry Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, Concluding Chapter §1 (7th ed., 1907)

He called this the **dualism of practical reason**: reason tells us to promote both the general good and our own, and when they conflict it gives no verdict. Sidgwick found the result deeply troubling, since it seemed to leave ethics incoherent at its core unless something, perhaps God, guaranteed that virtue and happiness coincide. Derek Parfit spent much of his career trying to dissolve the dualism (see [Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)).

So the state of play is this. *Psychological* egoism is almost certainly false. *Ethical* egoism is harder to refute; its most serious problems are arbitrariness and its inability to serve the public function of morality. But the egoist's question, "why should I care about others' interests as such?", is the question of Chapter 1, and it does not go away because the egoist's own answer is unattractive.

## Living it

**Catch the slide from "differs" to "no fact."** When you hear (or think) "who's to say?", ask which of the four relativist theses is in play. Usually the speaker means something true and modest, such as "this is a matter of convention" or "we should be cautious about judging." Say that instead, and see whether the stronger claim is still needed.

**Test yourself for ethnocentrism.** Take a custom of your own that you consider simply correct (tipping, eating meat, marrying for love, sending the elderly to care homes) and describe it as Herodotus would, as the strange habit of a distant people. Then ask what value it serves, and whether a people with the opposite custom might share that value.

**Notice when you are doing La Rochefoucauld's trick.** Cynicism about other people's motives is often self-flattering: if everyone is selfish, your own selfishness is just honesty. When you explain someone's kindness by a hidden payoff, ask what evidence would show you were wrong. If none would, you are protecting a theory, not observing.

**Use Butler's paradox.** If you are unhappy, Butler's advice is not to think harder about your own happiness but to find something outside yourself worth caring about for its own sake.

## Summary

- Herodotus's story of Darius shows that customs differ and that each people prefers its own. His own conclusion, that mocking others' customs is madness, is not a relative claim.
- The Greeks debated *nomos* and *physis*. Protagoras made justice whatever a city takes it to be; Callicles called conventional justice a trick of the weak against the natural right of the strong; Sextus argued from diversity to suspension of judgment.
- **Cultural relativism** bundles four claims: diversity, dependency, metaethical relativism and tolerance. The **cultural differences argument** from the first to the third is invalid without further premises.
- Metaethical relativism implies that other cultures cannot be criticized, that morality is settled by poll, that reformers are wrong by definition and that there is no moral progress; it cannot say which "society" counts, and it undermines tolerance.
- Disagreement often reflects shared values applied to different facts, and some values seem necessary for any society. Sophisticated relativisms (Harman, Wong, Williams) and the critique of ethnocentrism remain valuable.
- **Psychological egoism** (Hobbes, La Rochefoucauld) fails. **Butler** showed that the pleasure of satisfaction presupposes a prior desire for the thing itself; Hume added the argument from simplicity, Feinberg the charge of unfalsifiability, Batson experimental evidence for altruism. Butler's **paradox of hedonism**: immoderate self-love is self-defeating.
- **Ethical egoism** faces the objections that it cannot settle conflicts and is arbitrary; Moore's charge of contradiction is answered by agent-relative reasons. Hobbes's reply to the Foole and Sidgwick's **dualism of practical reason** show how hard the egoist's challenge is to answer fully.

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Herodotus in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Try to say whether there is any fact of the matter, and why.*

**You:** Neither, I think. It's just custom. Each people does what it's used to.

**Herodotus:** Then is there nothing either people could do to their dead fathers that would be wrong?

**You:** Within their own customs, sure. A Greek who ate his father would be doing wrong by Greek standards.

**Herodotus:** And if a Greek mocked the Callatians for their custom, as Cambyses mocked the Egyptians, would he be doing wrong?

**You:** You said that only a madman would mock other people's customs. So yes, I agree, that's wrong.

**Herodotus:** Wrong by Greek standards, or wrong by Callatian standards? Suppose Greek custom permitted such mockery.

**You:** Then I'd still say he was wrong. It's disrespectful.

**Herodotus:** So you have one thing, at least, that is not merely custom: the respect owed to the customs of others. Where did you get it?

**You:** Fair point. Maybe the respect is the real principle, and the funeral rites are just conventions.

**Herodotus:** Suppose you could ask each people why they do as they do. What do you imagine they would say?

**You:** Probably something like: the Callatians keep the father within the family instead of giving him to the worms, and the Greeks free him from the corruption of the body. Either way, it's about what a son owes his father.

**Herodotus:** Then is the disagreement about morality at all?

**You:** Partly. They agree on the value and disagree about how to honor it, which depends on beliefs about bodies and souls. If one of those beliefs is false, then maybe one practice is mistaken after all, not in intention but in fact.

**Herodotus:** You began by saying neither was right. Now you say both share a value that is not a mere custom, and one of them might be mistaken.

**You:** It means the diversity is real, but it doesn't go all the way down.

**Herodotus:** Then here is a harder case to take home. The Tauri sacrificed shipwrecked strangers to their goddess. Is that also a different way of honoring a value you share?

### Dialogue 2 — Butler on the selfish helper

**Joseph Butler:** You told your friend that you only volunteered at the shelter because it makes you feel good. Did you mean it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Butler before opening the continuation. Is it true that everything you do is ultimately for yourself?*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Basically, yes. I like the feeling. If it made me feel bad I wouldn't do it. So in the end it's for me.

**Butler:** Why does it make you feel good?

**You:** Because I'm helping people who need it.

**Butler:** Would you feel the same pleasure if you spent the evening stacking empty boxes in a warehouse, with no one helped at all?

**You:** No, of course not.

**Butler:** Then the pleasure depends on the people being helped. You must have wanted them to be helped before the pleasure came. Otherwise helping them would give you no more pleasure than swallowing a stone.

**You:** OK, but I still wanted the feeling.

**Butler:** Perhaps you did, as well. But you could not have it without first wanting something else, their good, for its own sake. That prior desire is not about you. It is about them. It is yours, of course, as every desire you have is yours. Would you call a man selfish because the desires he acts on are his own?

**You:** No. Everyone's desires are their own. That would make everyone selfish by definition.

**Butler:** Then the word would no longer distinguish anyone from anyone else. We would need a new word for the man who will not cross the street to help a stranger, and another for the man who gives his life for one.

**You:** But some people do volunteer for selfish reasons, to look good on a résumé, for example.

**Butler:** Certainly. I never said self-love has no part in our actions. I said it is not the whole of our nature. You might test yourself. Would you go on volunteering if no one ever knew you did it, and if you somehow were prevented from feeling pleased with yourself?

**You:** I'd like to think so. I'm not sure.

**Butler:** That uncertainty is honest, and it is worth more than the theory you began with, which was certain about everyone and true of no one in particular. One more question. You said you are happiest at the shelter. Were you thinking about your happiness while you were there?

**You:** Not really. I was thinking about the people there.

**Butler:** Then perhaps the surest way to be happy is to stop making yourself the object of your attention. Consider that, and tell me next week whether it was true.

</details>
