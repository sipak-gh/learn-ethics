# Chapter 15 — Nietzsche and the Critique of Morality

Every theory in the last six chapters takes morality for granted and asks how to systematize it. Kant asks what reason demands; Mill asks what produces the most happiness; Rawls asks what free and equal persons would agree to; Hursthouse asks what a virtuous person would do. None of them asks whether morality itself is good for us. In 1887 Friedrich Nietzsche asked exactly that. His *On the Genealogy of Morals* proposes that our most cherished values (compassion, humility, equality, selflessness, guilt) have a history, that the history is not edifying, and that once we know it we must ask a question no moral philosopher had asked: what is *the value of these values*? This chapter reads Nietzsche as a moral philosopher: what he attacks, how his method works, what he proposes instead, and how critics have answered. His announcement of the death of God and his diagnosis of nihilism are treated in [learn-nihilism, Chapter 6](../learn-nihilism/06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md); his affirmative doctrines (eternal recurrence, *amor fati*, the Übermensch) in [learn-nihilism, Chapter 7](../learn-nihilism/07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md); and a short overview of his life and thought in [learn-philosophy, Chapter 15](../learn-philosophy/15-nietzsche.md). Here the question is narrower and sharper: is Nietzsche right about morality? Previous: [Chapter 14 — Care and Feminist Ethics](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md). Next: [Chapter 16 — Existentialist Ethics](16-existentialist-ethics.md).

## An immoralist among moralists

Nietzsche (1844–1900) called himself an "immoralist." He meant it provocatively, and he did not mean that he recommended cruelty or lying. He meant that he stood outside morality and asked questions about it that could not be asked from inside, much as an anthropologist studies a religion she does not share.

What exactly is his target? Not every system of values: Nietzsche values honesty, courage, discipline, creativity, cheerfulness, and above all the flourishing of exceptional human beings. Brian Leiter's influential reconstruction calls the target **morality in the pejorative sense** (MPS). On this reading, Nietzsche attacks a family of values with two components:

1. **Descriptive presuppositions**: that agents have free will and are responsible for what they do; that the self is transparent to itself, so that motives can be known; and that people are similar enough that one morality can bind them all.
2. **Normative content**: that pity, selflessness, humility, equality and the relief of suffering are the highest values, and that pride, self-assertion, hardness and inequality are bad.

Nietzsche's objection is not that these values are false (he doubts that values can be true or false in the way that facts are). It is that they are *bad for* the highest human types. A culture organized around MPS, he thinks, prevents the emergence of a Goethe or a Beethoven by teaching potential greatness to despise itself. That is a striking objection, and it requires a different method from any we have seen: not argument from premises, but **genealogy**.

## Genealogy as method

The Preface of the *Genealogy* explains what is new:

> we need a _critique_ of moral values, _the value of these values_ is for the first time to be called into question—and for this purpose a knowledge is necessary of the conditions and circumstances out of which these values grew, and under which they experienced their evolution and their distortion
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, Preface §6 (tr. Samuel)

He adds that morality may turn out to be "a symptom, as a mask, as Tartuffism, as disease," but "also morality as a cause, as a remedy, as a stimulant." The list is not only hostile. The question is what morality does *now*, and for whom.

Nietzsche's model genealogists were the "English psychologists," especially his former friend Paul Rée, whose *The Origin of the Moral Sensations* (1877) explained morality as useful altruism whose usefulness had been forgotten. Nietzsche thought this got the history backward, and it got the method wrong too. The second essay contains the key methodological principle:

> The origin of the existence of a thing and its final utility, its practical application and incorporation in a system of ends, are _toto cœlo_ opposed to each other—everything, anything, which exists and which prevails anywhere, will always be put to new purposes by a force superior to itself, will be commandeered afresh, will be turned and transformed to new uses
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, II §12 (tr. Samuel)

You cannot read the origin of an institution off its present function. Punishment is now justified as deterrence, or reform, or retribution; it has meant all of these and more at different times, and its present meaning is a sediment of all of them. That is why, Nietzsche says, punishment cannot be defined: "it is only that which has no history, which can be defined" (II §13).

### The genetic fallacy objection

A familiar objection says that genealogy commits the **genetic fallacy**: confusing the origin of a belief with its justification. Even if arithmetic was invented by tax collectors, it is still true that 7 + 5 = 12. Even if compassion arose from resentment, compassion may still be good.

There are three replies on Nietzsche's behalf:

- **Contingency.** Genealogy does not refute a value; it shows that it is not inevitable. A value that seemed to be simply "what morality is" turns out to be one option among others, adopted for reasons. That reopens a question that seemed closed.
- **Unmasking.** Some values claim a particular origin as part of their authority. Christian morality claims to come from God; Kantian morality claims to come from pure reason. If it turns out that the values express the interests and frustrations of particular groups, that undercuts the claim on which their authority rests.
- **Diagnosis.** Nietzsche treats values as symptoms of the life that holds them. If a value expresses exhaustion or hatred of life, that is relevant to whether a flourishing person should hold it, even if it says nothing about its "truth."

Bernard Williams (*Truth and Truthfulness*, 2002) generalized the point: genealogies can be **subversive**, showing that a value's real explanation is one its holders could not accept; or **vindicatory**, showing that a value would arise for good reasons in any society like ours. Williams's own genealogy of truthfulness was mostly vindicatory. Nietzsche's genealogy of morality is meant to be subversive. ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md) takes up the debunking arguments that descend from this idea.)

## The first essay: "good and bad," "good and evil"

### Two origins of "good"

The first essay begins with a philological claim. The English psychologists said that "good" originally meant "useful to others" and that unselfish actions were called good by those who benefited. Nietzsche replies that this is the wrong place to look:

> the judgment "good" did not originate among those to whom goodness was shown. Much rather has it been the good themselves, that is, the aristocratic, the powerful, the high-stationed, the high-minded, who have felt that they themselves were good, and that their actions were good, that is to say of the first order, in contradistinction to all the low, the low-minded, the vulgar, and the plebeian.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, I §2 (tr. Samuel)

This is the **noble mode of valuation**: "good" begins as self-affirmation ("we aristocrats, we good ones, we beautiful ones, we happy ones"), and "bad" is an afterthought, a word for those who lack what the nobles have, said with more pity and indifference than hatred. *Beyond Good and Evil* §260 says the same: in master morality, value-terms are applied first to *people* and only later to actions, and the noble person "regards HIMSELF as a determiner of values." Notice the implication for the history of ethics: the earliest ethics, on Nietzsche's view, is something like Aristotle's *megalopsychos* ([Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md)), not like Kant's good will.

### The slave revolt

The second mode of valuation is born from frustration. Nietzsche attributes its invention to a priestly caste, who could not defeat the warrior nobles by force and so defeated them by revaluing their values. Here Nietzsche notoriously assigns the decisive role to "the Jews," as the priestly people who began the "revolt of the slaves in morals," with Christianity as its heir. Readers should know two facts. Nietzsche despised the antisemites of his day, broke with Wagner partly over his antisemitism, and mocked his brother-in-law, the antisemitic agitator Bernhard Förster. And his sister Elisabeth, who controlled his papers after his collapse, presented him to the Nazis as their prophet, a misappropriation that scholarship has spent decades undoing. Neither fact makes his use of ethnic stereotypes in the *Genealogy* acceptable; both are needed to read it honestly.

The engine of the slave revolt is **ressentiment** (Nietzsche uses the French word): the festering hatred of the powerless, who cannot act on their resentment and so take "imaginary revenge." Its key move is a reversal of direction:

> While every aristocratic morality springs from a triumphant affirmation of its own demands, the slave morality says "no" from the very outset to what is "outside itself," "different from itself," and "not itself": and this "no" is its creative deed.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, I §10 (tr. Samuel)

Noble morality begins with "good" (us) and derives "bad" (them). Slave morality begins with "evil" (them, the masters) and derives "good" (us, who are not like them). The words change meaning in the process. The nobles' "bad" meant *contemptible, unlucky, low*; the slaves' "evil" means *wicked, culpable, deserving punishment*. And the noble's virtues (strength, pride, self-assertion) become the slave's vices.

### The bird of prey and the free subject

For the masters to be *blamed* for being strong, a further invention is needed: the idea of a free subject behind each action, who could have chosen otherwise. Nietzsche's image is of lightning:

> just exactly as the people separate the lightning from its flash, and interpret the latter as a thing done, as the working of a subject which is called lightning, so also does the popular morality separate strength from the expression of strength, as though behind the strong man there existed some indifferent neutral _substratum_, which enjoyed a _caprice and option_ as to whether or not it should express strength. But there is no such _substratum_, there is no "being" behind doing, working, becoming; "the doer" is a mere appanage to the action.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, I §13 (tr. Samuel)

The lamb says: the bird of prey *could* refrain from taking lambs; it *chooses* to be evil; therefore it deserves blame. And the lamb, which cannot take anything, says: *I* choose not to; my weakness is a virtue freely chosen. In *Twilight of the Idols* Nietzsche put the point more bluntly: "The doctrine of the will was invented principally for the purpose of punishment,—that is to say, with the intention of tracing guilt" ("The Four Great Errors" §7). Whether he is right about the *origin* of the idea of free will, the claim that our concept of responsibility is shaped by our desire to blame is now a live hypothesis in moral psychology ([Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md)).

### The workshop where ideals are made

The first essay's most famous passage is a dramatic scene. Nietzsche invites "Mr. Inquisitive and Foolhardy" to peer into the dim workshop where ideals are manufactured, and to report what he hears:

> "And the impotence which requites not, is turned to 'goodness,' craven baseness to meekness, submission to those whom one hates, to obedience (namely, obedience to one of whom they say that he ordered this submission—they call him God). The inoffensive character of the weak, the very cowardice in which he is rich, his standing at the door, his forced necessity of waiting, gain here fine names, such as 'patience,' which is also called 'virtue'; not being able to avenge one's self, is called not wishing to avenge one's self, perhaps even forgiveness"
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, I §14 (tr. Samuel)

The observer finally cries: "Bad air! Bad air! These workshops _where ideals are manufactured_—verily they reek with the crassest lies."

This is the core charge, and it is a charge of **self-deception**. The weak do not *choose* meekness; they have no alternative. Then they describe their necessity as a free and meritorious choice, and their hatred of the strong as love of justice. Morality, on this view, is the most successful act of revenge in history: it persuaded the strong themselves to feel guilty for their strength.

Compare Mill's "willing slave" and Wollstonecraft's "spaniel-like affection" ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)). They too argued that the virtues praised in the subordinated (gentleness, docility, self-abnegation) were products of subordination dressed up as nature or merit. The diagnosis is similar. The conclusions are opposite. Wollstonecraft and Mill concluded that the subordination should end and everyone should be educated to the same rational virtue. Nietzsche concluded that the subordinates' morality should not be allowed to bind those who are not subordinate.

## The second essay: guilt, debt and bad conscience

### The animal that can promise

The second essay opens with a question: "The breeding of an animal that _can promise_—is not this just that very paradox of a task which nature has set itself in regard to man?" (II §1). To promise, a creature must remember its commitments, regard its future self as bound by its present will, and be calculable to others. That capacity was not natural; it was bred into humans by thousands of years of what Nietzsche calls the "morality of custom," and the means were brutal. "When man thinks it necessary to make for himself a memory, he never accomplishes it without blood, tortures, and sacrifice" (II §3). The penal codes of the past, with their quartering and boiling, were mnemonics.

The product is the **sovereign individual**, who has a right to promise because he can keep his word. Nietzsche admires him: the *Genealogy* contains appreciation as well as attack.

### Owe and ought

The origin of guilt, Nietzsche argues, lies in the relation of creditor and debtor. In German, *Schuld* means both "guilt" and "debt" (Samuel's translation renders the double meaning with "owe" and "ought"):

> Have these current genealogists of morals ever allowed themselves to have even the vaguest notion, for instance, that the cardinal moral idea of "ought" originates from the very material idea of "owe"? Or that punishment developed as a retaliation absolutely independently of any preliminary hypothesis of the freedom or determination of the will?
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, II §4 (tr. Samuel)

A debtor who cannot pay gives the creditor the right to inflict suffering as compensation; the creditor is repaid in the pleasure of exercising power over him. Punishment did not originally presuppose that the wrongdoer "could have done otherwise"; that idea, Nietzsche says, is "an exceedingly late, and even refined form of human judgment." He extends the point to morality's highest concepts: the world of "guilt," "conscience" and "duty" has its cradle in contract law, and "has never really lost a certain savour of blood and torture (not even in old Kant; the categorical imperative reeks of cruelty)" (II §6). ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md) gives Kant's side.)

### Bad conscience

Guilt in the full moral sense requires one more step. When humans were enclosed within society and peace, their aggressive instincts lost their outlet:

> All instincts which do not find a vent without, _turn inwards_—this is what I mean by the growing "internalisation" of man: consequently we have the first growth in man, of what subsequently was called his soul.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, II §16 (tr. Samuel)

The cruelty once directed outward is now directed at oneself. This is **bad conscience**: the self-torment of an animal that cannot express its nature. Then the priests give it a meaning. The debt is reinterpreted as a debt to God, a debt so great that it can never be repaid, so that guilt becomes infinite and permanent. Christianity is the "stroke of genius" in which God pays the debt himself, "the creditor playing scapegoat for his debtor, from _love_": an act of love that makes the debt even greater (II §21).

Nietzsche calls bad conscience an illness, "but an illness like pregnancy is an illness" (II §19). The internalized human being, the being with a soul, is far more interesting than the healthy animal it replaced. Here again the genealogy is not simply hostile: morality made us deep.

## The third essay: the meaning of suffering

The third essay asks why the **ascetic ideal** (poverty, humility, chastity, self-denial, the scholar's selfless devotion to truth) has dominated human culture. Its answer comes in the final section:

> his problem was not suffering itself, but the lack of an answer to that crying question, "_To what purpose_ do we suffer?" Man, the bravest animal and the one most inured to suffering, does _not_ repudiate suffering in itself: he _wills_ it, he even seeks it out, provided that he is shown a meaning for it, a _purpose_ of suffering. _Not_ suffering, but the senselessness of suffering was the curse which till then lay spread over humanity—_and the ascetic ideal gave it a meaning!_
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, III §28 (tr. Samuel)

The ascetic priest's meaning was: you suffer because you are guilty. That explanation "brought in its train new suffering, deeper, more penetrating, more venomous," but it gave the will something to will, and "any meaning is better than no meaning." For ethics, the lesson is that moral systems are not only codes of conduct; they are interpretations of suffering. A moral theory that tells people their suffering is deserved, or redemptive, or meaningless, shapes how they live with it. The connection to nihilism, what happens when the ascetic meaning collapses, is developed in [learn-nihilism, Chapter 6](../learn-nihilism/06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md).

## The natural history of morals

*Beyond Good and Evil* (1886), the book the *Genealogy* was written to supplement, approaches morality as a naturalist approaches a species.

**A typology, not a foundation.** Philosophers, Nietzsche complains, all wanted to give morality a basis, while taking morality itself as "given." What is needed first is description: "the collection of material, the comprehensive survey and classification of an immense domain of delicate sentiments of worth" as "preparation for a THEORY OF TYPES of morality" (§186). This is the agenda that moral psychology and anthropology now pursue ([Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md), [Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md)).

**Morality as symptom.** Each morality reveals its author. One helps its author forget, another makes him feared, another lets him take revenge. Of Kant, Nietzsche says that his morals tell us that "what is estimable in me, is that I know how to obey—and with you it SHALL not be otherwise than with me!" His conclusion: "systems of morals are only a SIGN-LANGUAGE OF THE EMOTIONS" (§187).

**Constraint as a good.** Yet Nietzsche immediately praises morality for its discipline:

> every system of morals is a sort of tyranny against "nature" and also against "reason", that is, however, no objection
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil*, §188 (tr. Zimmern)

What is "essential and invaluable" in any morality is that "it is a long constraint," like the meter and rhyme that taught poets to write. Everything great in art, thought and character, he says, came from long obedience in one direction. Nietzsche is not against discipline. He is against discipline for the wrong ends.

**Fear and the herd.** Moralities arise from the needs of communities. When a community is threatened, aggressive and dominating instincts are honored because they are needed. When it is safe, the same instincts are felt as dangerous to equality and branded immoral: "here again fear is the mother of morals" (§201). The result:

> MORALITY IN EUROPE AT PRESENT IS HERDING-ANIMAL MORALITY, and therefore, as we understand the matter, only one kind of human morality, beside which, before which, and after which many other moralities, and above all HIGHER moralities, are or should be possible.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil*, §202 (tr. Zimmern)

The offense of herd morality is not that it exists (herds need it) but that it claims to be the only morality: it says, "I am morality itself and nothing else is morality!"

**Against universality.** Nietzsche's central objection to MPS appears, characteristically, in someone else's mouth: a "moralistic pedant" who says that every unconditional, universal morality sins against good taste, and that "it is IMMORAL to say that 'what is right for one is proper for another.'" (§221). What counts as a virtue depends on who you are: in a person destined to command, "self-denial and modest retirement, instead of being virtues, would be the waste of virtues." This directly rejects the universalizability that Kant ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)) and Hare ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)) take as the mark of the moral.

**Against the utilitarians.** Nietzsche's scorn for the English is a running joke: "Man does not aspire to happiness; only the Englishman does that" (*Twilight*, "Maxims and Missiles" §12). In *Beyond Good and Evil* he watches "the indefatigable, inevitable English utilitarians" stalking along "in the footsteps of Bentham" (§228). His serious objection is that pleasure and pain are "accompanying circumstances and secondary considerations," not the measure of a life (§225). A life worth living is measured by what it creates, and creation requires suffering.

## Against pity

Nietzsche's attack on pity (*Mitleid*, also translated "compassion" or "sympathy") is his most direct challenge to modern ethics. His target was his former teacher Schopenhauer, who had made compassion the foundation of morality ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)):

> Boundless compassion for all living beings is the surest and most certain guarantee of pure moral conduct, and needs no casuistry.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The Basis of Morality*, Part III (tr. Bullock)

The *Genealogy* began, Nietzsche says in the Preface, as a quarrel with exactly this: "the value of the 'un-egoistic' instincts, the instincts of pity, self-denial, and self-sacrifice which Schopenhauer had so persistently painted in golden colours." His arguments against pity can be separated:

1. **It multiplies suffering.** Pity adds the pitier's suffering to the sufferer's, without necessarily relieving either. (This is the weakest argument: pity often motivates relief.)
2. **It humiliates.** To be pitied is to be looked down on. The noble person, Nietzsche says, helps the unfortunate, "but not—or scarcely—out of pity, but rather from an impulse generated by the super-abundance of power" (*BGE* §260). Compare Addams's charity visitor ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)): help from above can wound.
3. **It misunderstands suffering.** Pity wants to abolish suffering. But suffering is a condition of growth:

> You want, if possible—and there is not a more foolish "if possible"—TO DO AWAY WITH SUFFERING; and we?—it really seems that WE would rather have it increased and made worse than it has ever been! … The discipline of suffering, of GREAT suffering—know ye not that it is only THIS discipline that has produced all the elevations of humanity hitherto?
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil*, §225 (tr. Zimmern)

4. **It is a symptom.** A culture that makes pity its highest value is a tired culture, one that no longer wants anything except the absence of pain. The "last man" of *Zarathustra*, who has "discovered happiness" and blinks, is its endpoint ([learn-nihilism, Chapter 6](../learn-nihilism/06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)).

Nietzsche is not saying that people in agony should be left there; he praises friendship and gift-giving. His claim is that a morality organized around minimizing suffering cannot see the value of the struggles that make people great. Whether that is an insight or a rationalization for indifference is one of the central questions about him.

## Morality as the enemy of nature

*Twilight of the Idols* gives the critique its most compressed form. Traditional morality treats the passions as enemies to be destroyed: "if thy eye offend thee, pluck it out." Nietzsche replies:

> To annihilate the passions and desires, simply on account of their stupidity, and to obviate the unpleasant consequences of their stupidity, seems to us to-day merely an aggravated form of stupidity. We no longer admire those dentists who extract teeth simply in order that they may not ache again.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Twilight of the Idols*, "Morality as the Enemy of Nature" §1 (tr. Ludovici)

The alternative is the "spiritualisation of passion": sensuality refined into love, hostility refined into the valuing of enemies. (This is closer to Aristotle's training of the emotions than to Stoic extirpation; compare [learn-stoicism, Chapter 7](../learn-stoicism/07-the-passions.md).) Nietzsche then states a principle:

> All naturalism in morality—that is to say, every sound morality is ruled by a life instinct,—any one of the laws of life is fulfilled by the definite canon "thou shalt," "thou shalt not," and any sort of obstacle or hostile element in the road of life is thus cleared away. Conversely, the morality which is antagonistic to nature—that is to say, almost every morality that has been taught, honoured and preached hitherto, is directed precisely against the life-instincts
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Twilight of the Idols*, "Morality as the Enemy of Nature" §4 (tr. Ludovici)

He mocks the moralist who says "Man should be thus and thus!" in the face of "a marvellous wealth of types" (§6). Moralities that teach people to be ashamed of having bodies, desires and ambitions express a hatred of life, and that hatred is what Nietzsche means by "decadence."

## What does Nietzsche want?

If not morality, then what? Nietzsche gives no system, and readers disagree. Three things are reasonably clear.

**Perfectionism.** The standard of value is the flourishing of the highest human types: creative, self-disciplined, joyful individuals who give their lives a unified style. John Rawls cited Nietzsche as the extreme case of **perfectionism**, the view that society should be arranged to maximize human excellence rather than to distribute goods fairly, and rejected it ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)). Thomas Hurka and others have defended more moderate perfectionisms.

**Self-creation.** In place of judging others and oneself by universal rules, Nietzsche urges each person to become who they are:

> We, however, _would seek to become what we are_,—the new, the unique, the incomparable, making laws for ourselves and creating ourselves!
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §335 (tr. Common)

Notice "making laws for ourselves." Nietzsche takes over Kant's idea of autonomy and reverses it: the law is not universal but individual.

**Nobility.** *Beyond Good and Evil* ends with a portrait of the noble soul, which is marked not by its actions but by "some fundamental certainty which a noble soul has about itself": "THE NOBLE SOUL HAS REVERENCE FOR ITSELF" (§287). Nobility is a kind of self-relation: pride without vanity, the capacity to be generous without needing to be thanked, and the absence of ressentiment.

The doctrines that complete this picture, the eternal recurrence as a test of whether one can affirm one's life, *amor fati*, and the Übermensch, belong to [learn-nihilism, Chapter 7](../learn-nihilism/07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md). The existentialists who took over the theme of self-creation without Nietzsche's aristocratic politics are the subject of [Chapter 16](16-existentialist-ethics.md).

## Is Nietzsche right?

### Is the history true?

As history, the *Genealogy* is speculative: little evidence for the slave revolt, and partly wrong etymologies. Defenders (Leiter, Williams, Christopher Janaway) read it as a psychologically plausible story whose force lies in what it reveals about our motives. But if the history is not literally true, what does the story prove?

### Is the psychology true?

Max Scheler (*Ressentiment*, 1912) accepted that ressentiment is real and powerful, and that much modern humanitarian morality (he named bourgeois egalitarianism) was shaped by it. But he denied that Christian love was its product. Love that flows from abundance, from security in God, is the opposite of ressentiment; it stoops to the weak not from envy but from a fullness that has nothing to lose. Ironically, this is close to Nietzsche's description of noble generosity. If love can be noble, the slave-morality story explains at most its corruptions.

### Does the critique undermine itself?

Nietzsche's critique is driven by intellectual honesty: the will to see through self-deception. But truthfulness is one of the values Christian morality cultivated, and Nietzsche knew it: he says that Christian truthfulness, turned on itself, destroys Christianity (*Genealogy* III §27). If morality is debunked, is truthfulness debunked with it? Nietzsche's answer seems to be that truthfulness survives as a value for *him*, a value of the strong, not as a universal obligation. Whether a value can survive its own genealogy is the question Williams addressed in *Truth and Truthfulness*.

### Foot's challenge

Philippa Foot ("Nietzsche's Immoralism," 1991) took Nietzsche more seriously than most analytic philosophers of her generation. She granted that he identified real phenomena: moral judgment can be a vehicle of resentment, self-righteousness and timidity. But she pressed a question about justice. Nietzsche's objections are directed at the motives of moral people and at their effect on exceptional individuals. Neither touches the claim that people must not be treated unjustly. If the "higher type" needs to oppress others, so much the worse for the higher type. And if he does not need to, Nietzsche's critique does not dislodge justice. Foot thought the honest response to Nietzsche was to ask, of each element in our morality, whether its defense rests on illusions he exposed; but she concluded that the core requirements of justice, and the virtue ethics she defended ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)), survive the test.

### Who benefits from Nietzsche's ethics?

Nietzsche's ethics is explicitly inegalitarian. He thought most people should live by herd morality, and he wrote that a high culture requires something like slavery. The Danish critic Georg Brandes called his position "aristocratic radicalism," and Nietzsche accepted the label. Readers who admire his psychology must decide whether his politics can be detached from it. MacIntyre posed the choice starkly: *After Virtue* says that once the Enlightenment project failed, the options are "Nietzsche or Aristotle" ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)). Care ethicists ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)) might answer that Nietzsche's "higher types" are a fantasy of independence that forgets who raised them.

### What survives

After Nietzsche it is harder to treat moral convictions as self-certifying. His questions can be put to any morality: whose interests does this serve, what is it a symptom of, does it make human life larger or smaller? Freud's superego, Foucault's genealogies of punishment, feminist critiques of feminine "virtue," and debunking arguments in metaethics all descend from his method.

## Living it

**The ressentiment audit.** Recall the last time you morally condemned someone with real heat. Ask, as honestly as you can: did you envy them anything? Were you in a position to do what they did? Would your judgment have been the same if you could? This is not a test of whether your judgment was correct (envy and correctness can coexist) but of what was driving it.

**Origin and use.** Pick one moral practice you follow (tipping, apologizing, keeping Sabbath, recycling, saying "please"). Find out what you can about its origin. Then ask: does its present function differ from its origin? Does knowing the origin change your attitude toward it? Why or why not?

**Pity versus help.** For a week, notice when you feel pity. Each time, ask whether the feeling helped you act or replaced action, and how the other person would feel if they knew you pitied them. Then notice when you help someone without pity, from what Nietzsche calls overflow. Which kind of help would you rather receive?

## Summary

- Nietzsche asks for "the value of these values." His target, in Leiter's term, is "morality in the pejorative sense": free will, self-transparency and sameness as presuppositions; pity, selflessness and equality as content; harm to the highest human types as the charge.
- **Genealogy**: a thing's origin and its present use are wholly different; only what has no history can be defined. Against the genetic-fallacy objection: contingency, unmasking, diagnosis. Williams: genealogies can subvert or vindicate.
- **First essay**: "good" began as noble self-affirmation; the slave revolt begins with ressentiment saying "no" to what is outside itself; no doer behind the deed; the workshop where weakness becomes merit.
- **Second essay**: the animal that can promise; "ought" from "owe"; punishment before free will; bad conscience as instincts turned inward, then as infinite debt to God.
- **Third essay**: the senselessness of suffering, not suffering, was the curse; the ascetic ideal gave it meaning.
- ***Beyond Good and Evil***: a typology of moralities; morals as a "SIGN-LANGUAGE OF THE EMOTIONS"; morality as "a long constraint"; "fear is the mother of morals"; herd morality; against universality and the utilitarians.
- **Pity**: against Schopenhauer; pity multiplies suffering, humiliates, misreads suffering, and signals weariness.
- **Positive ethics**: perfectionism, self-creation, nobility as self-reverence.
- **Assessment**: speculative history; Scheler on noble love; the self-reference of truthfulness; Foot's defense of justice; aristocratic radicalism; the lasting method of suspicion.

## Dialogues

### Dialogue 1 — On the lightning and its flash

**Nietzsche:** A bird of prey takes a lamb. The lambs say the bird is evil: it could have refrained. Could it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Nietzsche in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** A bird, no. It's just doing what birds do. But people aren't birds. A strong person who bullies someone weaker could have chosen not to.

**Nietzsche:** Could he? Behind his bullying, you imagine a second thing, a neutral self that looked at the options and chose. Where is it? Have you ever met it in yourself?

**You:** I experience choosing. When I decide whether to lie, I feel like I could go either way.

**Nietzsche:** You feel uncertain about what you will do. That is not the same as a self that stands outside your drives and selects among them. The people separate the lightning from its flash. Then they blame the lightning for flashing.

**You:** But if there's no one who chooses, there's no one to blame. Then nobody is responsible for anything.

**Nietzsche:** And why does that frighten you? Ask who needed the idea of a free subject. Not the strong: they do not need to blame. The weak needed it, so that they could hold the strong guilty and call their own weakness a choice.

**You:** That's a story about where the idea came from. Even if it's true, the idea might still be correct.

**Nietzsche:** Granted. But notice how much depends on it. Without it, your hatred of the bully loses its justification, and your meekness loses its merit. Do you still want the idea as much, now that you see what it does for you?

**You:** I still think the bully shouldn't bully. And I want to be able to say so.

**Nietzsche:** Say so, then. Say: I do not want such people near me; I will stop him. That is honest. What I object to is the additional claim that he is *evil*, that he deserves suffering for being what he is.

**You:** So you'd keep the resistance and drop the blame.

**Nietzsche:** I would drop the revenge that calls itself justice.

</details>

### Dialogue 2 — On whether origins matter

**Foot:** Suppose Nietzsche is right, and the morality of compassion was invented by resentful slaves. What follows about whether you should help a stranger in distress?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Foot in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what she will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Nothing, strictly. That's the genetic fallacy. A value's origin doesn't settle whether it's good.

**Nietzsche (stepping in):** Then why do you care where your arithmetic comes from? You do not. But you care very much where your morality comes from. You say it comes from God, or from pure reason, or from human nature. If it came from the cellar of resentment, your morality has been lying about its own credentials.

**You:** So the origin matters because the morality itself made claims about its origin.

**Foot:** Fair. But let us separate the claims. Grant that some motives for compassion are poisonous: self-righteousness, the pleasure of feeling superior to the unfortunate. Does it follow that the stranger's need gives you no reason?

**You:** No. The stranger is still in distress whatever my motives are.

**Nietzsche:** And your help will either express your strength or your weakness. That is what I care about.

**Foot:** I care about that too. Virtue is about motives as well as actions. But you have said nothing to show that justice is a disguise. If your higher man must trample others to flourish, he is not admirable; he is unjust.

**You:** Maybe Nietzsche is a better diagnostician than a doctor. He's good at finding the poison in my motives. He's less good at telling me what I owe to other people.

**Foot:** That is roughly my view. Read him to test your morality, not to replace it.

**Nietzsche:** Then you have read me as a tool. Be careful: tools have a way of becoming masters.

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On counterfeit virtues

**Wollstonecraft:** I agree with you, Mr. Nietzsche, that weakness is often dressed up as virtue. The meekness taught to women is servility with a pretty name. What should we conclude?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Wollstonecraft in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what she will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** That those virtues aren't real virtues. They're survival strategies for people without power.

**Nietzsche:** Precisely. Patience is a fine name for being forced to wait at the door.

**Wollstonecraft:** Then the remedy is obvious. Give women the education and independence that men have, and they will acquire real virtues: courage, judgment, self-command. Make all human beings rational creatures and free citizens.

**Nietzsche:** All human beings? You see the disease and then prescribe more of it. Equality is exactly what the slave revolt wanted.

**Wollstonecraft:** I want women to have power over themselves, not over others. Is that slavish?

**Nietzsche:** Not in you. But a whole society of equals would be a herd. Greatness requires distance: some must stand higher.

**You:** But your own argument cuts against you. You say counterfeit virtues come from powerlessness. Then the way to end them is to end the powerlessness, which is what she's proposing.

**Nietzsche:** Or to free the strong from the morality the weak invented, and let the weak keep it.

**Wollstonecraft:** And who decides who is strong? In my day men decided that women were weak, then educated them to be so, then pointed to the result as proof.

**You:** That's the problem with "higher types." You never know how many were never allowed to develop.

**Nietzsche:** A fair hit. Mill's hothouse, I suppose. But I do not withdraw the question: when everyone is equal, what will anyone strive for?

**Wollstonecraft:** For virtue, sir. It has no sex, and I doubt it has a rank.

</details>
