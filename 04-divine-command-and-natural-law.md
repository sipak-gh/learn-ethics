# Chapter 4 — Divine Command and Natural Law

For most of human history, and for most people alive today, ethics and religion have been bound together. The commandments came down the mountain, the Buddha's precepts belong to a path of liberation, and the Qur'an joins prayer and almsgiving in a single list of duties. It is natural to think that right and wrong come from God, and that without God they would collapse. Philosophy has a 2,400-year-old argument that this cannot be quite right, and a 2,400-year-old tradition of replies. This chapter covers both. It begins with Plato's *Euthyphro*, the most famous short argument in ethics. It then examines divine command theory, from medieval voluntarism to Robert Adams's modern version. Next comes the great alternative that religious thinkers themselves developed: natural law, from Sophocles and Cicero through Thomas Aquinas to John Finnis. It ends with the empirical question that troubled Dostoevsky: can people be good without God? Previous: [Chapter 3 — Relativism and Egoism](03-relativism-and-egoism.md). Next: [Chapter 5 — Ancient Virtue Ethics](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md).

## Ivan's question

In Dostoevsky's *The Brothers Karamazov*, the worldly liberal Miüsov tells a scandalized gathering about something the middle brother, Ivan, said at a party:

> That there was no law of nature that man should love mankind, and that, if there had been any love on earth hitherto, it was not owing to a natural law, but simply because men have believed in immortality.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book II, ch. 6 (tr. Constance Garnett)

Miüsov goes on:

> Moreover, nothing then would be immoral, everything would be lawful, even cannibalism.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book II, ch. 6 (tr. Constance Garnett)

"Everything is lawful" haunts the rest of the novel; Ivan's half-brother Smerdyakov takes it seriously and commits a murder. Readers compress it into "If God does not exist, everything is permitted," a sentence not in the book in that form. Before evaluating it, separate three questions that it runs together:

1. **The metaphysical question.** Does the *existence* of moral facts depend on God? If there were no God, would anything be right or wrong?
2. **The epistemic question.** Does *knowing* right and wrong depend on religion, through revelation, scripture or a church? Could an atheist know that cruelty is wrong?
3. **The motivational and social question.** Does *being* good depend on religious belief? Will people behave decently if they stop believing that God is watching and that there is a judgment to come?

The questions are independent. A theist can hold that morality depends on God yet is knowable by reason to everyone, believer or not; Aquinas held roughly that. An atheist can hold that morality is independent of God yet that religious belief, true or false, helps societies cooperate. Ivan's remark is about question 3, but people usually hear it as an answer to question 1. The *Euthyphro* and divine command theory concern question 1; natural law answers questions 1 and 2 together; the last section turns to question 3 and the evidence.

## The *Euthyphro*: the case

The dialogue is set in 399 BCE outside the office of the magistrate who handled religious offenses. Socrates has just been indicted for impiety, the charge that will kill him (see [Chapter 1](01-what-is-ethics.md)). He meets Euthyphro, a religious expert, who is there to file a murder charge. Against whom?

> EUTHYPHRO: You will think me mad when I tell you.
>
> SOCRATES: Why, has the fugitive wings?
>
> EUTHYPHRO: Nay, he is not very volatile at his time of life.
>
> SOCRATES: Who is he?
>
> EUTHYPHRO: My father.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 4a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

A laborer on the family farm had killed a slave in a drunken fight. Euthyphro's father tied him up and threw him into a ditch while sending to Athens for advice:

> For such was the effect of cold and hunger and chains upon him, that before the messenger returned from the diviner, he was dead.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 4d (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Euthyphro's family is furious, since prosecuting one's father was itself thought impious; this shows, he says, "how little they know what the gods think about piety and impiety." He may be right that his father did wrong. The question is whether he *knows why*: whether he can say what makes something pious. Socrates, facing his own trial for impiety, asks to be taught.

## What makes the pious pious?

Euthyphro first offers an example (prosecuting wrongdoers), and Socrates asks for the general form. The second answer is the first divine command theory in Western literature:

> Piety, then, is that which is dear to the gods, and impiety is that which is not dear to them.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 6e–7a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

But the gods quarrel, and about exactly these matters (see [Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md)), so one act could be both pious and impious. Euthyphro amends: the pious is what *all* the gods love. Monotheists can skip this step. What comes next applies to them as much as to Euthyphro:

> The point which I should first wish to understand is whether the pious or holy is beloved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because it is beloved of the gods.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 10a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Socrates explains with analogies: a thing is carried because someone carries it; being loved is a state that results from someone's loving. Euthyphro agrees that the gods love the pious *because* it is pious; they love it for a reason. Then the trap closes:

> SOCRATES: I mean to say that the holy has been acknowledged by us to be loved of God because it is holy, not to be holy because it is loved.
>
> EUTHYPHRO: Yes.
>
> SOCRATES: But that which is dear to the gods is dear to them because it is loved by them, not loved by them because it is dear to them.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 10d–e (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

So "pious" and "god-loved" cannot mean the same thing. Substitute one for the other and "the gods love the pious because it is pious" becomes "the gods love the god-loved because it is god-loved," which reverses the order of explanation Euthyphro just accepted. Hence:

> Thus you appear to me, Euthyphro, when I ask you what is the essence of holiness, to offer an attribute only, and not the essence--the attribute of being loved by all the gods.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 11a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Being loved by the gods happens to pious things, as being admired happens to beautiful paintings; it is not what *makes* them pious. Euthyphro's reaction is one of the most human moments in Plato:

> For somehow or other our arguments, on whatever ground we rest them, seem to turn round and walk away from us.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 11b (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Later attempts (piety as service to the gods, or as "an art which gods and men have of doing business with one another") circle back. When Socrates proposes starting over, Euthyphro remembers an appointment:

> EUTHYPHRO: Another time, Socrates; for I am in a hurry, and must go now.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 15e (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

## The dilemma, restated for monotheism

Strip away the polytheism and the *Euthyphro* poses a dilemma to anyone who thinks morality depends on God. Take a moral truth, say that torturing children for fun is wrong, and ask: *does God forbid it because it is wrong, or is it wrong because God forbids it?*

**Horn 1: wrong because God forbids it.** Then God's command *makes* the act wrong; apart from the command it would be neutral. This is **theological voluntarism** (from *voluntas*, will): moral facts depend on God's will.

**Horn 2: God forbids it because it is wrong.** Then the wrongness is already there for God to recognize, and the command reports it. God may be a perfect moral authority, as a perfect atlas is an authority on geography, but God no more makes morality than the atlas makes Everest tall.

Each horn has a serious cost.

### The cost of horn 1: arbitrariness

If wrongness simply *is* being forbidden by God, then had God commanded the torture of children for fun, it would have been obligatory. That seems monstrous, and it makes morality a matter of what God happened to want. Medieval voluntarists bit the bullet: William of Ockham is usually read as holding that God could command anything non-contradictory, even hatred of God himself, and that obeying would then be meritorious. Descartes held that even mathematical truths depend on God's free will. Scripture seemed to supply cases, most unsettlingly the binding of Isaac:

> And he said, Take now thy son, thine only son Isaac, whom thou lovest, and get thee into the land of Moriah; and offer him there for a burnt offering upon one of the mountains which I will tell thee of.
>
> — Genesis 22:2 (King James Version)

Abraham obeys and is stopped at the last moment. Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* (1843) asks whether there can be a "teleological suspension of the ethical," and finds Abraham's faith admirable and terrifying, since from outside it is indistinguishable from attempted murder.

Horn 1 also **empties the praise of God**. If "good" means "approved by God," then "God is good" means roughly "God approves of himself," which would be true of any deity, however cruel. Leibniz pressed this against the voluntarists: why praise God for willing what he wills, if he would be equally praiseworthy for willing the opposite? Kant added that even the highest example is judged by a standard we already have:

> Even the Holy One of the Gospels must first be compared with our ideal of moral perfection before we can recognise Him as such
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals*, Second Section (tr. T. K. Abbott)

And it **gives the wrong reason**. Someone who refrains from cruelty only because of a command, and would happily be cruel if the command were lifted, has missed something.

### The cost of horn 2: independence

On horn 2, often called **intellectualism**, God's will follows God's intellect: God sees what is good and wills it. Morality escapes arbitrariness but now looks like something God is subject to, which seems to compromise omnipotence; and an atheist can in principle have all of it. The Dutch jurist Hugo Grotius embraced this horn in 1625, in the founding work of international law:

> Now the Law of Nature is so unalterable, that it cannot be changed even by God himself. For although the power of God is infinite, yet there are some things, to which it does not extend. Because the things so expressed would have no true meaning, but imply a contradiction. Thus two and two must make four, nor is it possible to be otherwise; nor, again, can what is really evil not be evil.
>
> — Hugo Grotius, *The Rights of War and Peace* I.1.10 (tr. A. C. Campbell)

In his Prolegomena Grotius added that his account would have some validity even if we granted (*etiamsi daremus*), wickedly, that there is no God. Historians often date secular natural law from that sentence. The arithmetic analogy answers the omnipotence worry: omnipotence is power to do anything *possible*, and making cruelty good may be no more possible than making two and two five. The remaining cost is that God does not *ground* morality.

## Divine command theory today

For much of the twentieth century the *Euthyphro* was treated as a refutation of divine command theory. Since the 1970s, philosophers of religion have argued that the dilemma is false.

### Adams: wrongness as contrary to the commands of a loving God

Robert Merrihew Adams (1973, revised 1979) proposed a **modified divine command theory**: wrongness is contrariety to the commands of a *loving* God. If God commanded cruelty for its own sake, the presupposition of the believer's concept would fail, and the believer would not say "then cruelty would be right"; the concept would simply break down. In the 1979 version Adams borrowed from Kripke and Putnam: "water is H<sub>2</sub>O" is a discovery about what water *is*, not about what "water" means, and "wrongness is contrariety to the commands of a loving God" could likewise be a discovery about the nature of wrongness.

### The Good and the commands

Adams's mature view, in *Finite and Infinite Goods* (1999), splits ethics in two. **The good** (what is excellent and worth loving) is not made by God's will; it is identical with God's nature, and finite things are good insofar as they resemble God, a Platonism with God in place of the Form of the Good. **Obligation** is constituted by God's commands, much as obligations in general arise from the demands made within valuable relationships; God's commands bind because they come from the most excellent party in the most important relationship we have. William Alston's "Some Suggestions for Divine Command Theorists" (1990) has a similar structure: God does not meet a standard of goodness; God *is* the standard, much as the standard meter bar once *was* the meter rather than measuring one.

The view claims to escape both horns. God's commands are not arbitrary, since they flow from an essentially good nature; and the standard of goodness is not independent of God, since it *is* God.

### Does the third way work?

**The dilemma returns one level up.** Is God's nature good because it is loving and just, or are lovingness and justice good because God's nature has them? The first is horn 2 again; the second implies that had God been cruel, cruelty would be good. The defender replies that God's nature is *necessary*: "had God been cruel" is like "had two and two been five." Critics answer that it is then a brute fact that the necessary being is loving rather than cruel.

**Every theory stops somewhere.** The defender's strongest counter: the Platonist stops at the Form of the Good, and the secular realist (Erik Wielenberg's *Robust Ethics* defends "godless normative realism") stops at brute necessary facts such as "pain is bad." None can say *why* the ultimate standard is good without circularity. If all stop at a necessary first term, the *Euthyphro* hits all equally or none. Whether God is a *better* stopping point is a question the dilemma does not settle.

**Epistemic access.** How do people who never heard God's commands have obligations? Adams answers that God speaks through conscience as well as scripture. But then moral knowledge is the same for believers and nonbelievers.

The fair verdict: the *Euthyphro* refutes the crude theory that "right" *means* "commanded by God," but not the sophisticated ones. It shows that any theistic ethics must say something about God's *goodness*, and once it does, much of the work is done by goodness rather than command. That is where natural law starts.

## Natural law: the older idea

Long before Christianity, Greek and Roman thinkers spoke of a law higher than the laws of cities. The most famous scene is in Sophocles' *Antigone* (c. 441 BCE). King Creon has forbidden anyone to bury the rebel Polynices, and his sister Antigone has buried him anyway. Asked whether she knew of the decree, she answers:

> Nor did I deem that thou, a mortal man,  
> Could’st by a breath annul and override  
> The immutable unwritten laws of Heaven.  
> They were not born today nor yesterday;  
> They die not; and none knoweth whence they sprang.
>
> — Sophocles, *Antigone* (tr. F. Storr)

Aristotle cited this scene in the *Rhetoric* as an appeal to a natural justice common to all. In the *Nicomachean Ethics* he distinguished natural from conventional justice, and answered an objection that will sound familiar from [Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md):

> But there are some men who think that all the Justs are of this latter kind, and on this ground: whatever exists by nature, they say, is unchangeable and has everywhere the same force; fire, for instance, burns not here only but in Persia as well, but the Justs they see changed in various places.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* V.7 (tr. D. P. Chase)

His reply: natural things can vary too (people are naturally right-handed, yet some become ambidextrous) without ceasing to be natural. The Stoics made these hints a doctrine: the universe is governed by divine reason, of which human reason is a fragment (see [learn-stoicism Chapter 6](../learn-stoicism/06-virtue-and-the-good.md)). Cicero's statement, preserved by the Christian writer Lactantius, became the classic text:

> There is indeed a law, right reason, which is in accordance with nature; existing in all, unchangeable, eternal. Commanding us to do what is right, forbidding us to do what is wrong. … It is not one thing at Rome, and another thing at Athens: one thing to-day, and another thing to-morrow; but it is a law eternal and immutable for all nations and for all time.
>
> — Cicero, *The Republic* III (fragment, tr. G. W. Featherstonhaugh)

Paul gave the idea a biblical footing:

> For when the Gentiles, which have not the law, do by nature the things contained in the law, these, having not the law, are a law unto themselves: … Which shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness
>
> — Romans 2:14–15 (King James Version)

The pieces are in place: a law made by no city, known by reason and conscience, binding everyone, and in some sense divine.

## Aquinas on law

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) wrote the *Summa Theologica*, its prologue says, for the instruction of beginners, which says something about medieval beginners. Its "Treatise on Law" (I-II, qq. 90–108) is the most influential account of natural law ever written. Each article poses a question, lists objections, cites an authority "on the contrary," gives Aquinas's answer ("I answer that") and replies to the objections. Read "I answer that" first.

### What a law is

Aquinas begins by defining law in general:

> Thus from the four preceding articles, the definition of law may be gathered; and it is nothing else than an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community, and promulgated.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I-II, q. 90, a. 4 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

Law is a work of *reason*, aims at the *common good*, comes from proper *authority* and is *made known*. The first feature matters most: a law is a rational plan, and the will that gives it force must follow reason. That is how Aquinas avoids the arbitrariness horn from the first article.

### Four kinds of law

**Eternal law** is God's rational plan for the universe. **Natural law** is eternal law as grasped by rational creatures. **Human law** applies natural law to local circumstances. **Divine law** is revealed in scripture, needed because reason is fallible and our final end is supernatural. Acorns follow eternal law by becoming oaks; humans follow it by understanding it:

> Wherefore it has a share of the Eternal Reason, whereby it has a natural inclination to its proper act and end: and this participation of the eternal law in the rational creature is called the natural law.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I-II, q. 91, a. 2 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

So morality depends on God (question 1: yes) but is accessible without revelation (question 2: no). Paul's Gentiles are the model.

### The first principle and the basic goods

Theoretical reason rests on self-evident (*per se nota*) first principles such as non-contradiction. Practical reason has its own:

> Hence this is the first precept of law, that "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided." All other precepts of the natural law are based upon this
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I-II, q. 94, a. 2 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

This sounds empty, but it is *formal*, like non-contradiction: it says what practical reasoning is *about*. The content comes from natural inclinations:

> Wherefore according to the order of natural inclinations, is the order of the precepts of the natural law. Because in man there is first of all an inclination to good in accordance with the nature which he has in common with all substances: inasmuch as every substance seeks the preservation of its own being, according to its nature: and by reason of this inclination, whatever is a means of preserving human life, and of warding off its obstacles, belongs to the natural law.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I-II, q. 94, a. 2 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

So there are three levels: **as a substance**, we tend to preserve our life; **as an animal**, to what "nature has taught to all animals," such as sexual union and raising offspring; **as a rational being**, "to know the truth about God, and to live in society," whence it belongs to natural law "to shun ignorance, to avoid offending those among whom one has to live." The inclinations are not rules (an inclination to food does not license gluttony). They point reason to the goods of human flourishing, and natural law is the order reason brings to their pursuit. Here Aquinas descends directly from Aristotle ([Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md)).

### Universal but not uniform

Why, then, do moral codes differ? The *general principles* are the same for all, in truth and in being known. *Detailed conclusions* hold "for the majority of cases": goods held in trust should be returned, but not to an owner who wants his weapons back to fight against his country. And knowledge of details can fail:

> thus formerly, theft, although it is expressly contrary to the natural law, was not considered wrong among the Germans, as Julius Caesar relates (De Bello Gall. vi).
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I-II, q. 94, a. 4 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

Compare Herodotus in [Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md). Aquinas reads the same kind of data the other way, as error, "since in some the reason is perverted by passion, or evil habit." Whether that is arrogant or reasonable depends on the case, but the structure is coherent: disagreement about details is compatible with agreement on principles.

### Human law and unjust law

Human law derives from natural law in two ways. Some laws are *conclusions* ("do not kill" from "do harm to no one"). Others are *determinations*: natural law requires that wrongdoers be punished, but not whether by fine or prison, and it is silent on which side of the road to drive. Much of what Herodotus took for relativity of morals is variety of determinations. A human "law" that conflicts with natural law is defective as law:

> But if in any point it deflects from the law of nature, it is no longer a law but a perversion of law.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I-II, q. 95, a. 2 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

Martin Luther King Jr. invoked this doctrine in his "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (1963), citing Augustine and Aquinas to argue that segregation laws, which degrade human personality and are imposed by a majority on a minority denied a voice in making them, are not rooted in eternal and natural law. (The letter is under copyright; read it in full.) Aquinas is careful about what follows. A law can be unjust in its end (serving the ruler's greed), its author (exceeding authority) or its form (unequal burdens):

> The like are acts of violence rather than laws; because, as Augustine says (De Lib. Arb. i, 5), "a law that is not just, seems to be no law at all." Wherefore such laws do not bind in conscience, except perhaps in order to avoid scandal or disturbance, for which cause a man should even yield his right
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I-II, q. 96, a. 4 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

Laws commanding what divine law forbids, such as idolatry, "must nowise be observed." But an unjust tax may be paid to avoid chaos. Natural law gives a standard for judging laws and leaves room for prudence about what to do when they fail it.

### Aquinas and the *Euthyphro*

Aquinas is an intellectualist with a twist: God commands things because they are good, but the standard is not outside God; it is the divine reason itself. This is the ancestor of Adams's view. He faces the hard cases directly. An objector cites Abraham and Isaac, the Israelites taking the Egyptians' goods and Hosea told to marry "a wife of fornications," and concludes that God can change the natural law. Aquinas replies that the cases never fell under the prohibitions:

> Consequently, by the command of God, death can be inflicted on any man, guilty or innocent, without any injustice whatever. … The same applies to theft, which is the taking of another's property. For whatever is taken by the command of God, to Whom all things belong, is not taken against the will of its owner, whereas it is in this that theft consists.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I-II, q. 94, a. 5 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

God, as author of life and owner of everything, has rights no human has, so the act commanded is not murder or theft. This saves the immutability of natural law at the price of a very strong view of God's prerogatives; many readers find the reply more troubling than the problem.

## From God's reason to human reason: Grotius and Hobbes

Aquinas made natural law knowable without revelation, which let later thinkers keep the knowability and drop the theology. Grotius took one step; Hobbes took a larger one. He listed nineteen "laws of nature" (seek peace, keep covenants, show gratitude) and then said what they really were:

> These dictates of Reason, men use to call by the name of Lawes; but improperly: for they are but Conclusions, or Theoremes concerning what conduceth to the conservation and defence of themselves; whereas Law, properly is the word of him, that by right hath command over others. But yet if we consider the same Theoremes, as delivered in the word of God, that by right commandeth all things; then are they properly called Lawes.
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan*, ch. 15

The laws of nature are theorems of prudence that become law only when commanded, by God or, more practically, by the sovereign ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)). The passage is a hinge in the history of ethics: Hobbes is in effect a command theorist about moral *obligation* and an egoist about moral *content* ([Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md)).

## Modern natural law: Grisez and Finnis

By the mid-twentieth century natural law had a reputation among philosophers for crude inferences from biology ("the natural function of X is Y, so using X otherwise is wrong"). In 1965 Germain Grisez reinterpreted Aquinas's first principle of practical reason, and in 1980 the Australian legal philosopher John Finnis built on it in *Natural Law and Natural Rights*. Their view, developed with Joseph Boyle, is the **new natural law theory**.

### The basic goods

Practical reason grasps as self-evident a short list of **basic goods**, good in themselves and not merely as means: **life** (including health and procreation), **knowledge**, **play**, **aesthetic experience**, **sociability or friendship**, **practical reasonableness** (bringing intelligence to bear on one's own choices) and **religion** (broadly, concern with the ultimate source of meaning). Later work added marriage. The goods are **self-evident** (you cannot prove knowledge better than ignorance, but anyone who understands knowledge sees it is worth having) and **incommensurable** (friendship is not worth so many units of knowledge).

### The is/ought objection answered?

Hume complained that moralists slide from *is* to *ought* ([Chapter 8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md)), and the old natural law looked like a textbook case. Finnis replies that this was never Aquinas's argument: the basic goods are not *inferred* from facts about human nature but grasped directly by practical reason, as logical first principles are grasped by theoretical reason. If anything, we understand human nature partly *through* understanding what is good for humans. Critics inside the tradition (Russell Hittinger, Anthony Lisska) object that this cuts natural law off from nature, leaving something closer to intuitionism ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)). They argue that if nature includes teleology, "good for an oak" is already a fact about how oaks flourish, a line Philippa Foot later developed on secular grounds ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)).

### The requirements of practical reasonableness

Since every choice realizes some goods and neglects others, Finnis adds **requirements of practical reasonableness**. In paraphrase:

- Have a coherent plan of life, rather than living from impulse to impulse.
- Make no arbitrary preferences among the basic goods: you may specialize in some, but you must not treat any as worthless.
- Make no arbitrary preferences among persons: a version of the Golden Rule.
- Be detached enough from your projects to abandon them when reason requires, and committed enough not to abandon them lightly.
- Be efficient within reason: consequences matter, but they cannot be summed across incommensurable goods the way utilitarians suppose ([Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)).
- Respect every basic good in every act: never choose to act *directly against* a basic good, even to promote other goods.
- Foster the common good of your communities.
- Follow your conscience.

The sixth does the heavy lifting. Since the goods are incommensurable, you cannot justify attacking one instance of a basic good by pointing to a greater sum elsewhere. So there are **moral absolutes**: never intentionally kill the innocent, never lie, whatever the consequences. This is the sharpest break with consequentialism, and it supplies a rationale for the doctrine of double effect ([Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md)): what matters is whether you *choose against* a good, not merely what you foresee.

### Natural law and rights

Finnis also argued that natural law grounds **human rights**: if the goods are basic for everyone and no one may be arbitrarily preferred, everyone has a claim not to be directly attacked in any of them. Historians trace a line from Aquinas through the Spanish scholastics (Francisco de Vitoria argued for the rights of the indigenous peoples of the Americas) and Grotius to universal human rights. A law "not one thing at Rome, and another thing at Athens" is the natural language of international law.

## Objections to natural law

### What is "natural"?

Disease and earthquakes are natural; vaccines and literacy are not. Mill's essay "Nature" (1874) argued that if "natural" means "whatever happens," then "follow nature" is useless advice, and if it means "what happens without human intervention," it is terrible advice, since nature routinely does to people what we would hang a human being for doing. A defensible natural law must mean something like "fulfilling the flourishing of a rational social animal," and the hard work is specifying that flourishing.

### The perverted faculty argument

Traditional natural law's most controversial applications concern sex. The crude argument: the natural function of the sexual faculty is reproduction; frustrating a faculty's natural function is wrong; contraception frustrates it; so it is wrong. The standard reply is that the middle premise proves too much: sugar-free gum frustrates the stomach and earplugs the ears, and neither is wicked. Defenders say the sexual faculty is special because it serves the species, or (Grisez and Finnis) that the wrong is acting against the basic goods of life and marriage, not frustrating a function. Critics answer that the new theory's conclusions track the old ones too closely for the new premises to be the real reason. Either way, an appeal to function is only as strong as the account of why the function matters morally.

### Evolution and teleology

Aquinas's universe was Aristotelian, every kind having an end set by God. After Darwin, our inclinations exist because they helped ancestors leave descendants, not because they track the good, and some (in-group favoritism, a taste for revenge) should be resisted. The natural lawyer replies that inclinations are only starting points for reason, but that moves the theory from nature toward reason, the direction Finnis took.

### Reasonable disagreement

Natural law claims to be accessible to all, yet many reasonable people reject its conclusions about sex, end-of-life decisions and lying. If the precepts are self-evident, informed disagreement is an embarrassment, and Aquinas's answer ("perverted by passion") can dismiss any dissenter. Rawls's *public reason* ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)) asks citizens to justify coercive laws in terms others could reasonably accept. Natural lawyers say their arguments, relying on reason rather than revelation, pass this test; critics say they persuade only those who already share the conclusions.

### Absolutes and catastrophe

Should you refuse to tell a lie that would save a city? Finnis's theory says yes. Many readers find this a reductio. The debate continues in [Chapter 9](09-kant.md), with Kant's murderer at the door.

## Religion and morality: the empirical question

Now question 3: does *being* good depend on religious belief? John Locke's *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) refused toleration to atheists, since promises and oaths, the bonds of society, could have no hold on them. Pierre Bayle had argued the opposite in 1682: a society of atheists could be orderly, since people act less from professed beliefs than from temperament, habit, reputation and fear of the law. Ivan Karamazov is Locke's worry in fiction.

The worry persists. Will Gervais and colleagues (2017) gave participants in thirteen countries a vignette about a man who tortured animals as a child and later killed five homeless people. In most countries, even secular ones, participants (atheists among them) guessed he was more likely an atheist than a believer. What does the evidence say about the underlying claim?

- **Big Gods.** Ara Norenzayan argues in *Big Gods* (2013) that belief in watchful, moralizing gods helped large societies cooperate beyond kin and acquaintance. Moralizing high gods do tend to appear in larger societies, though whether they caused or followed that complexity is disputed.
- **Priming.** Shariff and Norenzayan (2007) found that people primed with religious words gave more to strangers in an economic game. A large preregistered replication (Gomes and McCullough 2015) found no effect, a caution against strong claims either way.
- **Secular societies.** Some of the least religious countries, such as Denmark, Sweden and Japan, have among the lowest rates of violent crime and the highest social trust (see Phil Zuckerman, *Society without God*, 2008). Wealth and good government may explain both, but this is hard to square with "everything is lawful."
- **Congregations.** Religious Americans give and volunteer more, including to secular causes; much of the effect seems to come from belonging to congregations, dense networks that ask people to help, rather than from belief as such.

Religion is one powerful way humans build moral communities, not the only way, and its absence does not produce Ivan's world. Bayle looks closer to the truth than Locke. Many religious thinkers never accepted the dependence thesis anyway. Kant held that morality must be autonomous, and that God may be *postulated* as guarantor that virtue and happiness coincide but is not the *ground* of obligation ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)). Mill argued that a good God would want what utilitarianism wants:

> If it be a true belief that God desires, above all things, the happiness of his creatures, and that this was his purpose in their creation, utility is not only not a godless doctrine, but more profoundly religious than any other.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *Utilitarianism*, ch. 2

On this picture believers and nonbelievers can argue in a shared language about what is good for human beings, and believers can add that this good has a source and a destiny. That is natural law's offer to a pluralistic world.

## Living it

- **Ask the *Euthyphro* question of your deepest authority**, whether God, scripture, a movement or a mentor. If it endorses X because X is right, try to say *what makes* X right in terms an outsider could accept.
- **Separate the three questions** whenever someone says "morality needs religion." Many disagreements shrink.
- **Distinguish conclusions from determinations.** Which side of the road, how high the tax, at what age adulthood begins: these may vary. Save your fervor for conclusions.
- **Test laws by Aquinas's three marks**: common good, proper authority, fair burdens. Failing one warrants scrutiny, not automatic disobedience.
- **Audit the basic goods.** Is there one (play, beauty, knowledge for its own sake, friendship) you treat as worthless?
- **Check your intuitions about atheists, or about believers**, against the evidence.

## Summary

- Three questions travel together: whether moral facts, moral knowledge and moral behavior depend on God or religion.
- The *Euthyphro* shows that "loved by the gods" is an attribute of piety, not its essence. Restated: is X wrong because God forbids it (voluntarism: arbitrariness, empty praise) or forbidden because wrong (independence)? Grotius took the second horn: natural law cannot be changed "even by God himself."
- Modern divine command theory (Adams, Alston) identifies the good with God's nature and obligation with a loving God's commands. Critics say the dilemma recurs; defenders say every theory stops somewhere.
- Natural law runs from Antigone, Aristotle, the Stoics, Cicero and Paul to Aquinas: law is "an ordinance of reason for the common good"; natural law is participation in eternal law; its first precept is that good is to be done; inclinations indicate the goods. Principles are universal, details hold for the most part; human law adds conclusions and determinations; an unjust law is "a perversion of law," though prudence may counsel compliance.
- Hobbes made the laws of nature "Theoremes" of self-preservation.
- Grisez and Finnis ground ethics in self-evident, incommensurable basic goods and requirements of practical reasonableness, yielding absolutes and human rights while claiming to avoid the is/ought gap.
- Objections: what "natural" means, the perverted faculty argument, Darwin, reasonable disagreement, absolutes.
- Religion supports cooperation but is not necessary for it; anti-atheist prejudice is widespread; secular societies are not lawless.

### Dialogue 1 — Socrates at the town hall

**Socrates:** I see you are in line to speak at the council meeting. You look like someone with a speech prepared.

**You:** …

*Your turn: tell Socrates what you are about to argue, and why you are sure it is right.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** The council wants to approve a casino. I'm going to argue against it. Gambling is wrong. My faith teaches that, and I believe God forbids it.

**Socrates:** A serious matter, and you seem very sure. Perhaps you can help me, because I have never understood something about such cases. Is gambling wrong because God forbids it, or does God forbid it because it is wrong?

**You:** Does it matter? Either way it's wrong.

**Socrates:** For tonight's vote, perhaps not. But for your speech it may matter a great deal. Most of the people on that council do not share your faith. If gambling is wrong only because God forbids it, what reason can you give them?

**You:** I'd tell them what God says.

**Socrates:** And if they reply that they do not believe God says it, or that God does not exist?

**You:** Then I suppose I'd have to give other reasons. It ruins families. People get addicted. Casinos target the poor.

**Socrates:** Those sound like good reasons. Would they be good reasons even if God had said nothing about gambling?

**You:** Yes. Ruining families is bad no matter what.

**Socrates:** Then perhaps God forbids gambling, if he does, *because* of what it does to families, the way a wise father forbids a child to play with fire because fire burns, and not the other way around.

**You:** But then you're saying God doesn't matter to morality.

**Socrates:** I am not saying anything; I am asking. Here is a third possibility, one some of your own theologians have proposed. Perhaps God is goodness itself, so that what is good for families is good because it reflects God's nature, while God commands nothing arbitrary. Would that satisfy you?

**You:** Yes, I think that's what I actually believe.

**Socrates:** Then you can make the same speech to believers and unbelievers alike. You can tell the council what gambling does to families, and those who share your faith can add, privately, why families matter so much. Only one question remains, and you need not answer it tonight. When you said gambling is wrong, did you mean all gambling? The church raffle? A friendly game of cards?

**You:** No, not those. I mean the kind that destroys people.

**Socrates:** Then you know something more precise than "God forbids gambling." You know what it is about some gambling that makes it bad. Begin your speech there, and you may find more allies than you expected.

### Dialogue 2 — Aquinas on an unjust ordinance

**Thomas Aquinas:** You told me your city has passed an ordinance forbidding anyone to share food with homeless people in public parks without a permit, and that permits cost more than your volunteer group can pay. You intend to keep serving meals in the park next Saturday. Let us examine the matter in order, as is my custom.

**You:** …

*Your turn: is the ordinance a law that binds you? What would you need to know to decide?*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It's an unjust law, so it isn't really a law. You said that yourself.

**Aquinas:** I said, following Augustine, that a law that is not just seems to be no law at all. But you must first show that it is unjust. A law is an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by one who has care of the community. Does the city have authority over its parks?

**You:** Yes, I suppose so.

**Aquinas:** Then the defect, if there is one, is not in the author. Is there any reason of the common good behind the ordinance?

**You:** They say it's about food safety and keeping the parks clean.

**Aquinas:** Those are real goods. A city may determine how food is served in public places, just as it may determine which side of the road carts must keep to. Such determinations vary from place to place and are not unjust merely because they are inconvenient. So, objection: the ordinance is a just determination, and you are bound by it.

**You:** But the permit fee is set so high that no volunteer group can pay it. The council members said in a meeting that they want the homeless out of downtown before tourist season.

**Aquinas:** Now you are speaking to the point. If the true end is not the common good but the comfort of some at the expense of the poorest, the law fails in its end. And if the burdens fall on those least able to bear them while others are spared, it fails in its form. Such ordinances are acts of violence rather than laws, and they do not bind in conscience.

**You:** So I can serve the meals.

**Aquinas:** You are not bound in conscience to obey. Whether you *should* disobey is a further question for prudence. I also wrote that a man may yield his right to avoid scandal or disturbance. Will your disobedience help those you feed, or will it lead the city to clear the park and scatter them where no one can find them?

**You:** I hadn't thought of that. Some of our guests are afraid of the police.

**Aquinas:** Then their good, and not your own sense of righteousness, must govern your decision. You might serve them just outside the park, or challenge the fee before the council or in the courts, or do as the minister jailed in Birmingham did and break the law openly and accept the penalty, so that the injustice is seen by all. Each is consistent with the natural law. Choose the one that best serves the common good.

**You:** That's harder than I expected.

**Aquinas:** Practical reason always becomes harder as it descends into particulars. The principles are certain; the conclusions hold for the most part; the choice in this case is yours.

</details>
