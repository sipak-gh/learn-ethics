# Chapter 17 — Metaethics

Most of this course has asked first-order questions: what makes an act right, what a good life is, what we owe to animals or strangers. Metaethics asks about those questions themselves. When you say "torturing for fun is wrong," are you describing a fact, expressing a feeling, issuing a command, or making a mistake? If it is a fact, what kind of fact, and how could you know it? Why does it seem to give you a reason to act, whatever you happen to want? Modern metaethics is usually dated from 1903, when G. E. Moore told the philosophers of Cambridge that "good" could not be defined, and it has been one of the busiest fields in philosophy ever since. In 1936 the twenty-five-year-old A. J. Ayer declared that moral judgments are neither true nor false. In 2011 Derek Parfit published *On What Matters*, more than 1,400 pages long, in part to show that if Ayer and his successors were right, nothing would matter at all. This chapter maps the whole landscape: Moore and intuitionism; emotivism, prescriptivism and modern expressivism (Blackburn, Gibbard); error theory (Mackie, Joyce) and evolutionary debunking (Street); naturalist realism (Cornell realism, Railton); robust non-naturalist realism (Parfit, Enoch, Scanlon); and constructivism (Korsgaard, Street). Moral nihilism and the error theory get their full, existential treatment in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 12](../learn-nihilism/12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md); here they take their place on the map. Previous: [Chapter 16 — Existentialist Ethics](16-existentialist-ethics.md). Next: [Chapter 18 — Moral Psychology and Responsibility](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md).

## The map

Metaethics asks four kinds of question about morality.

- **Semantic:** what do moral words mean, and what is a moral sentence doing? Describing, expressing, commanding?
- **Metaphysical:** are there moral facts or properties? If so, are they natural (part of the world science studies) or something else? Do they depend on anyone's attitudes?
- **Epistemological:** how could we know moral truths, if there are any? By intuition, observation, reflective equilibrium?
- **Psychological:** how are moral judgments connected to motivation? Can you sincerely judge that you ought to do something and not care at all?

Three distinctions organize the answers.

1. **Cognitivism vs non-cognitivism.** Cognitivists hold that moral judgments express *beliefs* that represent the world as being a certain way, and so are apt to be true or false. Non-cognitivists hold that they express *non-cognitive* states (emotions, attitudes, plans, prescriptions).
2. **Realism vs anti-realism.** Moral realists hold that some moral claims are true, and true in virtue of facts that do not depend on anyone's attitudes or choices (the realist answer to the Euthyphro question, below). Anti-realists deny one of these: error theorists deny that any are true; non-cognitivists deny that moral claims state facts at all; constructivists and relativists make the truths depend on attitudes or procedures. (Some constructivists and quasi-realists resist the label "anti-realist," as the table's last column suggests.)
3. **Naturalism vs non-naturalism.** Among realists, naturalists identify moral properties with natural properties; non-naturalists hold that they are *sui generis*, a distinct kind.

The main positions, roughly:

| Position | Moral judgments express | Some are true? | Moral facts attitude-independent? | Key figures |
|----------|------------------------|----------------|-----------------------------------|-------------|
| Non-naturalist realism | beliefs | yes | yes, non-natural | Moore, Ross, Parfit, Enoch, Shafer-Landau, Scanlon (in his way) |
| Naturalist realism | beliefs | yes | yes, natural | Boyd, Sturgeon, Brink, Railton, Jackson, Foot |
| Error theory | beliefs | no (all positive ones false) | there are none | Mackie, Joyce, Olson |
| Constructivism | beliefs (usually) | yes | no: constituted by a procedure or standpoint | Rawls, Korsgaard, Street, Scanlon (contractualism) |
| Emotivism | emotions/attitudes | no | no | Ayer, Stevenson |
| Prescriptivism | universal prescriptions | no (in the strict sense) | no | Hare |
| Expressivism / quasi-realism | attitudes, plans | yes, in a minimal sense | affirmed as a first-order moral claim, denied as metaphysics | Blackburn, Gibbard |

The table is a first approximation. The interesting work happens at the borders, where quasi-realists claim they can say everything the realist says, and where constructivists claim to be realists of a kind. Scanlon appears twice for that reason: he is a constructivist (a contractualist) about the part of morality concerned with what we owe to each other, but a non-naturalist realist about the reasons on which that construction rests.

### The oldest question

The deepest contrast is already in Plato. Socrates asks Euthyphro, who has defined piety as what all the gods love:

> The point which I should first wish to understand is whether the pious or holy is beloved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because it is beloved of the gods.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 10a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Replace "the gods" with "us," or "ideal observers," or "rational agents," and you have the realism question. Is cruelty wrong because we (or idealized versions of us) disapprove of it, or do we disapprove of it because it is wrong? Realists take the second horn. Constructivists, response-dependence theorists and relativists take versions of the first. The religious version of the dilemma is treated in [Chapter 4](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md).

## Moore and the open question

George Edward Moore (1873–1958) was by every account a disarmingly earnest man. His friends in the Cambridge Apostles, and later the Bloomsbury group, treated *Principia Ethica* (1903) as a manifesto, less for its metaethics than for its final chapter, which declares that personal affection and the enjoyment of beauty are by far the greatest goods. Keynes later remembered its effect as a kind of religion. The metaethics was in chapter I, and it set the agenda for a century.

Moore asks what "good" means, and his answer is famously deflating:

> If I am asked 'What is good?' my answer is that good is good, and that is the end of the matter. Or if I am asked 'How is good to be defined?' my answer is that it cannot be defined, and that is all I have to say about it.
>
> — G. E. Moore, *Principia Ethica*, §6

Good is a *simple* notion, like yellow. You can describe the light waves that cause the sensation of yellow, but they are not what you see:

> My point is that 'good' is a simple notion, just as 'yellow' is a simple notion; that, just as you cannot, by any manner of means, explain to any one who does not already know it, what yellow is, so you cannot explain what good is.
>
> — G. E. Moore, *Principia Ethica*, §7

The argument for indefinability is the **open question argument** (§13). Take any proposed definition of good in natural terms, such as "what we desire to desire." Then ask of something that has that property: "but is it good?" The question is still intelligible; it is "open." If "good" *meant* "what we desire to desire," the question would be as closed as "is this bachelor unmarried?"

> whatever definition be offered, it may be always asked, with significance, of the complex so defined, whether it is itself good.
>
> — G. E. Moore, *Principia Ethica*, §13

In premise form:

1. If "good" meant N (some natural property), then "x is N but is x good?" would be a closed question to any competent speaker.
2. For every natural N, that question is open.
3. So "good" does not mean any N.
4. So goodness is not identical to any natural property: it is a simple, non-natural property.

The mistake of identifying good with a natural property Moore called the **naturalistic fallacy**. He thought Bentham, Mill and Spencer committed it, and he thought the same argument refuted defining good in metaphysical terms (as what God wills, or what the true self wills). Moore's view is **non-naturalism**: good is a real property, but not one that science could study. We know what things are good by intuition: self-evident propositions grasped by reflection.

### What went wrong, and what survived

The argument has two famous flaws.

- **The step from 3 to 4.** Water is H₂O, but "this is water, but is it H₂O?" was an open question for most of history. Identities can be discovered *a posteriori*; they need not be analytic. After Kripke and Putnam in the 1970s, this became the standard naturalist reply: "good" might refer to a natural property without *meaning* it.
- **Premise 1 proves too much.** If every correct analysis were obvious to competent speakers, no philosophical analysis could ever be informative (the "paradox of analysis"). W. K. Frankena ("The Naturalistic Fallacy," 1939) added that calling the identification a "fallacy" begs the question: it is a fallacy only if good and N are in fact different.

And yet something survives. Even if the semantics is wrong, the argument dramatizes an intuition many philosophers still share: that normative properties, being *reasons* and *oughts*, are "just too different" from natural ones to be identical with them (Enoch's phrase, in *Taking Morality Seriously*). Parfit calls this the **normativity objection** to naturalism: if "right" just meant "maximizes happiness," then "you ought to do what maximizes happiness" would be the trivial claim that what maximizes happiness maximizes happiness. Since it is not trivial, the normative claim must say something more. That residue keeps Moore alive.

Moore's contemporary Henry Sidgwick had already said something similar about "ought," and Hume, long before, had warned against sliding from facts to oughts:

> What definition can we give of 'ought,' 'right,' and other terms expressing the same fundamental notion? To this I should answer that the notion which these terms have in common is too elementary to admit of any formal definition.
>
> — Henry Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, I.iii.3 (7th ed., 1907)

> In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remarked, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning … when of a sudden I am surprized to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, 3.1.1

Hume's point, the **is/ought gap**, is about inference: no set of purely descriptive premises entails a normative conclusion. Moore's point is about meaning. They are related but distinct, and neither entails realism or anti-realism by itself. Hume's own view, discussed in [Chapter 8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md), was a sentimentalism that later non-cognitivists would claim as their ancestor.

Moore's intuitionism was developed by H. A. Prichard ("Does Moral Philosophy Rest on a Mistake?", 1912) and W. D. Ross (*The Right and the Good*, 1930), whose plural "prima facie duties" (fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, non-maleficence) are still used in applied ethics ([Chapter 19](19-bioethics.md)). By the 1930s, though, intuitionism looked to many like an appeal to a mysterious faculty that perceived mysterious properties. The logical positivists took the next step.

## Emotivism: "Boo!" and "Hurrah!"

Alfred Jules Ayer (1910–1989) wrote *Language, Truth and Logic* after a year in Vienna with the Vienna Circle. Its principle of verification says that a sentence is literally meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable. Moral sentences are neither. So, chapter 6 concludes, they are not literally meaningful: they assert nothing. In Ayer's best-known example, saying "You acted wrongly in stealing that money" adds nothing to the factual content of "You stole that money"; it is as if you had said the latter in a peculiar tone of horror, or with exclamation marks. Ethical words are "pseudo-concepts," expressions of feeling, and also calculated to arouse feeling and stimulate action.

Two consequences follow, and Ayer accepted both. First, there are no ethical *truths* to know, so ethical philosophy reduces to analysis of ethical language plus psychology and sociology. Second, there is no genuine moral *disagreement* about values, only about facts: when we argue about ethics, we are really trying to show the other person that they have the facts wrong, and if they share our values but not our facts, we can hope to persuade them. If the disagreement goes all the way down, argument gives out and we resort to abuse.

Ayer's emotivism was not new. Hume had written that taste, unlike reason, has "a productive faculty," and that the mind gilds the world with its sentiments:

> The one discovers objects as they really stand in nature, without addition and diminution: the other has a productive faculty, and gilding or staining all natural objects with the colours, borrowed from internal sentiment, raises in a manner a new creation.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, Appendix I

> Take any action allowed to be vicious: Wilful murder, for instance. Examine it in all lights, and see if you can find that matter of fact, or real existence, which you call vice. … The vice entirely escapes you, as long as you consider the object.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, 3.1.1

C. L. Stevenson ("The Emotive Meaning of Ethical Terms," 1937; *Ethics and Language*, 1944) gave emotivism its subtlest form. He distinguished **disagreement in belief** (about facts) from **disagreement in attitude** (about what to favor), and argued that moral disputes involve both. Moral words have *emotive meaning*, a tendency to express and evoke attitudes, as well as descriptive meaning. His "first pattern" analysis renders "This is good" roughly as "I approve of this; do so as well." He also analyzed **persuasive definitions**, in which a word with strong emotive meaning ("freedom," "democracy," "culture") is quietly given a new descriptive sense while keeping its emotional charge. That analysis is still one of the best tools for reading political speech.

### The Frege–Geach problem

The most powerful objection to every form of non-cognitivism was put by Peter Geach ("Assertion," 1965), developing a point of John Searle's (1962) and drawing on Frege's distinction between the content of a sentence and the force with which it is asserted. Consider:

1. Lying is wrong.
2. If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong.
3. So getting your little brother to lie is wrong.

This is a valid argument (*modus ponens*). In premise 1, "lying is wrong" is asserted, and the emotivist says it expresses disapproval of lying. But in premise 2 the same words occur *unasserted*, inside a conditional. Someone who accepts premise 2 need not disapprove of lying at all; they may simply think that *if* lying is wrong, so is inciting it. So in premise 2, "lying is wrong" cannot be expressing disapproval. But then the phrase means different things in the two premises, and the argument equivocates, which it plainly does not. The non-cognitivist needs a semantics that explains how moral sentences work in conditionals, negations, questions and attitude reports ("she believes that lying is wrong") as smoothly as factual sentences do. Cognitivists get this for free: a moral sentence expresses a proposition, and propositions embed. This is the **embedding problem**, and every later non-cognitivist has had to solve it.

## Hare: prescriptivism

R. M. Hare (1919–2002), a prisoner of war of the Japanese who worked on the Burma railway, thought emotivism made moral reasoning look like propaganda. In *The Language of Morals* (1952) and *Freedom and Reason* (1963) he argued that moral judgments are a kind of **prescription**, like imperatives, but with two special features:

- **Prescriptivity:** to sincerely accept "I ought to do A" is to be committed to doing A (if able). Moral judgments guide action; they are not reports.
- **Universalizability:** a moral judgment about one case commits you to the same judgment about any case that is the same in its universal properties. If I say I ought to be repaid by my debtor, I must say the same of anyone exactly in my position, including my creditors' right to be repaid by me.

Because imperatives have a logic (Hare built one), moral reasoning is genuinely *reasoning*, not persuasion. Universalizability gives it teeth. Hare's famous case (from *Freedom and Reason*, 1963): a creditor considers jailing his debtor for non-payment. If he universalizes, he must accept that he too should be jailed by *his* creditor in the same situation. By imaginatively occupying everyone's position, Hare argued in *Moral Thinking* (1981), we are pushed to give equal weight to everyone's preferences, which yields a form of preference utilitarianism ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)).

The great objection is the **fanatic**. A committed Nazi who universalizes sincerely, accepting that if he himself turned out to be Jewish he too should be sent to the camps, is not refuted by Hare's logic. Hare admitted this and argued that real fanatics are rare and almost always inconsistent. Critics found the admission damaging: the theory cannot convict the person who most needs convicting. Hare also struggled with the Frege–Geach problem, and his view was widely thought to have been overtaken by more sophisticated expressivisms.

## Expressivism and quasi-realism

Since the 1980s the leading non-cognitivists have called themselves **expressivists**, and they have changed strategy. Instead of saying that moral language is defective (no truth, no knowledge, no real disagreement), they try to *earn* the right to everything ordinary moral talk takes for granted.

### Blackburn's quasi-realism

Simon Blackburn (*Spreading the Mind*, 1984; *Essays in Quasi-Realism*, 1993; *Ruling Passions*, 1998) takes his slogan from Hume's remark in the *Treatise* about causation: the mind, Hume says, has a tendency to project its responses onto the world.

> It is a common observation, that the mind has a great propensity to spread itself on external objects, and to conjoin with them any internal impressions, which they occasion …
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, 1.3.14

**Projectivism** is the view that moral properties are projections of our attitudes: we "spread" our sentiments onto actions and then talk as if wrongness were in them. **Quasi-realism** is the program of showing that, starting from projectivism, we can legitimately say all the things realists say: that moral claims are true or false, that we can know them, that we can be mistaken, that some views are better than others, even that cruelty would be wrong even if nobody disapproved of it.

How can a projectivist say the last thing? Blackburn's answer is ingenious. "Kicking dogs would be wrong even if we approved of it" is itself a *moral* claim, and it expresses an attitude we have: disapproval of kicking dogs that does not depend on whether anyone approves of it. Our sensibility is not one that takes its own approval as the reason for its verdicts. So the expressivist can affirm mind-independence in the only sense it has within moral practice. What the expressivist denies is the extra, *metaphysical* reading, on which wrongness is a fact out there that our attitudes track. Blackburn thinks that reading is idle: it adds nothing to moral practice except bad questions.

For truth, Blackburn relies on **minimalism** (or deflationism): to say "it is true that cruelty is wrong" is just to say that cruelty is wrong. Since the expressivist can say the latter, he can say the former. Knowledge, on this account, is having an attitude that would survive every improvement in information and sensibility.

### Gibbard's norm-expressivism

Allan Gibbard (*Wise Choices, Apt Feelings*, 1990; *Thinking How to Live*, 2003) offers the most formally developed expressivism. To judge that something is rational, or that you ought to do it, is to express acceptance of norms, or, in the later book, a **plan**: a contingency plan for what to do in various circumstances. Gibbard's answer to Frege–Geach models a judgment as a set of pairs, each consisting of a possible world (the factual part) and a "hyperplan" (a fully decided plan for every possible situation). Ordinary factual beliefs rule out worlds; normative judgments rule out hyperplans; mixed judgments like "if lying is wrong, then inciting lies is wrong" rule out world-hyperplan pairs. Logical relations, including *modus ponens*, then fall out of the same machinery as for factual sentences. Gibbard's later work goes further: he argues that a realist's "non-natural normative property" and an expressivist's plan-laden concept may in the end be the same thing described from two sides.

### Problems for expressivism

- **The negation problem.** Mark Schroeder (*Being For*, 2008), following Nicholas Unwin, argues that expressivists cannot explain why "murder is wrong" and "murder is not wrong" are inconsistent. For descriptive beliefs, inconsistency is a matter of content; for attitudes, the expressivist must posit a primitive "inconsistency" between attitudes. Schroeder argues that the only workable fix requires a single general non-cognitive attitude ("being for") with enough structure to mimic propositions, and that it then fails for other constructions.
- **Creeping minimalism.** If the quasi-realist can say everything the realist says ("true," "fact," "mind-independent"), what is left of the disagreement? James Dreier ("Meta-Ethics and the Problem of Creeping Minimalism," 2004) argues that the difference must lie in *explanation*: the expressivist explains moral thought by what it is to have attitudes, not by relation to moral properties. That reply is now widely accepted, but it makes the debate subtler than either side once thought.
- **Moral attitudes and wishful thinking.** If moral judgments are attitudes, why does it seem irrational to change them because it would be convenient? Expressivists reply that our sensibility disapproves of such changes; critics reply that this is the realist's insight dressed up.

**Hybrid** theories (Michael Ridge, *Impassioned Belief*, 2014; Daniel Boisvert; Stephen Barker) try to combine the belief-like and attitude-like aspects of moral judgment: a moral judgment expresses both a belief and an attitude.

## Error theory: morality as a mistake

The error theory accepts the cognitivist semantics and denies the ontology. John Leslie Mackie (1917–1981) opened his *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977) with a famous sentence:

> There are no objective values.
>
> — J. L. Mackie, *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, ch. 1, §1 (Penguin, 1977)

Mackie argued that ordinary moral thought aims to describe **objectively prescriptive** facts: facts that are "out there," and that would give anyone a reason to act whatever they wanted. Moral claims are thus truth-apt, as cognitivists say. But there are no such facts, so all positive moral claims are false. His two arguments:

- **The argument from relativity.** Moral codes vary radically across societies and times. This variation is better explained by the hypothesis that moral codes reflect ways of life than by the hypothesis that they are (mostly distorted) perceptions of objective values.
- **The argument from queerness.** *Metaphysically*, objectively prescriptive properties would be utterly unlike anything else in the universe: properties that somehow have "to-be-pursuedness" built into them. *Epistemologically*, we would need a special faculty to detect them, utterly unlike ordinary perception or inference. And how would such properties relate to the natural features they supervene on? What is the connection between the fact that an act is deliberate cruelty for fun and its wrongness?

Mackie then explains why we believe in objective values anyway: by Hume's projection, by the social usefulness of treating moral demands as non-negotiable, and by the patterns of language that derive moral claims from commands.

Richard Joyce (*The Myth of Morality*, 2001; *The Evolution of Morality*, 2006) sharpened the error theory around **categorical reasons**: moral claims purport to give reasons that apply to you whatever your desires and interests ("practical clout"). There are no such reasons. And he added an evolutionary account of why we are disposed to believe there are: creatures who experience cooperation as *required*, not merely advisable, were more reliable cooperators. Joyce proposes **moral fictionalism**: we should keep using moral language, as a useful fiction that stiffens our resolve, while not believing it in our critical moments. Jonas Olson (*Moral Error Theory*, 2014) defends the error theory with careful attention to queerness and to the idea that error theory need not commit us to nihilism about all reasons.

The standard objections:

- **Companions in guilt.** If categorical reasons are queer, so are epistemic reasons: reasons to believe what the evidence supports, whatever you want. If the error theory is true about epistemic reasons, then there is no reason to believe the error theory (Terence Cuneo, *The Normative Web*, 2007). Error theorists reply that epistemic reasons may be hypothetical, or differ from moral ones.
- **Moorean shift.** I am more certain that torturing babies for fun is wrong than I am of any premise in Mackie's argument. So I should reject a premise.
- **Conceptual objection.** Perhaps "objective prescriptivity" is not part of the *concept* of morality, only a philosopher's gloss; then moral claims could be true in a less demanding sense.

The existential question, what to do if the error theory is true, and the varieties of moral nihilism, are discussed in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 12](../learn-nihilism/12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md). Gilbert Harman's explanatory challenge (*The Nature of Morality*, 1977) belongs here too: if you see children pouring gasoline on a cat and setting it alight, you need not suppose the wrongness explains anything about your observation; your moral upbringing does all the explanatory work. Physical facts earn their place in our ontology by explaining observations; moral facts seem not to. Nicholas Sturgeon's reply ("Moral Explanations," 1985) is that moral facts do figure in good explanations: Hitler's moral depravity is part of the best explanation of what he did, and the injustice of slavery is part of the best explanation of the abolitionist movement.

## Evolutionary debunking

Darwin saw the problem clearly. In *The Descent of Man* he argued that a moral sense would arise in any sufficiently intelligent social animal:

> any animal whatever, endowed with well-marked social instincts … would inevitably acquire a moral sense or conscience, as soon as its intellectual powers had become as well, or nearly as well developed, as in man.
>
> — Charles Darwin, *The Descent of Man* (2nd ed., 1874), ch. IV

And he drew the unsettling corollary, that the content of that moral sense would depend on the species' way of life:

> If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering.
>
> — Charles Darwin, *The Descent of Man* (2nd ed., 1874), ch. IV

Sharon Street's "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" (2006) turned Darwin's point into the most discussed argument in recent metaethics.

1. Natural selection has had a tremendous influence on the content of our basic evaluative attitudes (that our survival is good, that we should care for our children, that we should repay favors, that we should punish cheaters).
2. The realist holds that there are evaluative truths that are independent of our evaluative attitudes.
3. Either there is a relation between the selective pressures and these independent truths, or there is not.
4. *If not*, then the influence of selection has pushed our values in directions that have nothing to do with the truth. Since our current evaluative judgments are thoroughly shaped by that influence, it would be a fantastic coincidence if many of them were true. So the realist faces skepticism.
5. *If so*, the realist must say that selection favored the ability to *track* independent evaluative truths, as it favored the ability to track predators. But that is bad science: the better explanation is that our ancestors who valued their survival and their offspring left more descendants, whether or not those values were "true." The tracking hypothesis is less parsimonious and less clear than this "adaptive link" account.
6. So realism leads either to skepticism or to scientific implausibility.

Street concludes for **constructivism**: evaluative truths are a function of what follows from our evaluative attitudes, so there is no gap between them and our attitudes for evolution to open up.

Replies by realists:

- **The third-factor reply.** David Enoch ("The Epistemological Challenge to Metanormative Realism," 2010) and Erik Wielenberg (2010) argue that there may be a third factor that explains both why we have certain attitudes and why they are true. Survival and flourishing really are good (a normative truth that the realist is entitled to assume in this context); selection favored creatures who valued them; so selection pushed us toward the truth, without any "tracking faculty." Critics say this begs the question.
- **The minimal-overlap reply.** Perhaps selection fixed only our most general tendencies, and reason then refined them. Moral progress, such as the expansion of concern to strangers, other races and animals, runs *against* what selection alone would predict. Michael Huemer ("A Liberal Realist Answer to Debunking Skeptics," 2016) argues that the long-run trend toward liberal values is better explained by realism than by debunking.
- **The overgeneralization reply.** If evolutionary influence debunks moral beliefs, does it also debunk mathematical or logical beliefs, or beliefs in the reliability of perception? Street says no: there the tracking explanation is good science. Others think the line is hard to draw.
- **The selective debunking move.** Some consequentialists (Peter Singer and Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek, *The Point of View of the Universe*, 2014) accept debunking of many intuitions (partiality to kin, the doing/allowing distinction) but argue that the principle of impartial benevolence survives, since it is hard to explain by selection. Guy Kahane ("Evolutionary Debunking Arguments," 2011) warns that such selective debunking is unstable.

## Naturalist realism

Naturalist realists say that moral facts exist, independent of our attitudes, and are natural facts. They take on Moore's open question with the water/H₂O model and Harman's challenge with moral explanations.

### Cornell realism

The name comes from Cornell University, where Richard Boyd, Nicholas Sturgeon and David Brink (*Moral Realism and the Foundations of Ethics*, 1989) worked. Their key moves:

- **Synthetic identity.** "Good" refers to a natural property, but the identity is discovered *a posteriori*, as "water is H₂O" was. So Moore's open question is no objection.
- **Non-reductive naturalism.** Moral properties need not be reducible to the vocabulary of physics, any more than biological properties like "predator" are. They are natural in that they are constituted by natural properties and figure in explanations.
- **Homeostatic property clusters.** Boyd's "How to Be a Moral Realist" (1988) suggests that "good" refers to a cluster of human goods (health, friendship, understanding, self-respect…) that tend to occur together and support one another, held together by causal mechanisms. Moral inquiry discovers this cluster as biology discovers species.
- **Causal regulation.** Our use of "good" is causally regulated by the goodness-cluster, as our use of "water" is regulated by H₂O.

The major objection is **Moral Twin Earth** (Terence Horgan and Mark Timmons, 1991). Imagine a planet where people use "good" exactly as we do, but their use is causally regulated by a different cluster of properties (say, one more like a deontological profile while ours is consequentialist). By the Cornell semantics, their "good" and ours refer to different properties, so when we and the Twin Earthlings disagree about whether an act is "good," we are not really disagreeing. But intuitively we *are* disagreeing. So the meaning of "good" is not fixed by causal regulation alone.

### Railton's reforming naturalism

Peter Railton ("Moral Realism," 1986; "Facts and Values," 1986) offers a more reductive naturalism. Start with a person's **non-moral good**: what is good for A is what A's fully informed and rational self (A+) would want A to want, if A+ were contemplating A's situation. This is a natural fact about A's psychology and circumstances. Then **moral rightness** is what is rational from the social point of view: what would be approved from an impartial standpoint giving equal weight to all the interests involved. These definitions are *reforming*: they do not claim to capture the meaning of our words but to explain the role that moral notions play, and they earn their keep by explaining, among other things, why societies that frustrate many people's interests generate discontent and pressure for change. Moral facts thus play a role in social explanation, much as Sturgeon suggested. Railton accepts that this vindicates less "categorical" force than morality claims; he is happy to bite that bullet.

Other naturalisms: Frank Jackson's **analytical descriptivism** (*From Metaphysics to Ethics*, 1998) identifies moral properties with whatever descriptive properties play the roles fixed by "mature folk morality," the ideal endpoint of reflection on our moral platitudes. Philippa Foot's **natural goodness** (*Natural Goodness*, 2001) treats moral evaluation as a species of the evaluation of living things, as a wolf that does not hunt with the pack is defective; see [Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md).

## Robust non-naturalist realism

Many of the most prominent recent metaethicists are non-naturalists, returning to Moore's conclusion by better routes. Their shared view: there are normative truths, they are not natural facts, and they are not constituted by anyone's attitudes.

### Parfit

Derek Parfit (1942–2017) spent the last twenty years of his life on *On What Matters*. His metaethics rests on **reasons**: the fact that a burning building would kill you counts in favor of jumping into the river. Such facts are irreducibly normative. They cannot be identified with natural facts, because then normative claims would become trivial (the normativity objection above). Parfit at first called his view non-naturalist realism, and later "Non-Realist Cognitivism" or "non-metaphysical cognitivism": normative truths are true, but they do not require the existence of strange entities in any ontological sense, any more than mathematical truths require numbers to be hanging in space. The claim is that some things matter, and that it is true that they matter.

Parfit also argued that the three main normative theories converge: the best version of Kantianism, the best version of contractualism and the best version of rule consequentialism coincide in a "Triple Theory." In a well-known image he described their proponents as climbing the same mountain on different sides. And he took the stakes personally. If there were no normative truths, he argued, nothing would matter, and he suggested that if non-cognitivists and naturalists were right, much of his own life's work would have been wasted. His critics (in Peter Singer, ed., *Does Anything Really Matter?*, 2017) include Blackburn, Gibbard and Street, who argue that nothing they believe implies that nothing matters.

### Enoch

David Enoch (*Taking Morality Seriously*, 2011) defends **robust realism** with two arguments.

- **The deliberative indispensability argument.** We are justified in believing in entities that are indispensable to explanation (as we believe in electrons). By parity, we are justified in believing in entities that are indispensable to *deliberation*: to the project of deciding what to do. When you deliberate, you try to find out what you have most reason to do, as if there were an answer to be found. Robust normative truths are indispensable to that project, which itself is not optional for agents like us. So we are justified in believing in them.
- **The argument from impartiality.** Some disagreements are merely preference-based (you like chocolate, I like vanilla), and fairness requires splitting the difference. Others are factual, and fairness does not. Moral disagreements, we think, are not the first kind: it would be absurd to resolve a dispute about whether to torture someone by meeting halfway. That suggests we regard them as factual.

Enoch admits the cost: epistemology. His answer to Street is the third-factor reply sketched above.

### Scanlon and the quietist turn

T. M. Scanlon (*Being Realistic about Reasons*, 2014) defends **reasons fundamentalism**: the relation of "being a reason for" is basic and irreducible. But he deflates its metaphysical cost. Different domains (mathematics, normativity, physics) have their own standards for what exists. In the normative domain, a claim that there is a reason is true if it holds up by the domain's standards, which are found by reflective equilibrium. There is no further question whether reasons "really exist" from outside all domains. Critics call this **quietism** and wonder whether it is too quiet, whether it answers the skeptic or merely declines to hear him. Scanlon's contractualist normative theory, which also has a constructivist flavor about the domain of "what we owe to each other," is discussed in [Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md). Russ Shafer-Landau (*Moral Realism: A Defence*, 2003) offers a related non-naturalism that models moral properties on the way mental properties are realized by physical ones without being identical to them.

### Objections to non-naturalism

- **Supervenience.** Moral properties *supervene* on natural ones: two situations alike in every natural respect must be alike morally. Blackburn ("Supervenience Revisited," 1985) asks why. For the expressivist, supervenience is a requirement of consistency in our attitudes. For the non-naturalist, it is a brute necessary connection between distinct existences: a mystery.
- **Epistemology.** Mackie's queerness argument and Street's dilemma both bite hardest here. Non-naturalists generally appeal to intuitions, understood as intellectual seemings (Huemer, *Ethical Intuitionism*, 2005), and to reflective equilibrium. The question is why such seemings should be reliable about a causally inert realm.
- **Motivation.** If moral facts are simply facts, why should knowing one move anyone? See the internalism debate below.

## Constructivism

Constructivists try to occupy the space between realism and expressivism. Moral truths exist, and moral claims are beliefs that can be true or false; but what makes them true is not a realm of independent facts. It is the outcome of a procedure, or what follows from the standpoint of a valuing agent.

### Rawls and Korsgaard

John Rawls named the view in "Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory" (1980): the principles of justice are not discovered but constructed, as what free and equal persons would agree to under fair conditions (the original position; see [Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)). Christine Korsgaard generalized the idea into a metaethics.

In *The Sources of Normativity* (1996), Korsgaard asks the **normative question**: when morality makes a demand on me that I would rather not meet, what justifies its claim on me? She argues that voluntarism (obligation comes from a sovereign's command), realism (obligation is a fact) and Humean sentimentalism (reflective endorsement of our sentiments) all fail to answer it as asked from the first-person standpoint. Realism, in particular, just insists that the obligation exists, which is no help to someone who doubts it. Her own answer:

1. Human consciousness is **reflective**: we can step back from our impulses and ask whether to act on them. So we need reasons.
2. A reason is an impulse that we endorse on the basis of a conception of ourselves, a **practical identity** (as a parent, a citizen, a scientist, a friend).
3. Practical identities are contingent, but the need to have *some* practical identity is not: without one you could not act at all.
4. So you must value your humanity, your nature as a reflective being who needs reasons.
5. Valuing your own humanity commits you, on reflection, to valuing humanity in others (Korsgaard argues, against egoism, that reasons are public and shareable).
6. So moral obligation follows from the structure of reflective agency itself.

Korsgaard's later books (*Self-Constitution*, 2009) develop the idea that action is self-constitution, and that the categorical imperative is constitutive of agency: to act at all is to be committed to its standards. The deepest objection is Enoch's ("Agency, Shmagency," 2006): suppose I grant that being an agent commits me to certain standards. Why should I care about being an agent rather than a "shmagent," something that acts but does not care about meeting the constitutive standards? Korsgaard replies that the question cannot coherently be asked from outside agency, since asking it is already deliberating. Whether that reply is enough is one of the open questions in the field.

### Street's Humean constructivism

Street ("Constructivism about Reasons," 2008; "What Is Constructivism in Ethics and Metaethics?", 2010) offers a more modest constructivism. The truth of a normative claim consists in its following, by the standards of correctness constitutive of valuing, from the evaluative attitudes of the agent who makes it. This **Humean constructivism** does not guarantee that every agent has reason to be moral: an "ideally coherent Caligula," who values torturing others and has fully consistent attitudes, would have no reason to stop. Street accepts this result as the price of answering the Darwinian dilemma. Korsgaard's **Kantian constructivism** claims to avoid it, by deriving morality from the standpoint of any agent as such.

### James's pragmatist constructivism

An earlier, wonderfully readable constructivism is William James's lecture to the Yale Philosophical Club in 1891, "The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life." James asks where obligation could come from in a world without God, and answers: from *claims*. There is no obligation in a world with a single mind (he calls this "a moral solitude"), but as soon as there are two:

> Wherever such minds exist, with judgments of good and ill, and demands upon one another, there is an ethical world in its essential features. Were all other things, gods and men and starry heavens, blotted out from this universe, and were there left but one rock with two loving souls upon it, that rock would have as thoroughly moral a constitution as any possible world which the eternities and immensities could harbor.
>
> — William James, "The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life," §II, in *The Will to Believe* (1897)

Value and obligation are lodged in actual demands, not in an abstract order:

> If one ideal judgment be objectively better than another, that betterness must be made flesh by being lodged concretely in some one's actual perception. It cannot float in the atmosphere, for it is not a sort of meteorological phenomenon, like the aurora borealis or the zodiacal light.
>
> — William James, "The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life," §II, in *The Will to Believe* (1897)

James's conclusion is that ethics is unfinished and experimental: "the essence of good is simply to satisfy demand," and since demands conflict, the best act is the one that satisfies as many demands as possible at the least cost to others. He compares ethics to physics in a striking phrase: there can be no final truth in either "until the last man has had his experience and said his say." James is a forerunner of both constructivism and of the reflective-equilibrium method that most metaethicists, of every school, now use.

### Is constructivism really a metaethics?

A common objection is that constructivism collapses. Either the procedure's outputs are correct because they track independent truths (and then it is realism), or they are correct simply because they are its outputs (and then why that procedure rather than another?). Constructivists answer that the procedure is privileged because it articulates the standpoint of practical reason itself, and that its outputs are not "tracked" but constituted, as the rules of chess constitute legal moves. Some (Street herself) concede that constructivism is best understood as a form of anti-realism that keeps truth and objectivity in a moderated sense.

## Motivation and the "moral problem"

One more question cuts across the map. **Motivational internalism** says that if you sincerely judge that you ought to do something, you are necessarily motivated (at least somewhat) to do it. **Externalism** says the connection is contingent: an "amoralist" could judge that she ought to help and simply not care (David Brink, 1989; Sigrún Svavarsdóttir, 1999).

Michael Smith (*The Moral Problem*, 1994) formulated the puzzle as three plausible claims that seem jointly inconsistent:

1. Moral judgments express beliefs (cognitivism).
2. Moral judgments are necessarily connected to motivation (internalism).
3. Motivation requires a desire as well as a belief, and beliefs and desires are distinct existences: no belief necessitates a desire (the Humean theory of motivation, from [Chapter 8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md)).

Non-cognitivists reject 1. Externalist realists reject 2. Smith rejects a version of 3: judging that you ought to do something is believing that your fully rational self would want you to do it, and being rational involves wanting what you believe your rational self would want. Most of the positions on the map can be read as choices about which of these three to give up.

## Where the debate stands

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey of professional philosophers, 62 percent accepted or leaned toward moral realism and 26 percent toward anti-realism (David Bourget and David Chalmers, "Philosophers on Philosophy," 2023). That is a majority, but the survey does not separate naturalist from non-naturalist realism, and it includes many philosophers who would describe themselves as quasi-realists or constructivists willing to use the word "realism." Realism has gained ground since the 2009 survey, when the figure was about 56 percent.

Some trends:

- The sharp line between cognitivism and non-cognitivism has blurred, because of minimalism about truth, hybrid theories and Gibbard's late convergence.
- Epistemology has become the central battlefield, driven by evolutionary debunking arguments and by work on moral disagreement (peer disagreement in ethics; see [Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md)).
- **Metanormativity** has broadened the subject: many arguments now concern reasons in general, including epistemic reasons, rather than morality alone ("companions in guilt").
- Empirical moral psychology ([Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md)) bears on old questions: whether ordinary people are folk realists (the evidence suggests they are realists about some moral claims and relativists about others: Goodwin and Darley, 2008; Pölzler and Wright, 2020), and whether moral judgment is driven by emotion.

## Living it

Metaethics may seem the least livable branch of ethics. It is not. Your metaethics shapes how you hold your values under pressure: in disagreement, in doubt, in grief. Try this.

1. **Locate yourself on the map.** Take three moral judgments you are very confident of (for example: torturing a child for fun is wrong; you should keep a promise to a dying friend; it is unfair to pay women less for the same work). For each, ask the Euthyphro question: would it be wrong even if everyone, including you after reflection, approved of it? Write down your first answer and then the best case for the opposite. Do your answers differ across the three? Many people turn out to be realists about cruelty and constructivists about fairness.
2. **Run Street's test on one belief.** Pick a moral conviction you hold strongly, and ask whether it would be held by creatures like us whether or not it was true, because of its evolutionary or social payoff. Partiality toward family is a good candidate; equal concern for distant strangers is a harder one. Then ask what follows for how firmly you hold it.
3. **Notice the "moral solitude" test.** James says there is no obligation in a world with one mind. Think of a moral demand you feel and ask *whose* demand it is: a person, a group, an animal, a future generation, your own ideal self, or "the universe." If you can't find a claimant, is the demand still binding?
4. **Practice disagreement as Enoch describes it.** The next time you disagree morally with someone, notice whether you would accept "meeting halfway." Where you would not, you are treating the question as having an answer. Ask what you think makes it so.

The aim is not to choose a label but to find out what kind of authority you are already granting your values, so that you can hold them neither dogmatically nor as mere preference.

## Summary

- Metaethics asks what moral judgments mean, whether there are moral facts, how we could know them, and how they connect to motivation. The main divides are cognitivism/non-cognitivism, realism/anti-realism and naturalism/non-naturalism. The Euthyphro question is their ancestor.
- Moore's open question argument concluded that good is a simple non-natural property. It fails as semantics (synthetic identities, the paradox of analysis) but leaves the "just too different" intuition that drives the normativity objection to naturalism. Hume's is/ought gap is a related but distinct point.
- Emotivism (Ayer, Stevenson) treats moral judgments as expressions and arousals of attitude; Hare's prescriptivism adds universalizability and reasoning. All non-cognitivism faces the Frege–Geach embedding problem.
- Modern expressivism (Blackburn's quasi-realism, Gibbard's plan-expressivism) tries to earn truth, knowledge and mind-independence talk from within. It faces the negation problem and the challenge of distinguishing itself from realism (creeping minimalism).
- Mackie's error theory holds that moral claims presuppose objectively prescriptive values, which do not exist (arguments from relativity and queerness). Joyce adds categorical reasons, evolutionary explanation and fictionalism. Companions-in-guilt and Moorean replies push back. See the Nihilism course for the full treatment.
- Street's Darwinian dilemma argues realism leads to skepticism or bad science. Realists offer third-factor replies, arguments from moral progress and charges of overgeneralization.
- Naturalist realists (Cornell realism, Railton, Jackson, Foot) identify moral properties with natural ones by synthetic identity or reforming definition; Moral Twin Earth is their main problem.
- Non-naturalist realists (Parfit, Enoch, Shafer-Landau, Scanlon) defend irreducibly normative truths, via the normativity objection, deliberative indispensability and the impartiality argument; supervenience and epistemology are their problems.
- Constructivists (Rawls, Korsgaard, Street; James before them) make moral truth the output of practical reason's standpoint. Korsgaard derives obligation from reflective agency and practical identity; Enoch's "shmagency" objection is the main challenge.
- Smith's "moral problem" shows how positions trade off cognitivism, internalism and the Humean theory of motivation.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether "good" just means "makes people happy"

**G. E. Moore:** You told me that "good" simply means "what makes people happy." Do you still hold it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Moore in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Yes. When we call something good, we mean it makes people happy. What else could we mean?

**Moore:** Then consider a drug that makes everyone permanently and blissfully happy while they lie in vats. Is it good?

**You:** I'm not sure. Maybe not.

**Moore:** You hesitate. But if "good" *meant* "makes people happy," your question would be like asking whether a bachelor is unmarried. Is it?

**You:** No. The question "it makes people happy, but is it good?" makes sense.

**Moore:** Then "good" does not mean "makes people happy." And the same will be true of any definition you propose.

**You:** But water is H₂O, and "it's water, but is it H₂O?" made sense to people in 1700. That didn't show water wasn't H₂O.

**Nicholas Sturgeon:** Exactly. Goodness might be identical to some natural property, discovered by moral inquiry the way chemistry discovered H₂O. The open question shows only that the identity is not analytic.

**Moore:** And how would moral inquiry discover it? Chemists isolated water and examined it. What instrument detects goodness?

**Sturgeon:** The same one that detects most theoretical properties: their role in explaining things. Injustice explains social unrest; cruelty explains suffering and resentment.

**Derek Parfit:** But there is a difference. If "water is H₂O" is true, nothing normative follows. If "good is what maximizes happiness" were a mere identity, then "you ought to do what is good" would mean "you ought to do what maximizes happiness," which is not trivial; it tells you something. A natural identity cannot capture that.

**You:** So the problem isn't really about meaning. It's that a normative claim seems to add something no description does.

**Moore:** That is what I meant by calling good a simple, non-natural quality.

**You:** Maybe. But I could also say, with the expressivists, that what it adds is my endorsement, not a strange property.

**Moore:** Then you must tell me why your endorsement could be mistaken.

**You:** So I've learned that my definition was really a substantive moral claim, that happiness is what matters, not a truth about meaning. That's a claim I'll have to argue for, not one I can win by definition.

### Dialogue 2 — On the Darwinian dilemma

**Sharon Street:** You believe that some moral truths hold independently of anyone's attitudes. Do you also believe that natural selection shaped your moral attitudes?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Street in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what she will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Yes, both. Evolution gave us the capacity for moral thought, and that capacity finds moral truths.

**Street:** Finds them how? Did our ancestors who perceived independent moral truths leave more offspring *because* they perceived them truly?

**You:** Maybe. People who thought killing kin was wrong probably survived better.

**Street:** Better because it is *true* that killing kin is wrong, or because not killing kin helps your genes? The second explanation is enough. It needs no moral truths at all.

**Darwin:** I once wrote that if men were reared like hive-bees, our unmarried females would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers. The moral sense follows the conditions of life.

**You:** Then the realist must say the fit between our values and the truth is a coincidence?

**Street:** Either a coincidence, which makes your confidence unjustified, or a tracking relation, which is bad biology. That is the dilemma.

**David Enoch:** There is a third possibility. Suppose it is true that survival and the flourishing of one's offspring are good. Then selection, by pushing us to value them, pushed us toward the truth, though not by detecting it. The correlation is explained by a third factor: what is good and what promotes reproduction overlap.

**Street:** You assume a normative truth in order to show that our normative beliefs are reliable. Isn't that circular?

**Enoch:** Every defense of a basic faculty is circular in that way. You trust perception using perception.

**You:** But some of my moral beliefs seem to go against evolution: caring about strangers on the other side of the world, or about animals.

**Street:** Those may be extensions by reasoning from the evolved core. My constructivism can explain them as what follows from your other values.

**You:** And realism can explain them as progress toward the truth.

**Socrates:** Then you two agree that reasoning refines what nature gave. You disagree about what the refinement aims at.

**You:** So the dilemma doesn't refute realism outright, but it shifts the burden. I need either the third-factor story or an argument that the values I'm most confident of aren't the ones that evolution would have given me anyway. That's a useful test for any belief I hold.

### Dialogue 3 — On whether quasi-realism is realism in disguise

**Simon Blackburn:** You call yourself an anti-realist about morality. Do you think it is true that slavery was wrong?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Blackburn in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Well, it's true *for me*. I disapprove of it. But I can't say it's true in some absolute sense.

**Blackburn:** Then if the slaveholders approved of slavery, it was true for them that slavery was fine?

**You:** That sounds terrible, but I suppose so.

**Blackburn:** It is terrible, and you need not say it. To say that slavery was wrong is to express your disapproval of it. To say it is *true* that slavery was wrong is simply to say again that slavery was wrong. You can say that without any "for me."

**You:** But my disapproval is just mine. How can it make slavery wrong for everyone?

**Blackburn:** It does not *make* it wrong. Would slavery have been wrong if everyone had approved of it?

**You:** Yes, it would.

**Blackburn:** Good. That is a moral judgment, and you made it from within your own sensibility: you disapprove of slavery whether or not anyone approves of it. My view lets you say so.

**Derek Parfit:** Then what is the difference between you and me? You say slavery is wrong, that it is true, that it would be wrong whatever we thought.

**Blackburn:** The difference is in the explanation. You think you need a realm of irreducibly normative truths to make sense of what we are doing. I think all we need is the psychology of creatures who have attitudes and can reflect on them.

**Parfit:** But if moral claims are expressions of attitudes, then a person with different attitudes is not mistaken in my sense, only different.

**Blackburn:** He is mistaken in every sense that matters to how you treat him. What extra sense is there?

**You:** Maybe this: when I doubt whether my values are right, I'm not just checking my attitudes. I'm asking whether they answer to something.

**Blackburn:** And when you check, what do you do? You gather facts, imagine the other side, test for consistency. That's improving a sensibility. Where does the "something" enter?

**You:** I can't point to it. But I can't drop the sense that it's there.

**Blackburn:** Then that sense is part of the phenomenon to be explained, not evidence for your metaphysics.

**You:** So the dispute has moved from what we can *say* to what best *explains* our saying it. I came in as a relativist; I'm leaving as someone who can say "slavery was wrong, full stop," and who still has to choose between your explanation and Parfit's.

</details>
