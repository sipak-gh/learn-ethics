# Chapter 10 — Classical Utilitarianism: Bentham, Mill, Sidgwick

Kant located morality in the form of the will. The utilitarians located it in the world: in how much happiness and misery there is, and in who feels it. Their idea is so simple that it can be stated in a breath (do whatever produces the most happiness, counting everyone equally) and so radical that it has fueled two centuries of reform and two centuries of horrified counterexamples. This chapter follows the theory through its three classical authors. Jeremy Bentham built it as a tool for reforming law. John Stuart Mill humanized it, defended it against the charge of being a "doctrine worthy only of swine," and tied it to liberty and the emancipation of women. Henry Sidgwick, the most careful of the three, tested it against common-sense morality and found an unresolved contradiction at the heart of practical reason. Along the way you will meet the felicific calculus, the higher pleasures, Mill's notorious "proof," the act/rule distinction, and the objections that drive the next chapter. Previous: [Chapter 9 — Kant: Duty, Dignity, and the Moral Law](09-kant.md). Next: [Chapter 11 — Contemporary Consequentialism](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md).

## The man in the glass box

If you visit University College London, you can still meet Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832). He sits in a glass-fronted cabinet, dressed in his own clothes, holding his walking stick (he called it "Dapple"), his skeleton padded out with straw. The head is wax; the real one, mummified rather badly, is kept elsewhere. Bentham left instructions for this "Auto-Icon" in his will, partly as a joke and partly as a utilitarian statement: a dead body should be useful, and his could serve both anatomy and the memory of his ideas.

Bentham trained as a lawyer and was appalled by English law, which he thought a tangle of fictions protecting the powerful. His project was to rebuild law and government on a single rational foundation. The causes he argued for read like a list of what later became common sense, and some that were dangerous to publish in his lifetime: legal equality for women, the abolition of slavery and of corporal punishment, the decriminalization of homosexual acts (an essay he wrote around 1785 and never dared print), the protection of animals, a wide franchise, and the secret ballot. He also designed the *Panopticon*, a prison in which a single unseen inspector could watch every inmate, which Michel Foucault would later make a symbol of modern surveillance. Bentham was a reformer, an eccentric and, for the critics who followed him, the man who tried to reduce morality to arithmetic.

## The principle of utility

The *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (printed 1780, published 1789) opens with a flourish that Bentham immediately apologizes for:

> Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do. On the one hand the standard of right and wrong, on the other the chain of causes and effects, are fastened to their throne.
>
> — Bentham, *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, ch. 1, §1 (Clarendon ed., 1907)

A few lines later: "But enough of metaphor and declamation: it is not by such means that moral science is to be improved." Then the definition:

> By the principle of utility is meant that principle which approves or disapproves of every action whatsoever, according to the tendency which it appears to have to augment or diminish the happiness of the party whose interest is in question.
>
> — Bentham, *Principles of Morals and Legislation*, ch. 1, §2

Notice that the first quotation makes two claims at once. Pleasure and pain determine what we *shall* do (psychological hedonism, a descriptive claim about motivation) and what we *ought* to do (ethical hedonism, a normative claim). Later utilitarians, starting with Sidgwick, dropped the first claim; it is false (people often act against their own pleasure) and the theory does not need it.

Contemporary philosophers break classical utilitarianism into separable components. Keeping them apart lets you see exactly what each objection targets:

1. **Consequentialism.** The rightness of an act depends only on its consequences.
2. **Welfarism.** The only consequences that matter are effects on well-being.
3. **Hedonism.** Well-being consists in pleasure and the absence of pain.
4. **Sum-ranking (aggregation).** The value of an outcome is the *sum* of individual well-being.
5. **Impartiality.** Each person's well-being counts equally. Mill attributes to Bentham the dictum "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one."
6. **Maximization.** The right act is the one that produces the *best* outcome, not merely a good-enough one.

Abandon hedonism and keep the rest, and you get preference or objective-list utilitarianism. Abandon maximization, and you get "satisficing" consequentialism. Abandon impartiality, and you get something like egoism. Abandon consequentialism entirely, and you are in Kant's territory or the virtue ethicist's.

Bentham's polemical edge was aimed at the alternatives. He thought appeals to natural rights, moral sense, the law of nature or the will of God were masks for the speaker's own prejudices. He called the doctrine of natural and imprescriptible rights "nonsense upon stilts" (in *Anarchical Fallacies*, written against the French Declaration of the Rights of Man). Any principle other than utility, he argued in chapter 2, turns out on inspection to be either *asceticism* (approving of actions because they reduce pleasure) or *sympathy and antipathy* (approving of what the speaker happens to like).

## The felicific calculus

If pleasure and pain are what matter, you need a way to measure them. Bentham's chapter 4 lists the dimensions along which a pleasure or pain is to be valued:

1. **Intensity**
2. **Duration**
3. **Certainty** or uncertainty
4. **Propinquity** or remoteness (how soon)
5. **Fecundity** (the chance it will be followed by more of the same kind)
6. **Purity** (the chance it will *not* be followed by sensations of the opposite kind)
7. **Extent** (the number of persons affected)

To evaluate an act, you sum the values of all the pleasures and pains it produces for each person affected, then sum across persons. Later writers called this the *felicific calculus*. Bentham conceded that it need not be "strictly pursued previously to every moral judgment," but held that it should "be always kept in view" (ch. 4, §6).

There is no need to mock the calculus. Every cost-benefit analysis, every quality-adjusted life year (QALY) in health economics, every regulatory impact assessment is its descendant. The serious objections are three:

- **Measurement.** Can pleasures be measured on a single scale at all? Is a toothache comparable with a lost friendship?
- **Interpersonal comparison.** Even if you can measure your own pleasures, can you compare yours with mine? Economists after Lionel Robbins (1932) largely gave up on such comparisons, which is why welfare economics shifted to preferences and Pareto improvements.
- **Content.** Bentham seems indifferent to *what* the pleasure is in. The famous line "quantity of pleasure being equal, push-pin is as good as poetry" is often quoted as his, but it is a paraphrase, popularized by Mill's 1838 essay on Bentham and repeated by Sidgwick as "Bentham's illustration." Bentham's actual sentence, in *The Rationale of Reward* (1825), says that, prejudice apart, the children's game of push-pin is of equal value with the arts and sciences of music and poetry. His point was egalitarian: the pleasures of the poor are not inferior to those of the cultivated rich.

## Can they suffer?

Impartiality, followed consistently, does not stop at the species boundary. In a footnote to chapter 17, Bentham compares the legal status of animals to that of slaves:

> It may come one day to be recognized, that the number of the legs, the villosity of the skin, or the termination of the os sacrum, are reasons equally insufficient for abandoning a sensitive being to the same fate. What else is it that should trace the insuperable line? Is it the faculty of reason, or, perhaps, the faculty of discourse? … the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?
>
> — Bentham, *Principles of Morals and Legislation*, ch. 17, §1, note

Compare Kant, for whom only rational beings are ends in themselves ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)). The utilitarian criterion of moral standing is *sentience*, the capacity to feel pleasure and pain. Peter Singer's *Animal Liberation* (1975) takes this footnote as its starting point; see [Chapter 20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md).

## Mill: the crisis and the higher pleasures

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) was raised to be the next leader of the Benthamite movement. His father James Mill, Bentham's close ally, educated him at home in an experiment of forced intellectual growth:

> I have no remembrance of the time when I began to learn Greek; I have been told that it was when I was three years old.
>
> — Mill, *Autobiography*, ch. 1

By his teens he was editing Bentham's manuscripts. At twenty, in the autumn of 1826, he broke down. The *Autobiography* tells the story with clinical honesty:

> In this frame of mind it occurred to me to put the question directly to myself: "Suppose that all your objects in life were realized; that all the changes in institutions and opinions which you are looking forward to, could be completely effected at this very instant: would this be a great joy and happiness to you?" And an irrepressible self-consciousness distinctly answered, "No!" At this my heart sank within me: the whole foundation on which my life was constructed fell down.
>
> — Mill, *Autobiography*, ch. 5

He recovered, he says, partly through tears over a memoir by Marmontel and partly through Wordsworth's poetry, which gave him the "culture of the feelings" his education had starved. He drew a lesson that every reader of the next chapter's effective altruists should know, the so-called *paradox of hedonism*:

> Ask yourself whether you are happy, and you cease to be so. The only chance is to treat, not happiness, but some end external to it, as the purpose of life.
>
> — Mill, *Autobiography*, ch. 5

This is not a rejection of utilitarianism. Happiness remains the standard of right action; Mill's point is that it is a poor *target* for an individual's attention. That distinction, between the *criterion* of right action and the *decision procedure* an agent should use, is one of the most important in this chapter.

The crisis also explains the most famous move in *Utilitarianism* (1861, book form 1863). Mill states the creed:

> The creed which accepts as the foundation of morals, Utility, or the Greatest Happiness Principle, holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness. By happiness is intended pleasure, and the absence of pain; by unhappiness, pain, and the privation of pleasure.
>
> — Mill, *Utilitarianism*, ch. 2

Critics called this "a doctrine worthy only of swine." Mill turns the insult around: it is the critics who suppose humans capable only of pig-like pleasures. Pleasures differ in *quality* as well as quantity, and there is a test:

> Of two pleasures, if there be one to which all or almost all who have experience of both give a decided preference, irrespective of any feeling of moral obligation to prefer it, that is the more desirable pleasure.
>
> — Mill, *Utilitarianism*, ch. 2

And the verdict of the "competent judges" is clear:

> It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. And if the fool, or the pig, is of a different opinion, it is because they only know their own side of the question. The other party to the comparison knows both sides.
>
> — Mill, *Utilitarianism*, ch. 2

Why do people with access to the higher pleasures sometimes sink into the lower ones? Mill's answer is touching: "Capacity for the nobler feelings is in most natures a very tender plant, easily killed, not only by hostile influences, but by mere want of sustenance." Those who lose their high aspirations, he thinks, have usually lost the *capacity* for them first.

### Is Mill still a hedonist?

The higher-pleasures doctrine has been attacked from both sides.

- **The consistency objection** (F. H. Bradley, *Ethical Studies*, 1876; G. E. Moore, *Principia Ethica*, 1903): if one pleasure is better than another of equal quantity, then something other than pleasure must be making it better. So Mill has abandoned hedonism.
- **The circularity objection:** who are the competent judges? If they are simply people who prefer poetry, the test is rigged. If they are anyone with experience of both, the empirical evidence is mixed: plenty of educated people choose television over Wordsworth.

Defenders reply in two ways. Roger Crisp (*Mill on Utilitarianism*, 1997) argues that "quality" can be understood as a feature of the *experience itself* (its felt character), so that a higher pleasure is simply one that feels better in a way not captured by intensity and duration. Others accept that Mill's theory is a hybrid, moving toward an *objective list* view of well-being, on which knowledge, achievement, friendship and autonomy are good for you independently of the pleasure they bring. Robert Nozick's "experience machine" (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974), which offers you a lifetime of simulated pleasant experiences, is the modern argument that hedonism is false: most people refuse to plug in, which suggests that we care about actually doing things, not just about feeling as if we do.

## The "proof"

Mill's chapter 1 admits that "Questions of ultimate ends are not amenable to direct proof." You cannot prove that health is good by deduction from something more basic. But chapter 4 offers considerations "capable of determining the intellect":

> The only proof capable of being given that an object is visible, is that people actually see it. The only proof that a sound is audible, is that people hear it: and so of the other sources of our experience. In like manner, I apprehend, the sole evidence it is possible to produce that anything is desirable, is that people do actually desire it.
>
> — Mill, *Utilitarianism*, ch. 4

He continues: each person desires his own happiness; therefore "each person's happiness is a good to that person, and the general happiness, therefore, a good to the aggregate of all persons."

Laid out:

1. The only evidence that something is desirable is that people desire it.
2. Each person desires his or her own happiness.
3. So each person's happiness is a good to that person.
4. So the general happiness is a good to the aggregate of all persons.
5. People desire nothing except happiness (other apparent ends, like virtue or money, are desired as *parts* of happiness).
6. So happiness is the sole good.

Moore pounced on step 1 in *Principia Ethica*: "visible" means *can be seen*, but "desirable" means *ought to be desired* (or worth desiring), so the analogy trades on an equivocation. This became a central exhibit for what Moore called the "naturalistic fallacy" ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)). Step 4 is accused of the fallacy of composition: from the fact that each person's happiness is good *for that person* it does not follow that the sum is good *for anyone*, or that I have reason to promote yours.

Sympathetic readers (for example, Elijah Millgram, Henry West) answer that Mill is not offering a deduction at all; he has said that ultimate ends cannot be proved. He is offering *evidence*: the best evidence of what is worth desiring is what people, reflectively and with experience, actually desire. On this reading step 4 is not composition but impartiality: if your happiness is a good and mine is a good, then, from a standpoint that favors neither of us, their sum is a greater good. That standpoint is exactly what Sidgwick will make explicit.

## Mill on rules, motives and justice

Mill anticipates the objection that a utilitarian will be paralyzed by calculation. His answer is one of his best images:

> Nobody argues that the art of navigation is not founded on astronomy, because sailors cannot wait to calculate the Nautical Almanack. Being rational creatures, they go to sea with it ready calculated; and all rational creatures go out upon the sea of life with their minds made up on the common questions of right and wrong.
>
> — Mill, *Utilitarianism*, ch. 2

The accumulated rules of common morality ("secondary principles") encode humanity's experience of what tends to promote happiness. You should usually follow them and appeal to the first principle only when they conflict. He also separates the rightness of the *act* from the worth of the *agent*:

> He who saves a fellow creature from drowning does what is morally right, whether his motive be duty, or the hope of being paid for his trouble.
>
> — Mill, *Utilitarianism*, ch. 2

Set this beside Kant's shopkeeper ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)): both agree the self-interested act can be right; they disagree about whether its *moral worth* is the central question.

Chapter 5, the longest, faces the strongest objection: that utility cannot account for justice, whose demands feel absolute and independent of consequences. Mill's answer is that justice names the class of moral rules that protect the most vital human interests, above all security, and that rights are claims society should protect:

> To have a right, then, is, I conceive, to have something which society ought to defend me in the possession of. If the objector goes on to ask why it ought, I can give him no other reason than general utility.
>
> — Mill, *Utilitarianism*, ch. 5

The peculiar "energy" of the feeling of justice comes, he says, from an animal element, "the thirst for retaliation," moralized by being extended to everyone. But justice is not absolute. In a passage worth savoring, he says that to save a life it may be a duty "to steal, or take by force, the necessary food or medicine, or to kidnap, and compel to officiate, the only qualified medical practitioner."

### Liberty

Mill's *On Liberty* (1859) proposes "one very simple principle," now called the *harm principle*:

> That the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant.
>
> — Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. 1

Mill insists that this is a utilitarian principle: "I regard utility as the ultimate appeal on all ethical questions; but it must be utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being." Whether utility can really support so firm an anti-paternalism is debated. The same chapter contains a passage modern readers should not skip: the principle does not apply to "those backward states of society in which the race itself may be considered as in its nonage," and "Despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end be their improvement." Mill worked for thirty-five years at the East India Company. Scholars such as Uday Singh Mehta (*Liberalism and Empire*, 1999) argue that this "civilizational" proviso is not an aside but a structural feature of nineteenth-century liberalism. Mill's feminism, by contrast, was ahead of almost everyone: see *The Subjection of Women* in [Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md). The political side of Mill is covered in [the Philosophy course, Chapter 16](../learn-philosophy/16-utilitarianism-and-liberalism.md).

## Sidgwick: the most careful utilitarian

Henry Sidgwick (1838–1900) was a Cambridge don of exceptional scrupulousness. In 1869 he resigned his fellowship because he could no longer sincerely affirm the Thirty-Nine Articles of the Church of England, as fellows were then required to do. (The requirement was abolished two years later, partly because of the stir he caused.) He was a founder of Newnham College for women and a president of the Society for Psychical Research, spending years investigating mediums in the hope of finding evidence of an afterlife; his reasons for caring will become clear below. C. D. Broad thought *The Methods of Ethics* (1874) the best treatise on moral theory ever written. Rawls called Sidgwick's book "the first truly academic work in moral philosophy."

The Preface to the sixth edition is a short intellectual autobiography. Sidgwick began as a disciple of Mill, then found in Kant a principle of impartiality that seemed to support utilitarianism. But one thing would not go away: the rational egoist's reply that, while the general good is the greater good from an impartial standpoint, it is reasonable for each person to prefer his own:

> The rationality of self-regard seemed to me as undeniable as the rationality of self-sacrifice. I could not give up this conviction, though neither of my masters, neither Kant nor Mill, seemed willing to admit it.
>
> — Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, Preface to the 6th ed.

He describes himself as "a disciple on the loose, in search of a master," and found one, for this point, in Joseph Butler, who had treated reasonable self-love and conscience as two authoritative principles of human nature ([Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md)).

### Three methods

A *method of ethics*, for Sidgwick, is a rational procedure for determining what one ought to do. He finds three in common use:

1. **Egoism** (egoistic hedonism): aim at your own greatest happiness.
2. **Intuitionism**: follow self-evident moral rules (truthfulness, justice, keeping promises).
3. **Utilitarianism** (universalistic hedonism): aim at the greatest happiness of all.

Book III subjects the morality of common sense to a long and devastating examination. Sidgwick lays down criteria for a genuine self-evident axiom (clear and precise terms, real self-evidence on reflection, mutual consistency, and broad agreement among experts) and shows that the rules of common morality fail them. "Keep promises" dissolves into exceptions: promises made under duress, promises whose fulfillment would harm the promisee, promises to the dead. Each exception, examined, seems to be justified by consequences. So common sense is "unconsciously utilitarian."

But he does find a few genuine axioms. They are abstract, and they turn out to support utilitarianism:

- **Justice:** it cannot be right to treat two people differently unless there is some relevant difference between them.
- **Prudence:** "Hereafter *as such* is to be regarded neither less nor more than Now." Mere position in time is no reason to discount a good.
- **Benevolence**, derived from the equal worth of each person's good:

> I obtain the self-evident principle that the good of any one individual is of no more importance, from the point of view (if I may say so) of the Universe, than the good of any other; unless, that is, there are special grounds for believing that more good is likely to be realised in the one case than in the other.
>
> — Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, III.xiii.3

The phrase "the point of view of the Universe" gave the title to de Lazari-Radek and Singer's book, and Singer's whole program in the next chapter rests on it.

### The dualism of practical reason

So intuitionism, purified, yields utilitarianism. But it does not defeat egoism. The egoist's axiom (it is reasonable to prefer my own good) seems as self-evident to Sidgwick as the axiom of benevolence. When the two conflict, reason speaks with two voices. The Concluding Chapter faces this squarely:

> For the negation of the connexion must force us to admit an ultimate and fundamental contradiction in our apparent intuitions of what is Reasonable in conduct; and from this admission it would seem to follow that the apparently intuitive operation of the Practical Reason, manifested in these contradictory judgments, is after all illusory.
>
> — Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, Concluding Chapter, §5

Only a moral order in which virtue and self-interest coincide (a God who rewards virtue, an afterlife) would reconcile them. That is one reason Sidgwick spent so many evenings in darkened rooms with mediums. In the first edition he had put the stakes dramatically: without such a reconciliation, the "Cosmos" of duty would be reduced to a "Chaos." He found no evidence, and he did not pretend otherwise. Derek Parfit spent much of his career trying to dissolve this dualism; de Lazari-Radek and Singer argue that evolutionary debunking undermines the egoist axiom but not the impartial one ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)).

### Total versus average, and esoteric morality

Two further Sidgwickian passages shaped later debates.

**Population.** Sidgwick was the first to ask clearly whether utilitarians should maximize *total* or *average* happiness. His answer was total: population should increase to the point "at which the product formed by multiplying the number of persons living into the amount of average happiness reaches its maximum" (IV.i.2). Parfit would later show where this leads: to the "Repugnant Conclusion" ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)).

**Esoteric morality.** Because the utilitarian cares about the effects of *teaching* and *publicizing* principles, not just acting on them, Sidgwick reaches a startling conclusion:

> Thus, on Utilitarian principles, it may be right to do and privately recommend, under certain circumstances, what it would not be right to advocate openly; it may be right to teach openly to one set of persons what it would be wrong to teach to others; it may be conceivably right to do, if it can be done with comparative secrecy, what it would be wrong to do in the face of the world.
>
> — Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, IV.v.3

And, even more startling: "the opinion that secrecy may render an action right which would not otherwise be so should itself be kept comparatively secret." Bernard Williams later named this "Government House utilitarianism," after the colonial administrators who governed natives by rules they did not themselves believe. Rawls made *publicity* a formal constraint on any acceptable conception of justice partly to exclude it ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)). Whether a moral theory that cannot be openly avowed is thereby refuted remains a live question.

## Act and rule

Classical utilitarians did not sharply distinguish two versions that twentieth-century philosophers separated:

- **Act utilitarianism**: an act is right if and only if it produces at least as much utility as any alternative act available to the agent.
- **Rule utilitarianism**: an act is right if and only if it is permitted by the set of rules whose general acceptance (or compliance) would produce the most utility.

J. O. Urmson ("The Interpretation of the Moral Philosophy of J. S. Mill," 1953) argued that Mill was a rule utilitarian, citing the Nautical Almanack and the chapter on justice. Most scholars now think Mill is not consistently either, and that the distinction is anachronistic for him.

Rule utilitarianism seems to handle the classic counterexamples better: a rule permitting surgeons to harvest unwilling patients' organs would, if generally accepted, produce terror and distrust, so the rule is rejected even if a particular secret harvest would maximize utility. But it faces a dilemma pressed by David Lyons (*Forms and Limits of Utilitarianism*, 1965) and J. J. C. Smart:

1. If the optimal rules include all the exceptions needed to maximize utility ("keep promises, except when breaking them produces more utility"), rule utilitarianism **collapses** into act utilitarianism.
2. If the rules do not include those exceptions, then following them when breaking them would produce more good is irrational **rule worship** for anyone whose fundamental concern is utility.

Brad Hooker's *Ideal Code, Real World* (2000) is the most influential modern defense. Hooker evaluates codes by the consequences of their *acceptance* by the overwhelming majority, including the costs of internalizing them (complicated codes are expensive to teach), and he defends rule consequentialism not by deriving it from a commitment to maximizing utility but by its fit with our considered judgments in reflective equilibrium. That sidesteps the rule-worship charge, at the cost of giving up the classical utilitarians' claim to have found the single foundation of morality.

A third view, **two-level utilitarianism** (R. M. Hare, *Moral Thinking*, 1981), formalizes Mill's sailors and Sidgwick's esotericism. In everyday life we should think at the *intuitive* level, following firm rules and dispositions; only in calm reflection, or in novel cases, should we think at the *critical* level and calculate. The criterion of right action remains act-utilitarian; the recommended decision procedure is mostly rule-following. Critics reply that this asks you to live by rules you believe are, at bottom, mere heuristics, a kind of permanent self-manipulation.

## The classic objections

These are the objections every utilitarian must answer. Most return in the next chapter in contemporary form.

1. **Justice and the scapegoat.** H. J. McCloskey (1957) imagined a sheriff who can prevent a lethal riot only by framing and executing an innocent man. Philippa Foot's transplant case (1967) and Judith Jarvis Thomson's trolley variants ([Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md)) are cousins. If utility is all that matters, the innocent must die.
2. **The separateness of persons.** Rawls's charge: utilitarianism extends to society the principle of rational choice for one person, treating my pain today as compensated by my pleasure tomorrow, and so treats one person's loss as compensated by another's gain. But you do not experience my pleasure ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)).
3. **Rights.** Rights seem to function as side constraints that cannot be overridden by small gains to many. Mill's account of rights as protected interests struggles with the intuition that some things may not be done to a person whatever the total benefit.
4. **Demandingness.** If you must always maximize, almost every leisure activity is wrong. See Singer in [Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md).
5. **Integrity and special obligations.** Utilitarianism seems to leave no room for the projects and relationships that give a life its shape (Williams, [Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)).
6. **Hedonism.** The experience machine suggests that well-being is not just pleasure.
7. **Calculation and uncertainty.** We cannot foresee consequences; perhaps we should judge acts by *expected* rather than *actual* utility (most modern consequentialists do).

The standard replies are three. **Bite the bullet**: Smart argued that if framing the innocent really maximized utility, it would be right, and our contrary intuition is a useful but fallible rule of thumb. **Go indirect**: two-level and rule views explain why good utilitarians will almost never frame anyone. **Revise the value theory**: count rights, fairness or equality as goods to be promoted (but then, as Nozick objected, you have a "utilitarianism of rights" that would still violate one person's rights to minimize violations overall).

## Living it

The utilitarian tradition offers three practices, one from each author, and each tests something different in you.

**Bentham's ledger.** Pick a decision you face this week that affects more than one person (how to split a shared cost, whether to take on a project, where to spend a free evening). List everyone affected, including people you are tempted to forget. For each, estimate intensity, duration, certainty and extent. You will not get numbers. What you will get is the discovery of who was missing from your first draft. Bentham's real gift is less arithmetic than inclusion: "everybody to count for one."

**Mill's indirection.** Reread the passage from the *Autobiography*: "Ask yourself whether you are happy, and you cease to be so." Choose one external end (a skill, a cause, another person's flourishing) and give it attention this week without checking how it makes you feel. At the end of the week, notice whether your happiness changed. Then ask whether your moral thinking has the same structure: the criterion of right action may be the general good, but the best way to serve it may be to think about something else.

**Sidgwick's publicity test.** For one choice you would rather nobody knew about, ask: would I be willing to defend the *principle* behind it openly? Sidgwick shows that a utilitarian can coherently answer no. Notice whether you can.

## Summary

- Classical utilitarianism combines consequentialism, welfarism, hedonism, aggregation, impartiality and maximization; each can be abandoned separately.
- Bentham grounded morality and law in pleasure and pain, attacked natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts," proposed a felicific calculus (intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent), and made sentience the criterion of moral standing ("Can they suffer?").
- "Push-pin is as good as poetry" is a paraphrase, not Bentham's wording.
- Mill, after his crisis, distinguished higher and lower pleasures by the preference of competent judges; critics say this abandons hedonism. His "proof" from desire is accused of equivocation and composition; sympathetic readers treat it as evidence, not deduction.
- Mill separates the criterion of rightness from the decision procedure (secondary rules, the Nautical Almanack), and the rightness of acts from the worth of agents. He grounds justice and rights in utility and liberty in "utility in the largest sense," with a colonial proviso that critics now scrutinize.
- Sidgwick's *Methods* tests common-sense morality, finds it unconsciously utilitarian, identifies axioms of justice, prudence and benevolence ("the point of view of the Universe"), but cannot defeat egoism: the dualism of practical reason.
- Sidgwick also first posed total versus average utility (he chose total) and defended esoteric morality ("Government House utilitarianism," as Williams called it).
- Act versus rule utilitarianism; the collapse and rule-worship dilemma; Hooker's rule consequentialism; Hare's two-level view.
- Classic objections: the scapegoat, separateness of persons, rights, demandingness, integrity, hedonism, uncertainty.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether the satisfied pig has a point

**Socrates:** Mill says it is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. I am flattered. But tell me, which would you rather be?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Socrates dissatisfied, obviously. Being a contented fool sounds like a loss, even if it feels fine from the inside.

**Socrates:** A loss of what? Mill is a hedonist. He says the only thing good in itself is pleasure. If the fool has more pleasure, what has he lost?

**You:** He's lost the higher pleasures, the ones that come from thinking and understanding.

**Socrates:** But if he never misses them, how are they a loss *to him*?

**You:** Mill says the competent judges, the ones who've had both, prefer the higher ones. The fool and the pig "only know their own side of the question."

**Socrates:** Suppose I have tasted both wine and philosophy and prefer philosophy. Is that because philosophy is more pleasant, or because I think it more worthy?

**You:** Mill says the preference is "irrespective of any feeling of moral obligation." So it should be about pleasantness.

**Socrates:** Then the higher pleasure is just a more intense or lasting one, and Bentham's quantities would have sufficed. Or it is better in some way that is not pleasantness, and then pleasure is not the only good.

**Bradley (stepping in):** Exactly my objection in 1876. Mill must choose: either quality reduces to quantity, or he has abandoned hedonism.

**You:** Maybe "quality" is a feature of how the pleasure feels, a different texture of experience, not just more of it. Like the difference between a deep conversation and a sugar rush.

**Socrates:** Then you are inventing a new dimension for Bentham's calculus. Could the fool's pleasures have that texture too?

**You:** Possibly. Some simple pleasures, like a meal with friends, might be "higher" in that sense.

**Socrates:** And would you plug yourself into Nozick's machine if it gave you the experience of deep conversations, without the conversations?

**You:** No. I want the conversation to be real, with a real friend.

**Socrates:** Then you want something besides pleasant experience: the reality of the thing experienced. What does that tell you about Mill's theory?

**You:** That his examples are right but his theory is too narrow. What I value includes knowledge and real relationships, not only how they feel. That's an objective-list view, not hedonism.

**Socrates:** Then you agree with Mill's verdict and disagree with his explanation. That is a respectable place to stand.

### Dialogue 2 — On the sheriff and the scapegoat

**Bentham:** A town is about to riot over a murder. Five innocent people will die in the violence. The sheriff can stop it by framing and hanging one vagrant, whom no one will miss. The secret will never come out. What should he do?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Bentham in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** He shouldn't frame an innocent man. That's monstrous, whatever the numbers.

**Bentham:** You count five deaths as less monstrous than one? Pain is pain, whoever suffers it. Each of the five counts for one.

**You:** But the sheriff would be *killing* the vagrant. The rioters would be killing the five.

**Bentham:** A distinction I find in your grammar, not in the suffering. The dead are equally dead.

**Mill:** Allow me, Jeremy. Our friend's instinct contains utilitarian wisdom. Justice, as I argued, names the rules protecting security, "the most vital of all interests." A society in which sheriffs hang innocents when convenient is one in which no one is secure.

**You:** But Bentham stipulated the secret never comes out.

**Mill:** Stipulations are cheap. Secrets come out. And a sheriff who has done this once will find the next case easier. The rules of justice are our Nautical Almanack; a sailor who recalculates in the storm drowns.

**Sidgwick:** I fear John is avoiding the question. Grant the case exactly as stated. Utilitarianism says hang him. My own conclusion was that it might be right to do in secret what it would be wrong to advocate openly. I did not enjoy writing that.

**You:** Then that's a reason to reject utilitarianism. If a theory needs to be kept secret, something's wrong with it.

**Sidgwick:** Is it? Physicists do not hide their theories, but they also do not expect the public to calculate orbits. Is publicity a requirement of truth, or only of a certain kind of politics?

**You:** For morality, I think it's a requirement of truth. A moral rule is supposed to be something we can all recognize and hold each other to. If it only works when people don't know it, it isn't functioning as morality.

**Sidgwick:** Rawls agrees with you; he made publicity a condition on principles of justice. But note what you have conceded: your objection is not that the hanging produces a worse outcome, but that morality has a *form*, a public, reciprocal form, that utility alone does not capture.

**You:** Yes. I'm saying the separateness of persons matters. The vagrant isn't a smaller amount of the same stuff the five are made of.

**Bentham:** Nonsense upon stilts, I should say. But I see I will not persuade you.

**Sidgwick:** Then we have located the disagreement precisely, which is more than most debates achieve.

</details>
