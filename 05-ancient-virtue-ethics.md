# Chapter 5 — Ancient Virtue Ethics

Most modern moral theories start with one question: *what should I do?* Should I keep this promise? May I lie to the murderer at the door? Should I pull the lever? The ancient Greeks started further back, with *how should I live?*, and close behind it, *what kind of person should I become?* Their answer was that a good human life, *eudaimonia*, consists in exercising the virtues: settled excellences of character and intellect such as courage, justice, self-control, and practical wisdom. This chapter follows that idea from Socrates, who argued in the streets of Athens that virtue is a kind of knowledge, through Plato's picture of justice as the health of the soul, to its most careful statement in Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, with its function argument, the doctrine of the mean, practical wisdom, and friendship. The chapter ends with three hard objections. Virtue ethics may not tell us what to do. Psychology suggests there may be no such thing as character. And the ancient picture of the good life was built for a privileged few. The modern revival of virtue ethics, which answers these objections, is the subject of [Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md). Previous: [Chapter 4 — Divine Command and Natural Law](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md). Next: [Chapter 6 — Hellenistic Ethics](06-hellenistic-ethics.md).

## A different starting point

Ask a modern moral philosopher whether to take a hedge-fund job that would let you donate a fortune or a teaching job you would love, and they will assess an *action*: count its consequences, or test its maxim. Ask Aristotle and he will ask what it would mean for *your life* to go well, and whether the hedge-fund version or the classroom version of you is closer to it. That is not a selfish question. A good human life, on his account, includes justice, generosity, and friendship, and you can no more live it while cheating your neighbors than be a good doctor while poisoning patients.

Julia Annas, whose *The Morality of Happiness* is the best modern map of this territory, calls this the "entry point" of ancient ethics: reflection begins when a person stands back from the daily round and asks whether their life as a whole is going as it should. Every major ancient school accepted three things about the answer:

1. **There is a final end (*telos*).** Everything we do aims at something, and there is one thing at which everything ultimately aims.
2. **That end is *eudaimonia*.** The word is usually translated "happiness," but it means something closer to "flourishing" or "living well." It is not a mood. It is an assessment of a whole life, the way "healthy" assesses a body.
3. **Virtue is central to it.** The schools disagreed about how central. The Stoics said virtue is sufficient for happiness. Aristotle said virtue is the main ingredient but needs some external goods. The Epicureans said virtue is the indispensable means to a pleasant life. All of them agreed that no vicious person flourishes.

This framework is called **eudaimonism**. Its key term, *aretē*, means "excellence" before it means "virtue." A knife has an *aretē* (sharpness), and so does a racehorse (speed). To ask what human virtue is, the Greeks thought, is to ask what a human being is for.

## Socrates: virtue is knowledge

Socrates wrote nothing. On the standard (though disputed) view, defended by Gregory Vlastos, Plato's early dialogues, such as the *Apology*, *Crito*, *Euthyphro*, *Protagoras*, and *Gorgias*, give a fairly reliable picture of his ethics, while the later ones put Plato's own ideas in his mouth. The historical Socrates held a cluster of startling claims later called the **Socratic paradoxes**:

- Virtue is a kind of knowledge.
- No one does wrong willingly.
- The virtues are in some sense one.
- It is worse to do injustice than to suffer it.
- A good person cannot be harmed.

The *Meno* opens without any small talk, with the question that hangs over the whole tradition:

> MENO: Can you tell me, Socrates, whether virtue is acquired by teaching or by practice; or if neither by teaching nor by practice, then whether it comes to man by nature, or in what other way?
>
> — Plato, *Meno* 70a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Socrates answers, typically, that he cannot say how virtue is acquired, since he does not know what it is. But he does extract from Meno an argument that nobody wants what is bad:

> SOCRATES: But if there is no one who desires to be miserable, there is no one, Meno, who desires evil; for what is misery but the desire and possession of evil?
>
> MENO: That appears to be the truth, Socrates, and I admit that nobody desires evil.
>
> — Plato, *Meno* 78a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Every desire aims at something the desirer takes to be good. The thief wants money because money seems good. If he is wrong, his mistake is about what is good, and a mistake is a failure of knowledge. Hence the first two paradoxes: virtue is knowledge of what is truly good, and wrongdoing is ignorance.

### "Being overcome by pleasure"

The obvious objection is that people often know what they should do and fail to do it. You know you should go to bed instead of watching one more episode, and you watch it anyway. The Greeks called this *akrasia*, "lack of mastery," usually translated "weakness of will." In the *Protagoras*, Socrates states the common view before attacking it:

> Now the rest of the world are of opinion that knowledge is a principle not of strength, or of rule, or of command: their notion is that a man may have knowledge, and yet that the knowledge which is in him may be overmastered by anger, or pleasure, or pain, or love, or perhaps by fear,--just as if knowledge were a slave, and might be dragged about anyhow. Now is that your view? or do you think that knowledge is a noble and commanding thing, which cannot be overcome, and will not allow a man, if he only knows the difference of good and evil, to do anything which is contrary to knowledge, but that wisdom will have strength to help him?
>
> — Plato, *Protagoras* 352b–c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

His argument (352d–358d) runs roughly like this. Assume, as most people do, that good is ultimately pleasure and bad is ultimately pain. Then "he knew X was bad but did it because he was overcome by pleasure" becomes "he chose the greater pain because he was overcome by the lesser pleasure," which is absurd. What really happened, Socrates suggests, is a mistake of *measurement*: near pleasures look bigger than distant ones, as near objects do. The cure is a science of weighing:

> Is not the latter that deceiving art which makes us wander up and down and take the things at one time of which we repent at another, both in our actions and in our choice of things great and small? But the art of measurement would do away with the effect of appearances, and, showing the truth, would fain teach the soul at last to find rest in the truth, and would thus save our life.
>
> — Plato, *Protagoras* 356d–e (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

And so the paradox follows:

> Then, I said, no man voluntarily pursues evil, or that which he thinks to be evil. To prefer evil to good is not in human nature; and when a man is compelled to choose one of two evils, no one will choose the greater when he may have the less.
>
> — Plato, *Protagoras* 358c–d (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

The argument has two weak points. It assumes hedonism, which Socrates may not have accepted. And it treats the akratic person as *fooled*, while the person watching one more episode at 1 a.m. seems to see the costs clearly and do it anyway. Still, Socrates anticipated what behavioral economics calls *hyperbolic discounting*: someone who prefers $110 in 31 days to $100 in 30 days may still prefer $100 today to $110 tomorrow. Much of what we call weakness is a distortion in how goods *appear* at the moment of choice, and wisdom includes correcting for it. Aristotle's subtler account comes below.

### The unity of the virtues

If virtue is knowledge of good and bad, then courage (knowing what is truly to be feared) and self-control (knowing which pleasures are worth having) are applications of one knowledge. So you cannot have one virtue fully without them all. A "brave" bank robber is not really brave, since he lacks the knowledge of what is worth risking his life for. Aristotle keeps a version of this idea.

### Better to suffer wrong than to do it

The *Gorgias* contains Socrates' most defiant claim. Polus, a young teacher of rhetoric, thinks it obvious that a tyrant who can kill and confiscate at will is enviable. Socrates disagrees:

> POLUS: Then would you rather suffer than do injustice?
>
> SOCRATES: I should not like either, but if I must choose between them, I would rather suffer than do.
>
> — Plato, *Gorgias* 469c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Why? Because the most important thing about you is your soul, the seat of your character and judgment, and injustice damages the soul of the one who commits it, while being wronged damages only body or property. After answering Callicles' claim ([Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md)) that nature licenses the strong to take more, Socrates says the conclusion is held fast "in words which are like bonds of iron and adamant" (509a). Callicles is not persuaded, and many readers side with him. But notice the shape of the argument. Socrates does not say you have a duty to accept injustice whatever it costs you. He says committing injustice *is* the greater cost to you. Morality and self-interest, rightly understood, coincide. That claim, which [Chapter 1](01-what-is-ethics.md) traced through the Ring of Gyges, is the bet on which the whole eudaimonist tradition rests.

### The leaky jars

The *Gorgias* also contains one of the best images in philosophy. Callicles praises the life of unlimited appetite: the happy man lets his desires grow as large as possible and has the means to satisfy them. Socrates replies with a parable:

> There are two men, both of whom have a number of casks; the one man has his casks sound and full, one of wine, another of honey, and a third of milk, besides others filled with other liquids, and the streams which fill them are few and scanty, and he can only obtain them with a great deal of toil and difficulty; but when his casks are once filled he has no need to feed them any more, and has no further trouble with them or care about them. The other, in like manner, can procure streams, though not without difficulty; but his vessels are leaky and unsound, and night and day he is compelled to be filling them, and if he pauses for a moment, he is in an agony of pain.
>
> — Plato, *Gorgias* 493e–494a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Callicles' reply is honest: the man with full casks has "the life of a stone," and the pleasure is in the pouring. The disagreement has not gone away. Psychologists' "hedonic treadmill," our quick adaptation to new pleasures and possessions, is the leaky jar renamed.

## Plato: justice as the health of the soul

In the *Republic* Plato develops the Socratic view into a theory of the soul. In Book IV he argues that the soul has three parts, distinguished by the fact that they can conflict with one another:

- **Reason** (*to logistikon*), which calculates, seeks truth, and cares for the whole person.
- **Spirit** (*to thumoeides*), the seat of anger, pride, and the sense of honor. It is what makes you indignant when you are treated unfairly and ashamed when you have behaved badly.
- **Appetite** (*to epithumētikon*), the many desires for food, drink, sex, and money.

A thirsty man may refuse to drink because he knows the water is poisoned. One thing cannot both want and refuse the same object at the same time in the same respect, so there must be two things in him. Spirit shows itself in Leontius, who wanted to look at corpses by the executioner, was disgusted with himself, and finally ran up to them saying to his eyes, "Look, ye wretches, take your fill of the fair sight" (440a). Anger at one's own appetite is spirit taking reason's side.

This lets Plato say what Socrates could not: a person can know the good and act against it, when appetite overpowers reason and spirit fails to back it. It also yields new definitions of the virtues. Wisdom is reason ruling with knowledge of the whole. Courage is spirit holding fast to reason's judgment about what is to be feared. Self-control is the agreement of all three parts that reason should rule. And justice is each part doing its own work:

> But in reality justice was such as we were describing, being concerned however, not with the outward man, but with the inward, which is the true self and concernment of man: for the just man does not permit the several elements within him to interfere with one another, or any of them to do the work of others,—he sets in order his own inner life, and is his own master and his own law, and at peace with himself; and when he has bound together the three principles within him, which may be compared to the higher, lower, and middle notes of the scale, and the intermediate intervals—when he has bound all these together, and is no longer many, but has become one entirely temperate and perfectly adjusted nature, then he proceeds to act, if he has to act...
>
> — Plato, *Republic* IV, 443c–e (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

From here it is a short step to the conclusion quoted in [Chapter 1](01-what-is-ethics.md): justice is the health of the soul and injustice its disease, and asking whether injustice pays is as silly as asking whether it pays to be sick.

### The Sachs objection

In "A Fallacy in Plato's *Republic*" (1963), David Sachs pointed out that Glaucon asked Socrates to show that justice *in the ordinary sense* (not stealing, not breaking promises) is worth having, and Socrates instead showed that justice in *his* sense, inner harmony, is worth having. He never proves that the two coincide. Could a perfectly ordered soul embezzle? Plato sketches a reply (442e–443b): theft serves appetite and betrayal serves ambition, so a soul ruled by reason has no motive for them. Whether that is true is partly an empirical question about psychology, which the situationist critique below revives.

## Aristotle: the chief good

Aristotle studied in Plato's Academy for twenty years and then built something different. Where Plato reached for a transcendent Form of the Good, Aristotle treated ethics as a practical inquiry into the good achievable in action. The *Nicomachean Ethics*, probably edited lecture notes, opens:

> Every art, and every science reduced to a teachable form, and in like manner every action and moral choice, aims, it is thought, at some good: for which reason a common and by no means a bad description of the Chief Good is, “that which all things aim at.”
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.1, 1094a1–3 (tr. D. P. Chase)

We want a bridle for riding, riding for cavalry, and cavalry for victory. If every good were wanted for something further, desire would be empty, so there must be something wanted for its own sake. Everyone agrees on its name:

> So far as name goes, there is a pretty general agreement: for HAPPINESS both the multitude and the refined few call it, and “living well” and “doing well” they conceive to be the same with “being happy;” but about the Nature of this Happiness, men dispute, and the multitude do not in their account of it agree with the wise.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.4, 1095a17–22 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Aristotle dismisses three candidate lives. Pleasure is the life of grazing animals. Honor depends on others' opinions, and we seek it mainly as assurance that we are good, so goodness matters more. Money is a means. The chief good must be **final**, chosen for its own sake and never for anything else, and **self-sufficient**, making life worth choosing by itself. Happiness passes both tests.

### The function argument

What, then, is happiness? Aristotle's answer comes through the most famous and most criticized argument in ancient ethics. Everything that has a function (*ergon*) has its good in performing that function well. A flute-player's good lies in playing the flute well, and a sculptor's in sculpting well. So if a human being has a function, the human good lies in performing it well. Does a human being have one?

> Are we then to suppose, that while carpenter and cobbler have certain works and courses of action, Man as Man has none, but is left by Nature without a work? or would not one rather hold, that as eye, hand, and foot, and generally each of his members, has manifestly some special work; so too the whole Man, as distinct from all these, has some work of his own?
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.7, 1097b28–33 (tr. D. P. Chase)

The function must be peculiar to human beings. Living is shared with plants and perception with animals, so what remains is a life of reason in action, which a good human being does well. Hence:

> if all this is so, then the Good of Man comes to be “a working of the Soul in the way of Excellence,” or, if Excellence admits of degrees, in the way of the best and most perfect Excellence.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.7, 1098a16–18 (tr. D. P. Chase)

He adds at once that this activity must fill a whole life:

> for as it is not one swallow or one fine day that makes a spring, so it is not one day or a short time that makes a man blessed and happy.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.7, 1098a18–20 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Three objections are standard. First, "function" fits artifacts (we give the knife its job) and organs (biology fixes the eye's), but who assigned a whole human being a job? Second, why should the human good be what is *distinctive* of us? Humans are also the only animals that start wars and play the stock market. Third, being a good specimen of a human is not obviously the same as having a life good *for* you. A fine specimen of a pig need not be a happy pig.

Defenders reply that Aristotle is articulating, not deriving from biology, what we already think a good human life is, reading back from considered judgments (*endoxa*) as described in [Chapter 1](01-what-is-ethics.md). The "neo-Aristotelian naturalism" of Philippa Foot and Rosalind Hursthouse ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)) rebuilds the argument without a cosmic job description: calling a wolf that will not hunt with the pack defective is an ordinary judgment about living things, and moral judgment has the same form.

### Fortune and the good life

Aristotle, unlike Socrates, is unwilling to say that a good person on the rack is happy. Virtue is the main thing, but it is not enough:

> Still it is quite plain that it does require the addition of external goods, as we have said: because without appliances it is impossible, or at all events not easy, to do noble actions: for friends, money, and political influence are in a manner instruments whereby many things are done: some things there are again a deficiency in which mars blessedness; good birth, for instance, or fine offspring, or even personal beauty: for he is not at all capable of Happiness who is very ugly, or is ill-born, or solitary and childless...
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.8, 1099a31–b4 (tr. D. P. Chase)

The remark about ugliness makes modern readers wince, and it should. But the larger point is serious and humane. Happiness, as Aristotle understands it, is something that can be damaged by what happens to you. It is not a fortress the wise can retreat into. His illustration is the king of Troy, who lived well for decades and then saw his sons killed and his city burned:

> For to constitute Happiness, there must be, as we have said, complete virtue and a complete life: for many changes and chances of all kinds arise during a life, and he who is most prosperous may become involved in great misfortunes in his old age, as in the heroic poems the tale is told of Priam: but the man who has experienced such fortune and died in wretchedness, no man calls happy.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.9, 1100a4–9 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Should we then, as Solon told Croesus in Herodotus' story, call no one happy until they are dead? Aristotle's answer (I.10–11) is that a good person will not be made *miserable* by misfortune, since they will bear it nobly, as a good general makes the best use of the army he has. But they can be kept from being *blessed*. Martha Nussbaum's *The Fragility of Goodness* puts this exposure to luck at the center: what we value most, such as friends, children, and political community, makes us vulnerable, and a life without that vulnerability would be poorer, not safer. The Hellenistic schools of [Chapter 6](06-hellenistic-ethics.md) try to buy invulnerability at that price.

## Virtue of character: habit and the mean

**Intellectual virtues**, such as wisdom and practical wisdom, are mostly acquired by teaching. **Virtues of character** (*ēthikai aretai*), such as courage, self-control, generosity, and justice, are acquired by habit (*ethos*). That answers Meno's question, at least for character:

> The Virtues then come to be in us neither by nature, nor in despite of nature, but we are furnished by nature with a capacity for receiving them ... and are perfected in them through custom.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* II.1, 1103a23–26 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Practice produces character as it produces any skill:

> men come to be builders, for instance, by building; harp-players, by playing on the harp: exactly so, by doing just actions we come to be just; by doing the actions of self-mastery we come to be perfected in self-mastery; and by doing brave actions brave.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* II.1, 1103a33–b2 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Is there a circle? Must we already be just to do just acts? Aristotle's answer (II.4) is that the beginner does acts that *look* just, perhaps because a parent says so. The virtuous person acts knowingly, chooses the act for its own sake, and acts from a settled character. Practice turns the first kind of act into the second, as scales become fluent playing. Hence the importance of upbringing:

> So then, whether we are accustomed this way or that straight from childhood, makes not a small but an important difference, or rather I would say it makes all the difference.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* II.1, 1103b23–25 (tr. D. P. Chase)

A test of virtue is **pleasure**. The person who gives up an extra drink gladly is self-controlled. The person who does so with gritted teeth is only *continent*, a lesser state. The virtuous person's feelings are trained along with their actions, so that doing right feels natural. Contrast Kant ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)), for whom helping from duty without inclination shows a purer moral worth.

### The doctrine of the mean

Every virtue of character concerns some feeling or action that can be had too much or too little, and lies in a mean between them. Not an arithmetical midpoint, but the mean "relative to us": if ten pounds of food is too much and two too little, the trainer will not prescribe six to everyone, since six is too little for the champion wrestler Milo and too much for a beginner (1106a36–b4). The official definition:

> Virtue then is “a state apt to exercise deliberate choice, being in the relative mean, determined by reason, and as the man of practical wisdom would determine.”
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* II.6, 1106b36–1107a2 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Virtue is a *state* (*hexis*), not a passing feeling. It shows in *choice*. Its mean is *relative* to person and situation. And it is fixed by the judgment of the practically wise person, the *phronimos*. Aristotle's table (II.7) runs roughly:

| Sphere | Excess | Mean (virtue) | Deficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fear and confidence | rashness | courage | cowardice |
| Bodily pleasures | self-indulgence | self-control (temperance) | "insensibility" (no name in Greek) |
| Giving and taking money | prodigality | generosity (liberality) | stinginess |
| Large-scale spending | vulgarity | magnificence | meanness |
| Honor, great scale | vanity | greatness of soul | smallness of soul |
| Anger | irascibility | good temper | "inirascibility" (spinelessness) |
| Self-presentation | boastfulness | truthfulness | false modesty |
| Humor | buffoonery | wit | boorishness |
| Pleasing others | obsequiousness / flattery | friendliness | quarrelsomeness |

Three misunderstandings are common.

**The mean is not moderation.** Sometimes the right amount of anger is a great deal. Someone who watches a child being beaten and feels mild irritation has missed the mean by deficiency. The mean is about *fit*, not quantity:

> And so it is hard to be good: for surely hard it is in each instance to find the mean, just as to find the mean point or centre of a circle is not what any man can do, but only he who knows how: just so to be angry, to give money, and be expensive, is what any man can do, and easy: but to do these to the right person, in due proportion, at the right time, with a right object, and in the right manner, this is not as before what any man can do, nor is it easy; and for this cause goodness is rare, and praiseworthy, and noble.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* II.9, 1109a24–30 (tr. D. P. Chase)

**Not everything has a mean.** Some actions and feelings are wrong in themselves, and there is no right amount of them:

> But it must not be supposed that every action or every feeling is capable of subsisting in this mean state, because some there are which are so named as immediately to convey the notion of badness, as malevolence, shamelessness, envy; or, to instance in actions, adultery, theft, homicide; for all these and suchlike are blamed because they are in themselves bad, not the having too much or too little of them.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* II.6, 1107a8–13 (tr. D. P. Chase)

So Aristotle's ethics, like ordinary morality, contains absolute prohibitions, though the doctrine of the mean does not tell us how to identify them.

**The mean is not a decision procedure.** "Find the mean" tells you no more than "do the right amount." That objection is fair against the doctrine read as a method, but Aristotle never offers it as one. The real work is done by practical wisdom.

## Practical wisdom

*Phronēsis* is the intellectual virtue that tells a person of good character what to do here and now. Unlike scientific knowledge, it concerns what can be otherwise. Unlike craft (*technē*), it aims not at a product but at acting well. And it is above all knowledge of particulars:

> Nor again does Practical Wisdom consist in a knowledge of general principles only, but it is necessary that one should know also the particular details, because it is apt to act, and action is concerned with details: for which reason sometimes men who have not much knowledge are more practical than others who have; among others, they who derive all they know from actual experience: suppose a man to know, for instance, that light meats are easy of digestion and wholesome, but not what kinds of meat are light, he will not produce a healthy state; that man will have a much better chance of doing so, who knows that the flesh of birds is light and wholesome.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* VI.7, 1141b14–21 (tr. D. P. Chase)

So the young can be brilliant mathematicians but not practically wise (VI.8), because practical wisdom needs experience. It includes good deliberation and something like perception, the capacity to see what matters. Two people sit in the same meeting. One notices that a junior colleague has been talked over three times and is near tears. The other notices nothing. The difference is not a principle one knows and the other lacks. As [Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md) showed, Aristotle thought the decision in particular cases "rests with" perception.

### Character and wisdom need each other

Aristotle ties the two kinds of virtue together in a tight knot:

> From what has been said then it is clear that one cannot be, strictly speaking, good without Practical Wisdom nor Practically-Wise without moral goodness.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* VI.13, 1144b30–32 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Without good character, intelligence is mere *cleverness* (*deinotēs*), which the con artist has in abundance. Without practical wisdom, good impulses are blind: a child with "natural" generosity will give away the rent money. Since practical wisdom is one thing, whoever has it has all the virtues of character, a softened version of the Socratic unity of the virtues.

### Aristotle on weakness of will

Aristotle opens Book VII by noting that Socrates' denial of akrasia conflicts with plain observation. His own solution (VII.3) distinguishes ways of knowing. A drunk can recite Empedocles without understanding a word. The akratic person knows the general principle ("sweets are bad for me") but, under appetite's influence, does not fully activate the particular premise ("this is a sweet, and it is for me, now"). So Socrates was partly right: in the moment, the knowledge is not fully present. Aristotle also distinguishes *impetuous* akrasia, which acts before deliberating, from *weak* akrasia, which deliberates correctly and fails to stick to the conclusion. The cure is better habits. Both the continent person, who resists by effort, and the akratic, who gives in, fall short of the temperate person, who is not seriously tempted.

## Friendship

Two of the ten books of the *Nicomachean Ethics* are about friendship (*philia*), more than any single virtue gets. That proportion is itself a claim:

> because, in the first place, it is either itself a virtue or connected with virtue; and next it is a thing most necessary for life, since no one would choose to live without friends though he should have all the other good things in the world...
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* VIII.1, 1155a3–6 (tr. D. P. Chase)

*Philia* covers parents and children, spouses, business partners, and fellow citizens. Aristotle sorts it into three kinds by what each party loves the other for: **utility** (the colleague you swap shifts with), **pleasure** (the friend you go dancing with), and **character** (the friend you love for who they are). The first two are real but fragile:

> So then they whose motive to Friendship is utility love their friends for what is good to themselves; they whose motive is pleasure do so for what is pleasurable to themselves; that is to say, not in so far as the friend beloved _is_ but in so far as he is useful or pleasurable.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* VIII.3, 1156a14–17 (tr. D. P. Chase)

The third kind is complete:

> That then is perfect Friendship which subsists between those who are good and whose similarity consists in their goodness: for these men wish one another’s good in similar ways; in so far as they are good (and good they are in themselves); and those are specially friends who wish good to their friends for their sakes...
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* VIII.3, 1156b7–10 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Such friendships are rare, because good people are rare and because, as the proverb says, you must eat a good deal of salt together before you know someone. They are also pleasant and useful. But if eudaimonia is *my* flourishing, is my friend valued only as part of it, as the egoist of [Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md) would say? Aristotle's answer is that the friend is "a second self":

> Some say that they who are blessed and independent have no need of Friends, for they already have all that is good, and so, as being independent, want nothing further: whereas the notion of a friend’s office is to be as it were a second Self and procure for a man what he cannot get by himself...
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* IX.9, 1169b3–7 (tr. D. P. Chase)

So even the self-sufficient person needs friends, not as tools but as part of a complete life. Whether this fully escapes the egoism worry is debated, but it captures something true: caring about a friend for their own sake *is* part of your flourishing, and whoever calculates what each friendship does for them has already lost the good being calculated. Well-being research consistently finds close relationships among the strongest predictors of life satisfaction.

## Contemplation and the two lives

Book X contains a surprise. After nine books on practical virtue, Aristotle argues that the *highest* happiness is contemplation (*theōria*), understanding the eternal truths of mathematics, nature, and the divine. It is the activity of our highest faculty, the most continuous, pleasant, and self-sufficient, loved for its own sake alone, the life of the gods:

> Yet must we not give ear to those who bid one as man to mind only man’s affairs, or as mortal only mortal things; but, so far as we can, make ourselves like immortals and do all with a view to living in accordance with the highest Principle in us, for small as it may be in bulk yet in power and preciousness it far more excels all the others.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* X.7, 1177b31–1178a2 (tr. D. P. Chase)

The life of practical virtue, of "actions of justice, courage, and the other virtues, towards one another," comes second (X.8). On the **dominant** reading, happiness *is* contemplation, and all else matters only as support, which threatens an alarming conclusion: should a philosopher betray a friend for an extra year of study? On the **inclusive** reading, made famous by J. L. Ackrill's "Aristotle on Eudaimonia" (1974), happiness is a whole of all intrinsically valuable activities, with contemplation the best. Most scholars now seek a middle way: contemplation crowns a life whose substance is practical virtue and cannot be pursued by vicious means. Either way Aristotle makes a point modern ethics forgets. What we owe one another may not be the whole of a good life. Understanding a protein's structure, losing yourself in a fugue, or watching the night sky are not moral actions at all.

## Objections

### "But what should I *do*?"

The most common objection to virtue ethics is that it gives no guidance. Tell a utilitarian your dilemma and they will tell you to maximize well-being. Tell a virtue ethicist and they seem to say, "Do what a virtuous person would do." But you consulted them precisely because you do not know what that is. Aristotle's own answer is that no formula can replace perception trained by habit (II.9: such questions are "matters of detail, and the decision of them rests with the Moral Sense"), and that hard cases are hard for every theory. A sharper modern reply, Rosalind Hursthouse's criterion of right action and her "v-rules," is the subject of [Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md).

### The situationist challenge

Virtue ethics assumes robust *character traits* that produce consistent behavior across situations. Since the 1960s, social psychologists have reported that behavior depends far more than we think on small features of the situation: whether you are in a hurry, whether an authority tells you to continue. Gilbert Harman (1999) and John Doris (*Lack of Character*, 2002) concluded that robust traits are rare or nonexistent. Defenders reply that Aristotle always said virtue was rare, and that the practically wise person will *choose their situations*, which is very much in his spirit. [Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md) gives the debate as it bears on virtue ethics, and [Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md) examines the experiments themselves.

### Elitism and exclusion

Aristotle wrote for free, propertied Greek men with leisure. In the *Politics* he held that some people are slaves by nature, that women's deliberative capacity lacks authority, and that manual laborers lack the leisure for full virtue. His "great-souled man" (IV.3), who remembers favors done but not favors received, strikes most readers as insufferable. Martha Nussbaum's "capabilities approach" argues that the theory's core, that a good life is the exercise of human capacities and societies should be judged by whether they enable it, implies the rejection of these prejudices. If everyone has the capacity for practical reason, denying some people the education and voice to exercise it is unjust *by Aristotle's own standards*.

### Is it egoism in disguise?

Is the soldier who dies for his city acting for his own sake? Aristotle (IX.8) says the good person is the true self-lover, claiming the noblest things for themselves and preferring a brief noble act to a long, mild enjoyment. To some readers this is egoism dressed up. To others it dissolves the conflict between egoism and altruism: if your good *includes* others' good, caring about it is not selfishness in any ordinary sense.

## Living it

- **Audit habits, not principles.** You become what you repeatedly do. Pick one virtue you want, such as patience or generosity, and one small daily action that exercises it. Do it for a month and notice whether it starts to feel like you.
- **Ask "relative to whom?"** The right amount of directness or anger is not the same for a grieving friend and a careless contractor.
- **Correct for nearness.** When a near pleasure crowds out a distant good, imagine both a week away. Which would you choose?
- **Sort your friendships, gently.** Utility and pleasure friendships are real goods, but are you investing time in a friendship of character?
- **Choose your situations.** If the situationists are half right, character is partly architecture: keep the phone in another room and make promises in public. That is not cheating at virtue. It is practical wisdom about your own psychology.

## Summary

- Ancient ethics asks *how should I live?* Its framework, **eudaimonism**, holds that there is a final end, flourishing (*eudaimonia*), and that the virtues are central to it.
- **Socrates** held that virtue is knowledge, no one does wrong willingly, the virtues are one, and doing injustice is worse than suffering it. He treated weakness of will as an error of measurement.
- **Plato** divided the soul into reason, spirit, and appetite and made justice each part doing its own work, the health of the soul. Sachs objected that inner justice is not shown to yield ordinary just conduct.
- **Aristotle**'s **function argument** defines happiness as activity of the soul in accordance with excellence over a complete life. Fortune and external goods matter.
- **Virtues of character** come from habituation and are states of choosing a **mean relative to us**, fixed by the practically wise person. The mean is not moderation or a decision procedure, and some acts have no mean.
- **Practical wisdom** perceives particulars. It and good character require each other.
- **Friendship** comes in three kinds. The friend is "a second self." **Contemplation** is the highest happiness, which raises the dominant-versus-inclusive debate.
- Objections: action guidance (Hursthouse's reply), **situationism** (Milgram, Darley and Batson, Harman, Doris), elitism (Nussbaum's reply), and egoism.

### Dialogue 1 — Socrates and the midnight episode

**Socrates:** You look tired. Were you up late with your books?

**You:** …

*Your turn: tell Socrates honestly why you were up until 2 a.m., and whether you knew at the time it was a bad idea.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I wasn't studying. I was watching a series. I knew I had an exam this morning. I told myself "just one more" four times. I knew it was stupid the whole time.

**Socrates:** Did you? That is just what interests me. You say you *knew* it was bad to keep watching. Bad for what?

**You:** For the exam. For how I'd feel today. Which is terrible, by the way.

**Socrates:** So you judged that the pleasure of the episode was smaller than the pain of today?

**You:** Obviously. I'd give anything to have those three hours of sleep back.

**Socrates:** Now. But last night, at midnight, when you pressed the button for the next episode, did you judge then that the pleasure was smaller?

**You:** I… guess at that moment it felt bigger. The cliffhanger was right there. The exam felt far away.

**Socrates:** Like a tower on the horizon that looks smaller than your thumb?

**You:** Something like that.

**Socrates:** Then perhaps you knew it only in words, the way a child recites a lesson, and at the moment of choosing you misjudged the sizes. Your knowledge was not dragged about like a slave. At that moment, you did not have it.

**You:** That sounds like a nice excuse. "I didn't really know."

**Socrates:** Not an excuse, a diagnosis. If your fault were weakness, the cure would be to grit your teeth harder. If your fault was mismeasurement, the cure is a better way of measuring. Which cure has worked for you in the past?

**You:** Gritting my teeth has never worked. What would a better way of measuring be?

**Socrates:** You tell me. What could you do at noon, when the exam and the episode look their true sizes, so that at midnight they still look that way?

**You:** Delete the app on exam weeks. Or tell my roommate to take my laptop at eleven.

**Socrates:** That is wisdom acting in advance, when it can see clearly. Someone who knows how their eyes deceive them is already halfway to seeing straight.

### Dialogue 2 — Aristotle on the right amount of anger

**Aristotle:** You seem agitated. Something has happened?

**You:** …

*Your turn: describe a recent situation in which you were angry with someone, and say whether you think you handled it well.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** My project partner presented our work to the professor and took credit for all of it. Half of it was mine. I said nothing in the meeting, but afterward I sent him a really furious message. Now I feel bad about the message, but I also feel I should have said something in the meeting.

**Aristotle:** Good. You have noticed two failures, one of deficiency and one of excess. Was it right to be angry, and that angry?

**You:** I think so. He took credit for my work, and it's twenty percent of our grade.

**Aristotle:** I agree. Someone who feels nothing at such treatment is not gentle but slavish. Was it at the right time?

**You:** No. In the meeting, when it mattered, I froze. Then at ten at night I wrote three paragraphs.

**Aristotle:** And toward the right person, in the right way?

**You:** The right person, sure. The right way, no. I called him a parasite.

**Aristotle:** You see why I say that anyone can be angry, but to be angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, for the right purpose, and in the right way is not easy. You have the first two and lack the last three. What would the right way have looked like?

**You:** Maybe in the meeting just saying, calmly, "I worked on the analysis section, I'm happy to explain that part." Then talking to him privately.

**Aristotle:** That is close to the mean. It corrects the record without humiliating him. Can you do it next time?

**You:** I don't know. In the moment I freeze.

**Aristotle:** Then do it many times in smaller matters. When a waiter brings the wrong order, say so calmly. When a friend interrupts you, say "let me finish." We become good-tempered the way we become harp-players, by playing. And the message? You might send another, shorter one, saying that the first was too harsh but that your point stands.

**You:** That's practical wisdom, I guess. Knowing the particular thing to do.

**Aristotle:** It is the beginning of it. The rest is doing it until it no longer feels like a lesson.
