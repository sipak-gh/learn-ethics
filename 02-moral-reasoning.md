# Chapter 2 — Moral Reasoning

Chapter 1 described ethics as an inquiry with data (considered judgments) and a method (reflective equilibrium). This chapter shows the method at work. It covers the anatomy of a moral argument, using the oldest worked example we have, Socrates reasoning in his prison cell about whether to escape; the patterns and fallacies that recur in moral debate; the use of thought experiments, with the trolley problem as the main case study; and the problem of moral disagreement, which asks what you should do when careful people reach opposite conclusions. By the end you should be able to take a moral claim apart, find its weak premise, and argue about it without either shouting or shrugging. Previous: [Chapter 1 — What Is Ethics?](01-what-is-ethics.md). Next: [Chapter 3 — Relativism and Egoism](03-relativism-and-egoism.md).

## Reasoning in a prison cell

In 399 BCE Socrates sat in an Athenian prison waiting to be executed. His old friend Crito arrived before dawn with a plan: the guards were bribed, friends abroad were ready to receive him, the money was raised. Crito's arguments were the ones anyone would make. Your friends will be disgraced if people think they were too cheap to save you. Your children will be orphans. Your enemies win if you stay.

Socrates's reply is one of the earliest and most famous records of someone deciding a life-or-death question by explicit moral argument, and it is worth reading slowly. He begins by refusing to let urgency or emotion set the terms:

> For I am and always have been one of those natures who must be guided by reason, whatever the reason may be which upon reflection appears to me to be the best; and now that this chance has befallen me, I cannot repudiate my own words: the principles which I have hitherto honoured and revered I still honour, and unless we can at once find other and better principles, I am certain not to agree with you …
>
> — Plato, *Crito* 46b–c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Notice the structure of that commitment. Socrates does not say his principles are beyond question; he says he will keep them *unless better ones are found*. That is reflective equilibrium in miniature. He then sets aside the opinion of "the many" in favor of "what the truth will say," and secures two premises from Crito:

> And I should like to know whether I may say the same of another proposition—that not life, but a good life, is to be chiefly valued?
>
> CRITO: Yes, that also remains unshaken.
>
> SOCRATES: And a good life is equivalent to a just and honorable one—that holds also?
>
> — Plato, *Crito* 48b (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

From these, he says, "I proceed to argue the question whether I ought or ought not to try and escape." Money, reputation and the children's prospects drop out of the calculation unless they bear on whether escape is *just*. Then comes the principle that does most of the work:

> Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to any one, whatever evil we may have suffered from him. But I would have you consider, Crito, whether you really mean what you are saying. For this opinion has never been held, and never will be held, by any considerable number of persons; and those who are agreed and those who are not agreed upon this point have no common ground, and can only despise one another when they see how widely they differ.
>
> — Plato, *Crito* 49c–d (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Socrates knows his premise is radical. Greek popular morality, like most moralities, held that it is right to help friends and harm enemies. He flags the disagreement openly and asks Crito whether he *really* accepts it, because an argument built on a premise one party only pretends to accept is worthless.

### The argument laid out

Philosophers reconstruct arguments in numbered form to see exactly where they could fail. Here is the core of the *Crito* (49a–50a, with the "Laws of Athens" speech that follows):

1. One must never do wrong (injustice), even in return for wrong done to you.
2. To harm someone is to do wrong.
3. Escaping would harm the Laws and the city of Athens, because if court verdicts can be nullified by private persons, the laws lose their force.
4. One ought to keep just agreements.
5. By living in Athens for seventy years, having had the option to leave, Socrates agreed to abide by its laws and verdicts.
6. Therefore escaping would be doing wrong (from 1–3) and breaking a just agreement (from 4–5).
7. Therefore Socrates ought not to escape.

This is **valid**: if the premises are true, the conclusion must be. Whether it is **sound** (valid *and* with true premises) is another matter, and the interesting arguments about the *Crito* are all about the premises. Premise 3 is an empirical and causal claim: would one escape really "destroy" the laws? Premise 5 is the ancestor of every theory of tacit consent; David Hume's essay "Of the Original Contract" (1748) attacked it with the image of a man carried aboard ship while asleep, who can hardly be said to consent to the captain's authority by not jumping into the sea. And readers have long noticed that the *Apology* seems to clash with premise 4: there Socrates told the jury he would disobey an order to stop philosophizing. Is the *Crito* consistent with the *Apology*? Richard Kraut (*Socrates and the State*, 1984) argued that the Laws' speech allows a citizen to "persuade or obey," so that open, persuasive disobedience is permitted while secret flight is not.

You do not need to settle those debates to learn the lesson. A moral argument is good when it is valid, when its premises are more plausible than the denial of its conclusion, and when it does not hide its weakest step. Socrates's argument passes the first and third tests. Whether it passes the second is up to you.

### Moral and factual premises

Almost every applied moral argument mixes two kinds of premise: **normative** ("one must never harm a person"), and **descriptive** or factual ("escaping would harm the city"). This matters for two reasons.

First, many moral disagreements are really factual disagreements in disguise. People who disagree about the minimum wage often share the normative premise ("we should reduce poverty") and disagree about economics. Find the factual crux, and the argument may move from ethics to evidence.

Second, no purely factual set of premises can by itself yield a normative conclusion. If an argument goes from "this is how things are" to "so this is how things ought to be" without a normative premise, it has a gap. Hume made this point famous (Chapter 8). In practice, the gap is usually filled by an unstated premise, and stating it out loud often reveals that it is the weakest link. "Humans have always eaten meat, so eating meat is fine" needs the premise "whatever humans have always done is fine," which almost nobody believes once it is spelled out.

## Patterns of moral argument

Certain moves recur so often in moral debate that it pays to know them by name.

**Argument from consistency (or parity).** You accept A; B is relevantly like A; so you must accept B, or point to a relevant difference. This is the workhorse of moral reasoning. Most moral progress has come from it: if pain matters in humans, and animals feel pain, what relevant difference lets you ignore theirs? The reply is always the same: find a *relevant* difference. The whole fight is over relevance.

**Universalizability.** If an action is permissible for you, it is permissible for anyone in relevantly similar circumstances. Ask: "What if everyone did that?" or "Would I accept this if I were on the other side?" The golden rule, which appears in the Confucian *Analects* (Chapter 7), in Leviticus, in the Gospels and in many other traditions, is a rough version of this test. Kant's categorical imperative is a sharpened one (Chapter 9).

**Reductio ad absurdum.** Assume a principle, derive something unacceptable, and conclude that the principle is false. "If it's always wrong to lie, you must tell the murderer at the door where your friend is hiding." The defender can bite the bullet (Kant notoriously did, Chapter 9) or refine the principle.

**Counterexample.** A single clear case refutes a universal claim. "Pleasure is the only thing good in itself" faces Nozick's experience machine: would you plug into a machine that gives you any experiences you want, for life? If not, something besides experience matters.

**Slippery slope.** "If we allow A, we will end up allowing Z." There are two versions. The *logical* version says there is no principled line between A and Z, so whatever justifies A also justifies Z. The *empirical* version says that, as a matter of fact, allowing A will cause people to allow Z. The first is a consistency argument and can be strong. The second is a factual prediction and needs evidence. Many arguments in bioethics (Chapter 19) turn on which version is in play.

**Appeal to nature.** "It is natural, so it is good," or "unnatural, so bad." The premise "what is natural is good" is false on most readings (disease is natural, eyeglasses are not), so the argument usually needs a richer notion of nature, such as the one in natural law theory (Chapter 4), which makes it a different and better argument.

**Tu quoque** ("you too"). "You eat meat, so you can't criticize factory farming." This attacks the arguer's consistency, not the argument. Hypocrisy is a vice, but a hypocrite can still be right.

**Companions in guilt.** "Your objection to moral facts would also rule out mathematical facts (or epistemic norms), and you accept those." This is a parity argument at the metaethical level, much used in Chapter 17.

Two virtues of argument matter as much as any pattern. The first is **charity**: interpret your opponent so that her view comes out as strong as possible before you criticize it. Glaucon's reconstruction of Thrasymachus in *Republic* II (Chapter 1) is the model. The second is **knowing the other side**. John Stuart Mill made this the core of his defense of free discussion:

> He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that. His reasons may be good, and no one may have been able to refute them. But if he is equally unable to refute the reasons on the opposite side; if he does not so much as know what they are, he has no ground for preferring either opinion.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty*, chapter II (1859)

Mill adds that it is not enough to hear the opposing arguments from your own side's teachers; you must hear them "from persons who actually believe them." That is the spirit of this chapter's dialogues.

## Why imagine? Thought experiments in ethics

Ethics uses thought experiments the way physics uses laboratories. A real moral situation is messy: many factors vary at once, and you cannot tell which one is driving your judgment. A thought experiment holds everything fixed except one variable. Galileo did not need to drop balls from the Tower of Pisa to refute Aristotle's physics; he imagined tying a light ball to a heavy one and showed that Aristotle's theory predicted contradictory results. Moral philosophers do the same with cases.

You have already met one: the ring of Gyges removes the possibility of being caught so that you can ask whether justice is worth having for its own sake. Others you will meet: Singer's drowning child (Chapter 11), Thomson's violinist (Chapter 19), Rawls's veil of ignorance (Chapter 12), Nozick's experience machine, Williams's Jim and the Indians (Chapter 11). The most famous of all, and the most mocked, is the trolley problem.

### The original trolley: Foot (1967)

Philippa Foot introduced the case in a paper on abortion and the doctrine of double effect. She wanted to show that the doctrine was not needed to explain certain intuitions that seemed to support it. She asked her readers to compare a judge who could stop a murderous riot only by framing and executing an innocent man with the driver of a runaway tram. Her description of the second case is now famous:

> … he is the driver of a runaway tram which he can only steer from one narrow track on to another; five men are working on one track and one man on the other …
>
> — Philippa Foot, "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect" (1967), in *Virtues and Vices* (1978)

Whichever track the tram enters, the workers on it will die. Most people think the driver should steer toward the one. Most people think the judge should *not* frame the innocent man, even though here too it is one life against five. What is the difference?

Foot's answer appealed to the distinction between **negative duties** (not to harm) and **positive duties** (to help). Negative duties are much stronger. The judge would be violating a negative duty to one to fulfil a positive duty to five. The driver faces negative duties on both sides: whichever way he steers, he kills. So he should kill fewer.

### Thomson's variations (1976, 1985)

Judith Jarvis Thomson gave the puzzle its name and its most famous variants.

- **Bystander at the switch.** You are not the driver but a bystander next to a lever. If you pull it, the trolley diverts from the five to the one. Foot's driver-based explanation no longer applies: if you do nothing, you *let* five die rather than killing anyone. Yet most people still think you may pull the lever.
- **Footbridge** (Thomson called the victim "the fat man"). You stand on a bridge over the track beside a very large man. If you push him off, his body will stop the trolley and save the five. Most people think this is wrong, and many feel strongly that it is. But it is still one life for five.
- **Transplant.** A surgeon could harvest one healthy patient's organs to save five dying patients. Almost everyone thinks this is monstrous.

The puzzle is to find a principle that permits switching but forbids pushing and harvesting, and that is not ad hoc. That is the "trolley problem" in the strict sense: not "should you pull the lever?" but "what explains the pattern of our judgments across the cases?"

### Candidate principles

**The doctrine of double effect (DDE).** Its source is Thomas Aquinas's discussion of killing in self-defense:

> Nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention. Now moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention …
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* II-II, q. 64, a. 7 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

Aquinas adds a proportionality condition: "though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end." Later Catholic moral theology turned this into a four-part test. An act with a good and a bad effect is permissible if (1) the act itself is not wrong in kind; (2) the agent intends only the good effect; (3) the bad effect is not the *means* to the good effect; (4) there is proportionate reason for allowing the bad effect. In the bystander case, the death of the one is a foreseen side effect: if he somehow escaped, the five would still be saved. In the footbridge case, the large man's body is the means: you *need* him to be hit.

**The means principle.** A Kantian relative: it is wrong to use a person merely as a means (Chapter 9). Pushing the man uses him as a tool; diverting the trolley does not.

**Redirecting a threat.** Thomson's own 1985 proposal: it may be permissible to redirect an existing threat onto fewer people, but not to create a new threat or to use someone's body or property.

**Doing versus allowing.** Killing is worse than letting die. This explains why the surgeon (who would kill) should not harvest, but it struggles with the bystander, who kills by pulling the lever.

Every principle meets counterexamples. Thomson's **loop case** is the most famous. The side track loops back to the main track; the one man on the loop is large enough to stop the trolley, which would otherwise come round and kill the five. Diverting the trolley now saves the five *only because* it hits the one: his being hit is the means. DDE seems to forbid diverting. But many people feel that adding a few yards of track behind the one man cannot make a moral difference. Frances Kamm's "Doctrine of Triple Effect" (*Intricate Ethics*, 2007) distinguishes acting *in order to* bring about an effect from acting *because* it will occur, to handle such cases. The literature that grew out of this is sometimes called "trolleyology," not always kindly.

In 2008 Thomson changed her mind. In "Turning the Trolley" she argued that the bystander, unlike the driver, may *not* turn the trolley, since he would be making the one pay a cost that the bystander himself would not be willing to pay (she imagined a third option where the bystander could divert the trolley onto himself). Few have followed her, but the reversal is a good lesson in how reflective equilibrium works: a philosopher of the first rank revised a case judgment rather than adopt a principle she found unprincipled.

### Objections to trolleyology

The trolley problem has attracted sharp criticism, and you should know the objections.

1. **Unrealism.** Real choices never come with certainty that five will die, that the man will stop the trolley, that there is no other option. Allen Wood (in Parfit's *On What Matters*, vol. 2, 2011) argued that the cases are so artificial that our responses to them are poor evidence about anything. Reply: the artificiality is the point; it isolates a variable. But it is fair to ask whether intuitions trained on real life are reliable in conditions that real life never presents.
2. **Distraction.** Some critics think the focus on rare dilemmas distracts from ordinary moral life, where the hard part is not knowing what to do but doing it. That is a criticism of emphasis, not of the method.
3. **Unreliable intuitions.** Experimental philosophers have found that responses to trolley cases shift with the order in which cases are presented. Eric Schwitzgebel and Fiery Cushman (*Mind & Language*, 2012) found such order effects even among professional philosophers. Joshua Greene's brain-imaging studies (beginning with *Science*, 2001) suggested that "up close and personal" harms like pushing trigger fast emotional responses, while impersonal ones like switching allow cost-benefit reasoning. Greene concluded that deontological intuitions are often emotional reactions dressed up as principles, and should be discounted. Critics such as Selim Berker ("The Normative Insignificance of Neuroscience," *Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 2009) replied that the neuroscience does no normative work: you still need a philosophical argument that the factors the emotions track are morally irrelevant. Chapter 18 takes up this debate.

Despite all this, trolley cases have escaped the seminar room. Engineers designing self-driving cars face programming choices about whom a car should protect in an unavoidable crash. The "Moral Machine" project (Edmond Awad and colleagues, *Nature*, 2018) collected some forty million decisions from people in over two hundred countries and territories, finding broad agreement on sparing more lives and sparing the young, with significant regional variation in other preferences. Whether such data should guide the programming is itself an ethical question (Chapter 21).

### Doing and allowing: the bathtub cases

A cousin of the trolley problem shows how thought experiments can be used to *attack* a distinction. In "Active and Passive Euthanasia" (1975), James Rachels imagined two cases. Smith stands to inherit from his young cousin, so he drowns him in the bath. Jones stands to inherit from his cousin, and enters the bathroom intending to drown him; the boy slips, hits his head and falls face-down in the water, and Jones watches him drown, ready to push him under if needed. Rachels argued that Jones is no better than Smith, so killing is not, *in itself*, worse than letting die. He concluded that the medical distinction between active euthanasia (forbidden) and withdrawing treatment (permitted) could not rest on that difference.

Critics replied that the cases are poorly controlled: both agents are maximally vicious, so the comparison may hit a ceiling effect, like comparing two people who are both obviously guilty of the worst crime. Other pairs of cases, where the motive is benign, do seem to show a difference between killing and letting die. This is the general shape of debates about "equalizing" thought experiments: when two cases seem equally bad, ask whether something else is masking the difference.

## Particulars and principles

Foot's and Thomson's method assumes that the right response to a puzzling pattern of intuitions is to find a principle. Not everyone agrees. **Moral particularism**, defended by Jonathan Dancy (*Ethics Without Principles*, 2004), holds that the moral relevance of a feature can change from case to case: that an act gives someone pleasure is usually a reason in its favor, but not if it is pleasure in someone else's suffering. If reasons behave like that, no finite set of principles can capture morality, and moral wisdom is a matter of seeing what matters in each situation.

Particularism has ancient roots. Aristotle insisted that ethics is imprecise and that the final judgment about particular cases cannot be fixed in rules. In Chase's translation, which uses an eighteenth-century term for Aristotle's word *aisthēsis* ("perception"):

> But to what point or degree a man must err in order to incur blame, it is not easy to determine exactly in words: nor in fact any of those points which are matter of perception by the Moral Sense: such questions are matters of detail, and the decision of them rests with the Moral Sense.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* II.9, 1109b20–23 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Most philosophers hold a middle position. Principles are real and useful, but they are defeasible: they state what *tends* to matter, and applying them takes judgment. W. D. Ross's *prima facie* duties (fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, non-maleficence) are the classic version. You can know that you have a duty to keep promises and a duty to prevent harm without knowing, in advance, which wins when they collide.

## Moral disagreement

The most unsettling fact about ethics is that thoughtful people disagree about it. Socrates saw why such disagreement feels different from disagreement in other fields:

> SOCRATES: And what sort of difference creates enmity and anger? Suppose for example that you and I, my good friend, differ about a number; do differences of this sort make us enemies and set us at variance with one another? Do we not go at once to arithmetic, and put an end to them by a sum?
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 7b–c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Measuring settles disputes about size, and weighing settles disputes about weight. But:

> I dare say the answer does not occur to you at the moment, and therefore I will suggest that these enmities arise when the matters of difference are the just and unjust, good and evil, honourable and dishonourable.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 7c–d (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Socrates is not saying that there is no truth about justice. He is saying that we lack an agreed *procedure* for reaching it, and that this is why moral disagreement makes people angry. What should you make of that?

### Sources of disagreement

Before concluding anything, diagnose. Moral disagreements can come from:

1. **Factual disagreement**: about consequences, about what animals feel, about whether a fetus is conscious at a given stage.
2. **Different weights** on values that both sides share: liberty against equality, loyalty against fairness.
3. **Different scope**: who counts? Darwin, in *The Descent of Man*, saw moral history largely as the widening of the circle of concern:

> As man advances in civilisation, and small tribes are united into larger communities, the simplest reason would tell each individual that he ought to extend his social instincts and sympathies to all the members of the same nation, though personally unknown to him. This point being once reached, there is only an artificial barrier to prevent his sympathies extending to the men of all nations and races.
>
> — Charles Darwin, *The Descent of Man*, chapter IV (2nd ed., 1874)

He goes on to call humanity to animals "one of the latest moral acquisitions." Peter Singer's *The Expanding Circle* (1981) develops the idea.
4. **Bias and self-interest**: Sidgwick's warning that "whatever we desire we are apt to pronounce desirable" (Chapter 1).
5. **Conceptual confusion**: people using "natural," "violence" or "freedom" in different senses.
6. **Bedrock disagreement**: different fundamental values that would survive full information and reflection.

Only the last kind threatens the idea that ethics has answers. How much of moral disagreement is of that kind is an empirical question. John Doris and Alexandra Plakias ("How to Argue about Disagreement," 2008) discussed evidence that some differences, such as the greater acceptance of violent responses to insult in "cultures of honor" studied by Richard Nisbett and Dov Cohen, may not reduce to factual disagreement. Realists reply that defenders of honor cultures typically hold false beliefs or act under pressures that full reflection would remove. The debate is open.

### What disagreement implies

**For metaethics.** J. L. Mackie's "argument from relativity" (*Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, 1977) holds that moral disagreement is best explained by the hypothesis that moral codes reflect ways of life rather than perceptions of objective values. Realists respond that disagreement is equally widespread in philosophy, history and the frontiers of science, which are not therefore unreal; and that much moral disagreement is explained by factors (ignorance, bias, interest) that would distort perception of real facts. Chapter 17 and [the nihilism course's chapter on error theory](../learn-nihilism/12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) follow the argument.

**For your own beliefs.** Here the question is epistemological. Suppose you discover that someone you regard as your **epistemic peer** (as intelligent, informed and careful as you) disagrees with you about whether eating meat is permissible. Should you lower your confidence?

- **Conciliationists** (Adam Elga, David Christensen) say yes: you have no independent reason to think you are the one who got it right, so you should move toward the middle, or suspend judgment. This is Sidgwick's fourth test turned into a rule.
- **Steadfast** views (Thomas Kelly, in part) say you may sometimes keep your view, especially if the first-order evidence really does support it, or if you have special access to your own reasoning.

Conciliationism has a startling implication for ethics, where peer disagreement is everywhere: it seems to recommend suspending judgment on nearly every controversial question. Some see this as a reductio of conciliationism. Others accept a large dose of moral humility.

**For acting.** You cannot suspend action. If you are unsure whether eating meat is wrong, you still eat or do not eat. The new field of **moral uncertainty** (William MacAskill, Krister Bykvist and Toby Ord, *Moral Uncertainty*, 2020) asks how to act when you are unsure which moral view is true. One proposal: if one option is seriously wrong on a view you take seriously and only mildly suboptimal on your favored view, take the safer option. Critics ask how to compare the size of "wrongness" across theories that measure it in different currencies.

### Moral testimony and moral expertise

Suppose you could ask a wise person what to do, and simply believe her. In most fields that is fine; you believe your doctor about your blood test. But many philosophers find something wrong with deferring in ethics. If you believe eating meat is wrong only because an expert said so, without understanding why, something seems missing. Alison Hills ("Moral Testimony and Moral Epistemology," *Ethics*, 2009) argues that what matters in ethics is not just knowledge but **moral understanding**: grasping the reasons, so that you could apply them to new cases. Sarah McGrath has argued that the asymmetry between moral and non-moral testimony is a real puzzle for moral realism. The Socratic tradition sides with understanding: Socrates never asks Crito to defer to him, but to examine the argument and "refute me if you can."

## Living it

**Find the crux.** Pick one moral disagreement you have had recently, with a friend, a relative or a stranger online. Write down your position and the other person's in the most charitable form you can, as if each of you were Glaucon restating Thrasymachus. Then lay out each side's argument in numbered premises. Mark each premise **N** (normative) or **F** (factual). Now find the *first* premise where you part ways. Is it factual? Then the next step is evidence, not argument. Is it normative? Then ask whether you share any deeper principle that could settle it, and test it with a thought experiment that changes just one variable.

**Trolley diary.** Over the next month, notice the real-life cousins of trolley cases: choices where you would cause a small harm to one person to prevent a larger harm to several, or would decline to. (Reallocating a shared resource at work, deciding which of two friends to disappoint.) For each, ask: am I using someone as a means, or redirecting a burden that was already on its way? Does the distinction feel like it tracks something real, or does it seem like a rationalization? Crito would have said the stakes were too high for such questions. Socrates thought the stakes were exactly why they mattered.

## Summary

- A moral argument should be **valid**, rest on premises more plausible than the denial of its conclusion, and make its weakest premise visible. The *Crito* is the oldest worked example: never do wrong, even in return; escaping would wrong the Laws; therefore stay.
- Most applied arguments mix **normative** and **factual** premises; many disagreements are factual underneath. No set of purely factual premises yields an "ought" without a normative premise, often unstated.
- Recurring patterns: consistency and parity, universalizability, reductio, counterexample, slippery slope (logical vs. empirical), appeal to nature, tu quoque, companions in guilt. Virtues: charity, and knowing the other side (Mill).
- **Thought experiments** isolate variables. The trolley problem (Foot 1967, Thomson 1976 and 1985) asks what principle explains why switching seems permissible while pushing and harvesting do not. Candidates: double effect (from Aquinas), the means principle, redirecting threats, doing vs. allowing, negative vs. positive duties. Thomson's loop case and her 2008 reversal show the method under strain.
- Objections: unrealism, distraction from ordinary life, unreliable intuitions (order effects; Greene's dual-process findings, contested by Berker).
- **Particularism** (Dancy) denies that principles capture morality; Aristotle leaves the final judgment to perception; Ross's *prima facie* duties are a middle way.
- **Disagreement**: diagnose its source (facts, weights, scope, bias, confusion, bedrock). It bears on metaethics (Mackie's argument from relativity), on your confidence (conciliationism vs. steadfastness), on action under **moral uncertainty**, and on whether you may simply defer to moral experts (Hills: ethics aims at understanding).

## Dialogues

### Dialogue 1 — On the footbridge

**Philippa Foot:** You have just said that you would pull the lever to divert the trolley from five people to one, but that you would never push the large man off the footbridge. In both cases one dies and five live. Why the difference?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Foot in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what she will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Pushing him is actually killing him with my own hands. Pulling a lever is more indirect.

**Foot:** Suppose the footbridge has a trapdoor, and the man is standing on it. You need only pull a lever to drop him onto the track. Is that now permissible?

**You:** No. It still feels just as wrong. So it isn't about my hands.

**Foot:** Then what is it about?

**You:** In the footbridge case I'm using him. His body is the thing that stops the trolley. In the switch case the one person just happens to be on the other track. If he weren't there, the plan would work just as well.

**Foot:** That is the doctrine of double effect, or something close to it: his death is intended as a means in one case and merely foreseen in the other. I was suspicious of that doctrine. Let me test it. Thomson imagined a loop: the side track curves back to the main line, and the one man on it is heavy enough to stop the trolley. If you divert, you save the five only because the trolley hits him. Do you divert?

**You:** Hmm. I think I would. The loop doesn't seem to make a moral difference.

**Foot:** But in the loop case you need him to be hit, just as in the footbridge. So either your principle is wrong, or your judgment about the loop is.

**You:** Maybe I need a different principle. In the switch and loop cases I'm redirecting a threat that already exists. In the footbridge case I'm dragging someone who was completely safe into the path of danger.

**Foot:** Is the man on the side track not "completely safe" until you pull the lever?

**You:** He is. But he's on the tracks; he's already in the space where trolleys go. The man on the bridge isn't.

**Foot:** You are now saying that location matters morally. Some philosophers have said exactly that, and others think it is a symptom of desperation. Let me offer my own view. I thought the key was the difference between negative duties, not to injure, and positive duties, to aid. The driver of the tram will kill someone whatever he does, so he should kill fewer. The surgeon who harvests organs would violate a strong negative duty in order to meet a weaker positive one.

**You:** But then the bystander at the switch isn't like the driver. If he does nothing he only lets the five die. So on your view shouldn't he leave the lever alone?

**Foot:** That is precisely the objection Thomson pressed against me, and it is why the bystander case became the center of the debate. Thomson herself ended up agreeing that the bystander should not switch.

**You:** Then I'm left with three options: accept that I shouldn't switch, accept that pushing is fine, or find a principle no one has found yet.

**Foot:** Or a fourth: accept that your judgments are tracking something real that you cannot yet articulate, and keep working. That is not defeat. It is the condition of most interesting inquiry. What you should not do is pretend that a principle explains your judgments when it does not.

**You:** So the trolley problem isn't really about trolleys.

**Foot:** It never was. It is about what we may do to one person for the sake of others. Think about hospitals, wars and pandemics, and you will find that question everywhere.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether to change your mind

**Socrates:** You told me you are certain that eating meat is permissible. Your friend Ana, who is as thoughtful as you and has read the same arguments, is certain it is wrong. What should each of you do about that?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** We should each respect the other's view. It's a personal choice.

**Socrates:** Is it? Ana thinks that animals are being made to suffer. If she is right, is it a personal choice in the way that choosing a shirt is?

**You:** No. If she's right, it's more like a choice that affects others.

**Socrates:** Then "personal choice" does not end the matter. Let us ask instead: what explains the fact that you and Ana disagree?

**You:** Maybe we have different values.

**Socrates:** Do you think animal suffering matters at all?

**You:** Of course. I'm against cruelty to animals.

**Socrates:** And does Ana think human interests, such as pleasure in food and the livelihood of farmers, matter at all?

**You:** Yes, she does.

**Socrates:** Then you share your values, at least in kind. Perhaps you differ on facts: on how much animals on farms suffer, or on whether they are aware of what happens to them. Or you differ on weights: on how much their suffering counts against your pleasure.

**You:** Probably both. I've never actually looked into how farms work.

**Socrates:** Then the first step is not to respect her view or to defend yours, but to find out. Mill would add that you should hear the case for her view from someone who actually holds it, not from someone who mocks it.

**You:** Fine. But suppose I look into it, and we still disagree. We've both seen the same evidence.

**Socrates:** Then you have what the philosophers of your day call a peer disagreement. Some say that you should then split the difference and become less confident. Does that seem right?

**You:** It seems too easy. If I'm right, why should I give it up just because Ana is stubborn?

**Socrates:** And Ana says the same about you. Neither of you can point to anything that shows *you* are the stubborn one. That is the uncomfortable part.

**You:** So maybe I should be less certain. But I still have to decide what to eat tonight.

**Socrates:** Then perhaps ask a different question. Not "who is right?" but "what should a person do who is genuinely unsure whether this practice is a serious wrong?" If eating meat is wrong, it may be very wrong. If it is permissible, giving it up costs you some pleasure. How do those risks compare?

**You:** That pushes me toward eating less meat, at least. But that argument would also push me to give up almost anything that some serious person thinks is wrong.

**Socrates:** A good objection. Then the weight of the argument depends on how seriously you take the view, and on what it costs to follow it. Those are things you can examine. Notice where we are: you began with "it's a personal choice" and you now have a question about evidence, a question about weights, and a question about how to act under uncertainty. Which of the three will you investigate first?

</details>
