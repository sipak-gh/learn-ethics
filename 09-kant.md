# Chapter 9 — Kant: Duty, Dignity, and the Moral Law

Hume grounded morality in feeling. Immanuel Kant admired Hume and set out to show that morality cannot rest on anything so contingent: a feeling can describe us, but it cannot *bind* us. His alternative is the most ambitious idea in modern moral philosophy: a single principle, the categorical imperative, follows from the bare idea of a rational will, commands every rational being whatever they want, and explains why persons have a worth beyond all price. This chapter works through the *Groundwork* (Abbott's "Fundamental Principles"): the good will, acting from duty, maxims and imperatives, universal law, humanity, autonomy and the kingdom of ends. It then tests the theory on the murderer at the door and sets out the strongest criticisms and Kantian replies. Previous: [Chapter 8 — Hume and the Sentimentalists](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md). Next: [Chapter 10 — Classical Utilitarianism](10-classical-utilitarianism.md).

## The clockwork man of Königsberg

Kant (1724–1804) never travelled more than a few days' journey from Königsberg, the Baltic port where he was born, taught and died. The son of a harness-maker, he grew up among Pietists, Lutherans who prized an upright inner conscience over ritual. The legend that housewives set their clocks by his afternoon walk is probably polished, but it is well attested that a portrait of Rousseau hung in his study, and that Rousseau taught him, by his own account, to honor ordinary people rather than only the learned. That respect runs through the *Groundwork* (1785): Kant claims to make explicit the principle that ordinary decent people already use, not to invent a new morality.

The *Groundwork* has a modest-sounding aim. Kant says the treatise is "nothing more than the investigation and establishment of the supreme principle of morality" (Preface, Ak. 4:392). It moves in three steps. The First Section starts from "common rational knowledge" and extracts the principle that ordinary moral judgment presupposes. The Second Section restates that principle philosophically and gives it several formulations. The Third Section asks whether we are entitled to think the principle actually binds us, which turns out to depend on whether we are free. Treatment of Kant's theoretical philosophy (the *Critique of Pure Reason*, the synthetic a priori) lives in [the Philosophy course, Chapter 13](../learn-philosophy/13-kant.md); here the focus is practical reason.

## A jewel that shines by its own light

The *Groundwork* opens with one of the most famous sentences in philosophy:

> Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals*, First Section, Ak. 4:393 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

The argument is a sorting exercise. Take anything else people praise: intelligence, courage, wealth, health, even happiness. Each is good only *conditionally*: good if it is used well, bad if it serves a bad will. Kant's sharpest example is a quality the ancients admired without reservation:

> For without the principles of a good will, they may become extremely bad, and the coolness of a villain not only makes him far more dangerous, but also directly makes him more abominable in our eyes than he would have been without it.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, First Section, Ak. 4:394 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

The Stoic sage's calm (see [the Stoicism course, Chapter 7](../learn-stoicism/07-the-passions.md)) is splendid in Cato and chilling in a poisoner. So the good will alone has *unconditional* value. And its value does not depend on its success:

> Even if it should happen that, owing to special disfavour of fortune, or the niggardly provision of a step-motherly nature, this will should wholly lack power to accomplish its purpose, if with its greatest efforts it should yet achieve nothing, and there should remain only the good will (not, to be sure, a mere wish, but the summoning of all means in our power), then, like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, First Section, Ak. 4:394 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

Note the parenthesis: not a mere wish but "the summoning of all means in our power." Once you have done everything you can, the moral worth of what you did is settled, whatever happens next.

Put as an argument:

1. Something is good without qualification only if it is good in every circumstance and its goodness does not depend on anything else.
2. Every candidate except the good will (talents, temperament, fortune, happiness) can be bad when combined with a bad will.
3. The good will's goodness does not depend on its effects, since it remains good when fortune frustrates it.
4. So the good will alone is good without qualification.

The consequentialist will push back on premise 3: surely a will is good *because* the effects it aims at are good? Kant replies that identical effects can come from a good will, a lucky accident or a calculating egoist, so the value must lie in *how* the will is determined. That points to duty.

## Acting from duty: the shopkeeper and the philanthropist

Kant distinguishes four kinds of action (Ak. 4:397–399):

1. Actions **contrary to duty** (lying to a customer).
2. Actions **in accordance with duty** but done from self-interest.
3. Actions in accordance with duty done from **immediate inclination** (you help because you enjoy helping).
4. Actions done **from duty** (*aus Pflicht*), because they are right.

The shopkeeper illustrates the second case:

> For example, it is always a matter of duty that a dealer should not over charge an inexperienced purchaser; and wherever there is much commerce the prudent tradesman does not overcharge, but keeps a fixed price for everyone, so that a child buys of him as well as any other. Men are thus honestly served; but this is not enough to make us believe that the tradesman has so acted from duty and from principles of honesty: his own advantage required it.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, First Section, Ak. 4:397 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

The child gets correct change. Nothing is wrong with the action. But we do not *esteem* the shopkeeper for it, any more than we esteem a vending machine. Kant then turns to the third case, the naturally kind person, and says something that has scandalized readers for two centuries:

> But I maintain that in such a case an action of this kind, however proper, however amiable it may be, has nevertheless no true moral worth, but is on a level with other inclinations, e.g., the inclination to honour, which, if it is happily directed to that which is in fact of public utility and accordant with duty and consequently honourable, deserves praise and encouragement, but not esteem.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, First Section, Ak. 4:398 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

Then the famous thought experiment. Imagine the same philanthropist whose mind is "clouded by sorrow of his own," so that he feels nothing for others, and who nonetheless "tears himself out of this dead insensibility" and helps "simply from duty," then, Kant says, "then first has his action its genuine moral worth" (Ak. 4:398).

Friedrich Schiller mocked this in satirical epigrams (1796): a disciple fears he is not virtuous because he helps his friends with pleasure, and is told to learn to despise them and then do with disgust what duty commands. The jibe is funny but unfair. Kant does not say the sympathetic person acts *wrongly*. The sorrowful case *isolates* the motive of duty: when inclination and duty point the same way, we cannot tell which is doing the work.

Scholars read the point in two main ways:

- **The limiting-condition reading.** Barbara Herman ("On the Value of Acting from the Motive of Duty," 1981) and Marcia Baron (*Kantian Ethics Almost Without Apology*, 1995) argue that you may act from love, provided duty stands guard: you would not act if the action were wrong, and would still act if love failed.
- **The strict reading.** Kant really does deny moral worth to actions from inclination, and this is a cost of the view. *The Metaphysics of Morals* (1797) adds a duty to *cultivate* sympathy, which softens the tension without removing it.

What is the motive of duty? Kant's answer is *respect* (*Achtung*) for the moral law, a feeling unlike the others because reason produces it:

> What I recognise immediately as a law for me, I recognise with respect. This merely signifies the consciousness that my will is subordinate to a law, without the intervention of other influences on my sense.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, First Section, Ak. 4:401n (tr. T. K. Abbott)

## Maxims and imperatives

Two technical terms carry the rest of the theory.

A **maxim** (*Maxime*) is the subjective principle on which you act: roughly, "In circumstances C, I will do A in order to achieve E." Kant thinks every deliberate action has one, whether or not you have spelled it out. Moral evaluation targets maxims, not bodily movements: handing over money can be paying a debt, bribing an official or giving alms, depending on the maxim.

An **imperative** is how a principle of reason appears to a will that does not automatically follow it. A perfectly rational, "holy" will would simply do what is good; for it "ought is here out of place, because the volition is already of itself necessarily in unison with the law" (Ak. 4:414). We are not holy. We have inclinations that pull against reason, so reason speaks to us in the form of commands.

They come in two kinds:

> If now the action is good only as a means to something else, then the imperative is hypothetical; if it is conceived as good in itself and consequently as being necessarily the principle of a will which of itself conforms to reason, then it is categorical.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:414 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

"If you want to pass the exam, study" is **hypothetical**: it binds you only if you have the end. Drop the end, and the command evaporates. (Kant splits these into rules of *skill* and counsels of *prudence*, the latter aimed at happiness.) A **categorical** imperative (*kategorischer Imperativ*) says "Do A," full stop. It binds you whatever you want.

Why think there are any categorical imperatives? Because ordinary moral thought assumes them. Torturing the cat is not wrong "*if* you want to be liked"; if the torturer does not care about being liked, you do not withdraw the claim. Philippa Foot later challenged exactly this, in "Morality as a System of Hypothetical Imperatives" (1972), arguing that moral "oughts" are no more categorical than the rules of etiquette; that debate returns in [Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md) and [Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md).

The puzzle Kant has set himself: what could a command be that does not get its force from any end you happen to have? It cannot say "do whatever achieves X," because then it would be hypothetical. All that is left is the *form* of law itself: universality.

## The Formula of Universal Law

Kant had already reached the principle in the First Section:

> I am never to act otherwise than so that I could also will that my maxim should become a universal law.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, First Section, Ak. 4:402 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

In the Second Section he gives it its canonical form, the **Formula of Universal Law** (FUL):

> There is therefore but one categorical imperative, namely, this: Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:421 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

He immediately adds a variant, the **Formula of the Law of Nature** (FLN): "Act as if the maxim of thy action were to become by thy will a universal law of nature" (Ak. 4:421). The test is a thought experiment. Imagine a world in which everyone acts on your maxim as reliably as stones fall. Can you consistently *conceive* that world? Can you consistently *will* it while also willing your own action?

Kant runs four examples, sorted by two distinctions: duties to oneself versus duties to others, and **perfect** duties (which admit no exception for the sake of inclination) versus **imperfect** duties (which leave latitude about when and how).

**The lying promise** (perfect duty to others). A man needs money and plans to promise repayment he knows he cannot make. Universalize:

> For supposing it to be a universal law that everyone when he thinks himself in a difficulty should be able to promise whatever he pleases, with the purpose of not keeping his promise, the promise itself would become impossible, as well as the end that one might have in view in it, since no one would consider that anything was promised to him, but would ridicule all such statements as vain pretences.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:422 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

The lying promise works only because promises are generally kept. Universalized, it destroys the practice it parasitizes. This is a **contradiction in conception**: the universalized maxim cannot even be coherently thought together with the purpose of the maxim.

**Suicide from self-love** (perfect duty to oneself). Kant argues that a nature whose law was to destroy life "by means of the very feeling whose special nature it is to impel to the improvement of life would contradict itself" (Ak. 4:422). Almost no commentator finds this example convincing; it relies on a teleological claim about the purpose of feelings that the rest of the theory does not need.

**Neglecting one's talents** (imperfect duty to oneself). A world of idlers is conceivable, Kant concedes, but a rational being "necessarily wills that his faculties be developed" (Ak. 4:423).

**Refusing to help** (imperfect duty to others). A comfortable man says: let everyone be as happy as Heaven pleases; I will take nothing from anyone, and give nothing. Kant grants that such a world could exist, "and doubtless even better than in a state in which everyone talks of sympathy and good-will" but cheats when he can. Yet:

> For a will which resolved this would contradict itself, inasmuch as many cases might occur in which one would have need of the love and sympathy of others, and in which, by such a law of nature, sprung from his own will, he would deprive himself of all hope of the aid he desires.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:423 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

This is a **contradiction in the will**: the universalized maxim is conceivable, but you cannot will it while also willing what, as a finite and needy being, you necessarily will.

### What kind of contradiction?

Christine Korsgaard ("Kant's Formula of Universal Law," 1985) distinguishes three readings of "contradiction in conception":

- **Logical**: the universalized maxim is self-contradictory (a world where promising is universal and no promises exist).
- **Teleological**: the maxim conflicts with the natural purpose of some practice or faculty (the suicide example reads this way).
- **Practical**: in the universalized world, your action would no longer achieve your purpose. The lying promise fails because no one would lend on a promise; your *end* (getting the money) becomes unattainable by your *means*.

Korsgaard argues the practical reading fits Kant's text best and handles the most cases. It also explains why the test tracks something real: wrongdoing so often consists in **free-riding**, relying on others to follow a rule you exempt yourself from. Kant says this directly:

> If now we attend to ourselves on occasion of any transgression of duty, we shall find that we in fact do not will that our maxim should be a universal law, for that is impossible for us; on the contrary, we will that the opposite should remain a universal law, only we assume the liberty of making an exception in our own favour or (just for this time only) in favour of our inclination.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:424 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

"Just for this time only" is one of the most psychologically acute phrases in the book.

### False positives and false negatives

The standard objection to FUL is that it gives the wrong verdicts at the edges. Critics have pressed two kinds of counterexample:

- **False negatives** (innocent maxims that fail the test). "I will buy a coffee at the quiet café at 7:55 to avoid the 8:00 rush" cannot be universalized: if everyone did it, 7:55 would be the rush. "I will give more to charity than the average person" is unfulfillable if universal. Yet neither is wrong.
- **False positives** (wrong maxims that pass). A maxim gerrymandered to apply only to you, "When I am a 1.83-meter philosopher named Sam in Königsberg on a Tuesday, I will lie," can be universalized without any practical contradiction, since almost nobody else falls under it.

Kantians reply that maxims are the agent's *actual*, fundamental intentions (O'Neill, *Acting on Principle*, 1975): the gerrymandered liar's real maxim is "lie when convenient," and that fails. Herman (*The Practice of Moral Judgment*, 1993) adds that FUL is not a mechanical algorithm but presupposes "rules of moral salience" that tell you which situations need testing at all; nothing in the coffee case is morally salient. Critics worry that these replies import judgment from outside the formula, which is what the formula was supposed to supply.

## Humanity as an end in itself

The second major formulation has had even greater influence. Kant asks what could be an *end in itself*, something whose value is not conditional on anyone's wanting it. Not objects of inclination, which have value only because they are wanted. Not inclinations themselves. The answer:

> Beings whose existence depends not on our will but on nature's, have nevertheless, if they are irrational beings, only a relative value as means, and are therefore called things; rational beings, on the contrary, are called persons, because their very nature points them out as ends in themselves, that is as something which must not be used merely as means.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:428 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

From this comes the **Formula of Humanity** (FH):

> So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:429 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

Three details matter.

First, *merely* (Abbott: "only"). You use the barista as a means to coffee and the surgeon as a means to health, and Kant has no objection. The wrong lies in treating someone *merely* as a means: in a way that ignores the fact that they, too, set ends.

Second, "humanity" (*Menschheit*) means *rational nature*, the capacity to set ends and act on reasons, not membership in the species. That has consequences for animals, discussed below.

Third, "in thine own person." The formula grounds duties to yourself: not to degrade yourself, not to sell yourself, to develop your capacities.

What does "merely as a means" amount to? Kant's gloss on the lying promise suggests a **consent test**: the person deceived "cannot possibly assent to my mode of acting towards him" (Ak. 4:429–430). O'Neill ("Between Consenting Adults," 1985) asks not whether the person does or would consent but whether they *could*: you cannot agree to be deceived, because deception works only if you do not know about it. Deception and coercion bypass the other person's reason instead of addressing it.

## Dignity and price

The Formula of Humanity leads to Kant's most quoted distinction:

> In the kingdom of ends everything has either value or dignity. Whatever has a value can be replaced by something else which is equivalent; whatever, on the other hand, is above all value, and therefore admits of no equivalent, has a dignity.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:434 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

Abbott's "value" translates *Preis*, price; modern translations say "price" and "dignity" (*Würde*). Kant adds that what serves general wants has a "market value," and what pleases taste a "fancy value," but that which makes anything an end in itself has "not merely a relative worth, i.e., value, but an intrinsic worth, that is, dignity" (Ak. 4:435).

This is the root of the modern idea of human dignity in law and human-rights documents, and a direct challenge to the next two chapters. If persons have dignity, they cannot be traded off at an exchange rate; the utilitarian who would push one person off a footbridge to save five treats the one as a fungible unit of welfare. Each person is an irreplaceable source of value, not a container for it. Robert Nozick's "side constraints" and Rawls's charge that utilitarianism "does not take seriously the distinction between persons" ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)) both descend from this passage.

## Autonomy and the kingdom of ends

The third idea in the Second Section is **autonomy** (from Greek *autos*, self, and *nomos*, law):

> Autonomy of the will is that property of it by which it is a law to itself (independently of any property of the objects of volition).
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:440 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

This is a revolutionary claim. Every earlier moral theory, Kant argues, located the source of obligation outside the will: in God's command, in human nature, in the pursuit of happiness, in moral sentiment. He calls all of them **heteronomy** (*heteros*, other). They make morality a hypothetical imperative: obey God *if* you fear punishment; be virtuous *if* you want to flourish. Only a law the will gives itself can bind categorically. The moral law is not imposed on you; it is the law of your own reason. That is why obeying it is freedom rather than servitude.

The image that unifies the formulas is the **kingdom of ends** (*Reich der Zwecke*):

> A rational being belongs as a member to the kingdom of ends when, although giving universal laws in it, he is also himself subject to these laws. He belongs to it as sovereign when, while giving laws, he is not subject to the will of any other.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:433 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

Picture a commonwealth in which every member is both legislator and citizen, and each law is one that all could will. Kant admits "It is certainly only an ideal" (Ak. 4:433). The ideal is meant to guide: act as a legislating member of such a kingdom would.

Kant claims that the formulas are equivalent:

> The three modes of presenting the principle of morality that have been adduced are at bottom only so many formulae of the very same law, and each of itself involves the other two.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles*, Second Section, Ak. 4:436 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

Few scholars accept this at face value: FUL is a formal test of maxims, FH a substantive claim about persons, and they can come apart. Allen Wood (*Kant's Ethical Thought*, 1999) argues that FH is more fundamental. A practical reader can treat the formulas as lenses on one idea: you must be able to justify your principles to everyone affected, as an equal.

### Freedom, and the gallows

The Third Section argues that the moral law binds any being who must act "under the idea of freedom," as we must. The metaphysics (free as things in themselves, determined as appearances) is covered in [the Philosophy course, Chapter 13](../learn-philosophy/13-kant.md); the free-will debate in [Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md).

In the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) Kant reverses the order: our awareness of the moral law is a "fact of reason," and it *reveals* our freedom. His example is a small drama:

> Ask him, however—if his sovereign ordered him, on pain of the same immediate execution, to bear false witness against an honourable man, whom the prince might wish to destroy under a plausible pretext, would he consider it possible in that case to overcome his love of life, however great it may be. He would perhaps not venture to affirm whether he would do so or not, but he must unhesitatingly admit that it is possible to do so. He judges, therefore, that he can do a certain thing because he is conscious that he ought, and he recognizes that he is free—a fact which but for the moral law he would never have known.
>
> — Kant, *Critique of Practical Reason*, Book I, ch. 1, Ak. 5:30 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

The same man, Kant notes just before, would certainly resist his lust if a gallows stood outside the brothel. Self-interest can overcome inclination. But only the moral law shows that he could resist even the love of life. This passage is a classic source for the principle that "ought implies can."

The *Critique of Practical Reason* closes with the sentence later carved on Kant's memorial plaque in Königsberg:

> Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and awe, the oftener and the more steadily we reflect on them: the starry heavens above and the moral law within.
>
> — Kant, *Critique of Practical Reason*, Conclusion, Ak. 5:161 (tr. T. K. Abbott)

## The murderer at the door

In 1797 the French-Swiss liberal Benjamin Constant published an essay arguing that a duty to tell the truth, taken unconditionally, "would make all society impossible," and he gave an example: a "German philosopher" had claimed that it would be a crime to lie to a murderer who asks whether your friend, whom he is pursuing, is hiding in your house. Constant's own view: a duty to tell the truth is owed only to those who have a right to the truth, and "no man has a right to a truth that injures others."

Kant replied the same year in "On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives." Kant admits, in a comic footnote, "I hereby admit that I have really said this in some place which I cannot now recollect." No one has found where. Then he doubles down. A lie, even to a murderer, wrongs not the murderer (whose right it does not violate) but humanity in general, since it undermines the credibility of declarations on which all rights founded on contract depend. He also offers a consequentialist-sounding argument: you cannot know what your lie will cause. Suppose your friend has slipped out of the back door; your lie sends the murderer away, straight into him. Then "you might with justice be accused as the cause of his death." The essay's conclusion:

> To be truthful (honest) in all declarations is therefore a sacred unconditional command of reason, and not to be limited by any expediency.
>
> — Kant, "On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives," Ak. 8:427 (tr. T. K. Abbott, 1889, p. 363)

Most readers think Kant got this case wrong. The interesting question is *why*, and whether the theory forces his answer. Four positions:

1. **Bite the bullet.** Lying is always wrong; you may refuse to answer, stall, or slam the door, but not lie. Some contemporary absolutists accept this (and note that Kant's essay is narrower than often reported: refusal and silence are not lies).
2. **The theory permits the lie; Kant misapplied it.** Korsgaard ("The Right to Lie," 1986) argues that the maxim "lie to a murderer who has lied to me about his intentions" is universalizable: his plan depends on deceiving you, and universalizing your counter-deception does not undermine truthful assertion in normal conditions. FH is harder, so she distinguishes *ideal* theory, where FH sets the goal, from *nonideal* conditions, where evil has broken the reciprocity the ideal assumes.
3. **The essay is about right, not ethics.** Allen Wood (*Kantian Ethics*, 2008, ch. 14) notes that Kant's topic is "declarations" in a juridical sense, statements others may rely on, as in testimony. The essay then says the *legal* order cannot license lying, not that a decent person at the door has no ethical latitude.
4. **The case refutes Kant.** If a theory implies that you must help a murderer find his victim, so much the worse for the theory. This is a *reductio*, and it is the view of most consequentialists and many virtue ethicists.

The case matters because it tests something deeper than lying: whether morality consists of side constraints that hold whatever the outcome, or whether outcomes can in principle outweigh them. That is the fault line between this chapter and the next two.

## The great critiques

### Empty formalism (Hegel)

Hegel (*Philosophy of Right*, 1820, §135) charges that a purely formal test yields no content: "don't steal" is contradictory only if you already assume property, so the content must come from a community's concrete ethical life (*Sittlichkeit*). Kantians answer that FUL tests how you act *within* a social world, not how to generate one. Hegel's point resurfaces in MacIntyre ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)).

### Theology in disguise (Schopenhauer)

Schopenhauer, who admired Kant's metaphysics, went after his ethics with relish. He complained that philosophy had been resting too comfortably on Kant's invention:

> During more than half a century it has been lying comfortably on the restful cushion which Kant arranged for it—the cushion of the Categorical Imperative of Practical Reason.
>
> — Schopenhauer, *The Basis of Morality*, Part I, ch. 2 (tr. A. B. Bullock)

His substantive argument is that the very concept of an unconditional "ought" is borrowed from the Ten Commandments and makes no sense without a commanding authority:

> What ought to be done is therefore necessarily conditioned by punishment or reward; consequently, to use Kant's language, it is essentially and inevitably hypothetical, and never, as he maintains, categorical.
>
> — Schopenhauer, *The Basis of Morality*, Part II, ch. 2 (tr. A. B. Bullock)

Schopenhauer replaced duty with *compassion* (*Mitleid*) as the basis of morality, anticipating care ethics ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)). Elizabeth Anscombe made a similar historical diagnosis in 1958: the "moral ought" is a survival of a divine-law conception of ethics in a culture that has stopped believing in the lawgiver ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)). Nietzsche, with less patience, wrote that "the categorical imperative reeks of cruelty" ([Chapter 15](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md)).

### Impartiality and "one thought too many"

Williams's most famous criticism applies to Kantians and utilitarians alike. A man can save either his wife or a stranger from drowning. If he saves his wife only after checking that impartial morality permits favoring her, he has, in Williams's phrase, "one thought too many" ("Persons, Character and Morality," 1976, in *Moral Luck*, 1981). Some things should be done from love directly. The Herman–Baron reply: the husband need not consult the moral law at the scene, provided his love operates within limits he has already internalized.

### Duty and desolation: Maria von Herbert

In 1791 a young Austrian woman, Maria von Herbert, wrote to Kant. She had concealed an earlier relationship from her lover; when she told him the truth, as she believed Kant's ethics demanded, his love cooled, and she wrote that life had lost its purpose. Kant replied with a careful letter on truthfulness. She wrote again, more desolate; Kant passed the letters to a young friend as a cautionary example of an overwrought enthusiast. In 1803 she took her own life. Rae Langton ("Duty and Desolation," 1992) argues that Kant used the role of moral instructor to avoid the role of friend: the sharpest case of the gap between respecting a person's reason and caring for a person.

### Kant's prejudices

Kant held views about women, servants and non-European peoples flatly incompatible with his principle of equal dignity; his essays on race rank human groups. Charles Mills ("Kant's Untermenschen," 2005) argued that these views shape who counts as a full rational agent in his system. Pauline Kleingeld ("Kant's Second Thoughts on Race," 2007) argues that Kant revised them in the 1790s, when he condemned colonialism. If the theory's core is sound, its universalism is the best tool for criticizing its author.

### Animals

Because only rational beings are ends in themselves, Kant holds that we have no direct duties to animals; cruelty to them is wrong because it coarsens our feelings toward humans (*Lectures on Ethics*; *Metaphysics of Morals* §17). Korsgaard's *Fellow Creatures* (2018) argues instead that in valuing ourselves as ends we are committed to valuing the good of every being for whom things can be good or bad. See [Chapter 20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md).

## Kantians today

Kant's ethics is a living research program. Rawls recast the kingdom of ends as a choice of principles behind a veil of ignorance ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)); Korsgaard's *The Sources of Normativity* (1996) grounds obligation in the structure of reflective agency ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)); O'Neill, Herman, Thomas Hill Jr. and Baron develop Kant as a theory of judgment and character rather than a decision procedure; Arthur Ripstein's *Force and Freedom* (2009) rebuilds Kant's political philosophy around equal freedom; and Derek Parfit's *On What Matters* (2011) argues that a revised Kantian formula converges with contractualism and rule consequentialism, three teams "climbing the same mountain on different sides" ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)).

## Living it

Kant offers one of the few moral theories that gives you a test you can run in a few seconds, and his own account of what goes wrong when you fail it is a description of ordinary self-deception: the exception "just for this time only."

Try a **maxim audit** for a week. Each evening pick one choice you made that day that involved someone else (a request you refused, something you said about a colleague, a price you accepted, a small lie of convenience). Then:

1. **Write the maxim** honestly: "When C, I will do A in order to E." If you find yourself writing "When I'm really tired and the person is annoying…" you are already gerrymandering.
2. **Universalize it.** Imagine everyone acting on it. Does your purpose still work in that world? If not, you were relying on others' restraint.
3. **Run the humanity check.** Could the other person, knowing exactly what you were doing and why, have agreed to it? Not "would they have liked it," but "could they have consented," as O'Neill puts it. Deception and manipulation fail at once.
4. **Ask the Herbert question.** Did you treat someone *only* as a reasoner to be corrected, when they needed something else from you?

Then take up the imperfect duty of beneficence, easy to discharge in theory and neglect in practice. Pick one concrete end of another person to adopt as your own this week, and pursue it whether or not you enjoy it.

## Summary

- The good will alone is good without qualification, whatever its success ("like a jewel").
- Moral worth belongs to actions done *from* duty; the sorrowful philanthropist isolates the motive. Herman and Baron read duty as a limiting condition compatible with warm motives.
- Evaluation targets *maxims*. Hypothetical imperatives depend on ends you have; the categorical imperative binds unconditionally.
- **Universal Law**: contradiction in conception grounds perfect duties, contradiction in the will imperfect ones; Korsgaard's practical reading; false positives and negatives.
- **Humanity**: never treat rational nature merely as a means; O'Neill's "possible consent."
- Persons have *dignity*, not *price*: the root of human-dignity talk and side constraints.
- **Autonomy** versus heteronomy; the **kingdom of ends**; the disputed equivalence of the formulas.
- The murderer at the door: Kant held lying always wrong; Korsgaard and Wood argue the theory need not say so.
- Critiques: empty formalism (Hegel), disguised theology (Schopenhauer, Anscombe), "one thought too many" (Williams), the Herbert letters (Langton), Kant's prejudices (Mills, Kleingeld), animals (Korsgaard).

### Dialogue 1 — On lying to the murderer at the door

**Benjamin Constant:** A man with a knife is at your door. He asks whether your friend is hiding inside. She is. Do you tell him the truth?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Constant in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Of course not. I lie. Anyone who says otherwise has lost touch with what morality is for.

**Constant:** I agree with you. But my old opponent in Königsberg will want to know your principle. Is it "lie whenever lying produces better results"?

**You:** No, that would license far too much. Let's say: "Lie when someone has no right to the truth because they mean to use it to harm others."

**Constant:** That was my own formulation, almost word for word.

**Kant:** Then answer me this. You claim to know he is a murderer and that your lie will save your friend. Suppose she has already left by the back door. Your lie sends him into the street, where he meets her. Who caused her death?

**You:** He did. He holds the knife.

**Kant:** And yet you, by your free deception, put him on that road. Had you told the truth, he would have searched the house, perhaps the neighbors would have come. I said in my essay that whoever lies "must answer for the consequences of it," however good his intentions.

**You:** That argument is odd coming from you. You're the one who says consequences don't determine moral worth. Now you're scaring me with consequences.

**Kant:** A fair point, and some of my readers think that part of my essay was weaker than the rest. My main claim is different: the liar does wrong "to mankind generally," since a lie "vitiates the source of justice" by making declarations untrustworthy.

**Christine Korsgaard (joining them):** May I try your own test, Professor? The murderer does not announce "I am here to kill." He deceives the householder about his purpose. Consider the maxim "When someone deceives me in order to use my answer for murder, I will deceive him to prevent it." Universalize it. Does the practice of truthful assertion collapse?

**You:** No. Ordinary conversation goes on as before. Only murderers who lie about their aims get lied to, and they still don't know they're being lied to, so the maxim works.

**Korsgaard:** So the lie passes the Formula of Universal Law. The Formula of Humanity is harder, which is why I distinguish ideal theory from nonideal conditions, in which someone has already broken the reciprocity respect assumes.

**You:** So I can lie, but I'm not entitled to feel completely clean about it?

**Korsgaard:** You are entitled to recognize that something regrettable has happened, which the evil person forced on you. That is different from guilt.

**Constant:** I am satisfied. Professor?

**Kant:** I will say only that "silence" and "refusal" are always open to you, and that you should not let one hard case teach you a habit of convenience.

**You:** That I can accept. The real danger isn't the murderer at the door. It's the hundred small lies I tell because I've persuaded myself every case is that case.

### Dialogue 2 — On the cold philanthropist

**Socrates:** Two friends visit you in hospital. The first comes because she loves you and cannot stay away. The second dislikes hospitals, feels nothing much, but comes because she believes it is her duty. Kant says only the second visit has moral worth. Is he right?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** That's backwards. The first friend is the better friend. I'd rather have her visit.

**Socrates:** So would I. But is "the better friend" the same as "the action with moral worth"? You prefer a sunny day to a rainy one, yet you do not praise the sun.

**You:** You're saying her love is like the weather, something that happens to her?

**Socrates:** That is Kant's suggestion. He says inclinations deserve "praise and encouragement, but not esteem." Esteem is for what is up to the person.

**You:** But love isn't simply weather. People cultivate love. They choose to stay close, to pay attention.

**Socrates:** Kant later agreed: we have a duty to cultivate sympathetic feeling. Now imagine a third friend, warm like the first, who would also come on a day her love had gone cold, because she knows it is right. Is she better or worse than the first?

**You:** Better, I suppose. Her visit doesn't depend on her mood.

**Socrates:** Then you have half-agreed with Kant. What is valuable is a will that would do the right thing even if inclination failed. Barbara Herman calls this duty acting as a "limiting condition": the motive of duty stands behind love, ready if love falters.

**You:** But then there's Williams's husband who checks the moral law before saving his wife. If my friend had to remind herself of her duty every time before visiting me, I'd feel hurt.

**Socrates:** Must she *consult* duty at the bedside, or only *be* someone in whom duty is settled?

**You:** The second. Duty in the background, love in the foreground.

**Socrates:** Then perhaps Kant's sorrowful philanthropist is not a model of how to live but a laboratory, like the physicist's vacuum, in which we see one force acting alone.

**You:** That I can accept, provided we don't confuse the lab with the house.

</details>
