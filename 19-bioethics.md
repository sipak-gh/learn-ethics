# Chapter 19 — Bioethics

In 1932 the United States Public Health Service began a study in Macon County, Alabama, of what syphilis does to the body when left untreated. About six hundred Black men, most of them poor sharecroppers, were enrolled; roughly two-thirds had the disease and the rest served as controls. They were told they were being treated for "bad blood," given free meals and burial insurance, and never told their diagnosis. When penicillin became the standard cure in the late 1940s, the study's managers worked to keep it from them. The study ran for forty years and ended in 1972 only because a Public Health Service employee, Peter Buxtun, took the story to a reporter. By then dozens of the men had died of syphilis, wives had been infected, and children had been born with the disease. It was run not by rogue sadists but by respectable physicians, reviewed for decades and written up in medical journals. Bioethics exists largely because of cases like this, which showed that good intentions, professional codes and scientific value do not protect the people on whom medicine is practiced. This chapter covers the ethics of medical research, the "four principles" framework and its critics, autonomy and paternalism, abortion, and death and dying. It applies tools from earlier chapters (Kant on persons in [Chapter 9](09-kant.md), natural law in [Chapter 4](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md) and double effect in [Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md), virtue in [Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md), care in [Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)) to cases where the stakes are bodies and lives. Previous: [Chapter 18 — Moral Psychology and Responsibility](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md). Next: [Chapter 20 — Animals and the Environment](20-animals-and-the-environment.md).

## What bioethics is, and why it arrived when it did

Medicine has always had an ethics: Greek oaths, the rules of the medieval hospital, nineteenth-century professional codes. What changed in the 1960s was that questions which had been the private business of doctors became public business, and philosophers, lawyers, theologians and patients began to ask them too.

Several things happened at once. Ventilators, dialysis, transplants and the pill created choices that had never existed; in 1962 *Life* magazine described the Seattle committee of laypeople who chose which patients would get the new dialysis machines, and therefore who would live, and the label "God committee" stuck. Research expanded enormously after the Second World War, and in 1966 the Harvard anesthesiologist Henry Beecher published "Ethics and Clinical Research" in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, describing twenty-two studies from leading journals in which patients had been put at risk without their knowledge. And the civil-rights and women's movements taught a generation to distrust professionals who claimed to know people's good better than they did.

Institutions followed: the Hastings Center (1969), the Kennedy Institute of Ethics at Georgetown (1971), national commissions, hospital ethics committees. The biologist Van Rensselaer Potter coined the word "bioethics" at about the same time, meaning something broader that included ecology, the subject of [Chapter 20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md). Bioethics is now one of the places where moral philosophy most directly shapes law and practice, and one where theory meets its limits, because a hospital committee has to decide by Thursday.

### The Hippocratic inheritance

The Hippocratic Oath is the oldest text in the field, though its date (the fifth or fourth century BCE is the usual guess) and authorship are unknown, and the classicist Ludwig Edelstein argued in 1943 that it expresses the views of a Pythagorean minority rather than standard Greek practice. After swearing by the gods and binding the student to his teacher, it turns to patients:

> I will follow that system of regimen which, according to my ability and judgment, I consider for the benefit of my patients, and abstain from whatever is deleterious and mischievous. I will give no deadly medicine to any one if asked, nor suggest any such counsel; and in like manner I will not give to a woman a pessary to produce abortion.
>
> — Hippocrates (attributed), "The Oath," tr. Francis Adams

Much of this chapter is already here. The Oath commits the physician to the patient's benefit and to avoiding harm; it forbids, in one sentence, both assisted death and abortion; and it promises confidentiality: "Whatever, in connection with my professional practice, or not in connection with it, I see or hear, in the life of men, which ought not to be spoken of abroad, I will not divulge." It is silent on one thing modern ethics regards as central, asking the patient what he or she wants. The physician decides what is "for the benefit of my patients" by his own "ability and judgment." 

The famous slogan "First, do no harm" is not in the Oath, and the Latin version, *primum non nocere*, seems to be a nineteenth-century coinage. Its ancestor is in the Hippocratic *Epidemics*, which tells the physician to

> have two special objects in view with regard to diseases, namely, to do good or to do no harm. The art consists in three things--the disease, the patient, and the physician.
>
> — Hippocrates, *Of the Epidemics*, Book I, tr. Francis Adams

"To do good or to do no harm" is the ancestor of what modern bioethicists call beneficence and nonmaleficence. And the patient is one of the three parts of the art, not just its object.

### The gentleman physician

The phrase "medical ethics" became standard through the English physician Thomas Percival, whose *Medical Ethics* (1803) became the model for the American Medical Association's first code of 1847. It shows how a humane physician of the period thought about telling patients the truth:

> A Physician should not be forward to make gloomy prognostications; because they savour of empiricism, by magnifying the importance of his services in the treatment or cure of the disease. But he should not fail on proper occasions to give to the friends of the patient timely notice of danger when it really occurs, and even to the patient himself, if absolutely necessary.
>
> — Thomas Percival, *Medical Ethics*, chapter II

Tell the family, and the patient only "if absolutely necessary," because "the Physician should be the minister of hope and comfort to the sick." Percival was not cynical; he believed that hope heals. The view lasted. In a survey published in 1961, about 90 percent of American physicians said they generally did not tell patients they had cancer; when the survey was repeated in 1979, 97 percent said they generally did. Few moral changes in any profession have been so fast. The change was from *paternalism*, in which the professional decides what is good for the patient, to *autonomy*, in which the patient decides with the professional's help. Most of this chapter is about where that move has gone too far, or not far enough.

## Research ethics: from Nuremberg to Belmont

Research raises a problem that treatment does not. When a doctor treats you, the aim is your good. When a researcher studies you, the aim is knowledge that may help others later. Research is therefore the clearest case of what Kant forbade, using a person as a means, and it is also indispensable: every treatment that works was once an experiment. Research ethics says under what conditions using people for knowledge is permissible.

### Nuremberg and Helsinki

The modern rules begin with a trial. In 1946–47 an American military tribunal at Nuremberg tried twenty-three Nazi physicians and officials for experiments in which concentration-camp prisoners were frozen, infected and mutilated; sixteen were convicted and seven hanged. The judgment set out ten principles for permissible experimentation, known since as the Nuremberg Code. Its first sentence is the most important:

> The voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential.
>
> — The Nuremberg Code (1947), principle 1

The Code goes on to require that the subject have legal capacity, be free from "force, fraud, deceit, duress," and understand what is being done; that the experiment be necessary and designed to avoid unnecessary suffering; and that the subject may withdraw at any time. The World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki (1964, often revised) adapted these rules for physicians, added review by an independent committee, and allowed consent by a guardian for subjects who cannot consent.

Many American researchers regarded Nuremberg as a code for barbarians. Beecher's article showed otherwise, and the following years supplied cases. At the Jewish Chronic Disease Hospital in Brooklyn in 1963, live cancer cells were injected into elderly patients who had not been told what they were. At the Willowbrook State School on Staten Island, from the 1950s to the early 1970s, children with intellectual disabilities were deliberately infected with hepatitis; the researchers argued that the children would probably have caught it anyway in the institution, and critics replied that this was an argument for cleaning the institution. Then, in 1972, came Tuskegee.

### The Belmont Report

Congress responded with the National Research Act of 1974, which required institutional review boards (IRBs) for federally funded research and created a National Commission to identify the basic principles of research ethics. Its short report, named for the conference center where it was drafted, is *The Belmont Report* (1979). It names three principles. The first is **respect for persons**:

> Respect for persons incorporates at least two ethical convictions: first, that individuals should be treated as autonomous agents, and second, that persons with diminished autonomy are entitled to protection.
>
> — *The Belmont Report* (1979), Part B

The second is **beneficence**, understood as an obligation with two parts: "(1) do not harm and (2) maximize possible benefits and minimize possible harms." The third is **justice**, which asks: "Who ought to receive the benefits of research and bear its burdens?" The burdens of research had fallen on the poor, the institutionalized and minorities, while the benefits went to those who could pay; Tuskegee is the report's example.

Each principle has an application: respect for persons requires *informed consent*, beneficence a systematic *assessment of risks and benefits*, and justice fairness in the *selection of subjects*. These became the basis of the US Common Rule (1991, revised 2018) and, through it, of research regulation in much of the world. The report does not say which principle wins when they conflict, or where the principles come from. Its authors thought that a strength: people from different traditions could agree on the principles while disagreeing about their foundations.

### What informed consent requires

Informed consent sounds simple and is not. The standard analysis requires that the person be *competent*, receive *disclosure* of the relevant information, *understand* it, decide *voluntarily*, and *authorize* the intervention. Each element hides difficulties. Competence comes in degrees and is specific to the decision: a patient with early dementia may be able to choose lunch but not surgery. Disclosure raises the question of how much; since *Canterbury v. Spence* (1972), many American courts ask what a reasonable patient, not a reasonable physician, would want to know.

Understanding is the weakest link. Consent forms for clinical trials now run to twenty or thirty pages, and many participants do not grasp that they have been randomized or that the study is not designed to benefit them. Paul Appelbaum and colleagues named this the *therapeutic misconception* (1982): subjects assume every element of a study was chosen for their benefit, because that is what doctors do. Voluntariness raises the question of payment. Paying a lot may be an "undue inducement" to accept risks; critics of that worry reply that we do not call high wages for dangerous jobs coercive, and that refusing to pay poor people well in order to protect them is itself paternalistic.

Behind all this stands Kant's formula of humanity, which you met in [Chapter 9](09-kant.md):

> So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals*, Second Section, tr. T. K. Abbott

Research subjects are always used as means. Consent, on a Kantian reading, makes sure they are not used *merely* as means, because they share the end for which they are used. Onora O'Neill has argued that the deeper test is whether a person *could* consent, since deception and coercion are wrong precisely in making real consent impossible: a deceived subject cannot adopt an end that has been hidden from her. That is why the Tuskegee men were wronged even in the years before penicillin.

### Equipoise and the ethics of trials

The randomized controlled trial poses a puzzle. A physician owes each patient the best treatment she knows of; how can she let a coin decide? The standard answer was given by Benjamin Freedman in 1987. A trial is ethical when there is *clinical equipoise*: honest, professional disagreement among expert clinicians about which treatment is better. The individual researcher may have a hunch, but as long as the expert community is genuinely divided, no patient in the trial is knowingly given an inferior treatment, and the trial exists precisely to settle the disagreement. Equipoise also explains when a trial must stop: once the evidence favors one arm, continuing to randomize would knowingly disadvantage patients.

Franklin Miller and Howard Brody (2003) argued that equipoise rests on a mistake: it tries to show that research is a kind of therapy, but it is not. Placebo-controlled trials and first-in-human trials on healthy volunteers cannot meet equipoise and are sometimes plainly justified. The right questions are whether the research is valuable, the risks reasonable, the subjects fairly chosen and consenting, and not exploited.

The debate has teeth. In 1997 Peter Lurie, Sidney Wolfe and Marcia Angell, editor of the *New England Journal of Medicine*, attacked placebo-controlled trials in Africa and Asia of a cheap short course of AZT to prevent mother-to-child HIV transmission, when a longer effective regimen was standard in rich countries. Defenders replied that the long regimen was unavailable where the trials took place, so the relevant comparison was with what those women would actually get: nothing. What standard of care trials in poor countries owe their subjects is still disputed, and it is a question of justice as much as of research.

### Cases that still teach

Three later cases show how research can go wrong even after Belmont.

**Henrietta Lacks.** In 1951 cells were taken from the cervical tumor of a young Black woman treated at Johns Hopkins, who died that year. Her cells, HeLa, were the first human cells to grow indefinitely in culture and became one of biology's most important tools. Neither she nor her family was asked, which was legal and normal then. Rebecca Skloot's book made the case famous in 2010 and raised questions Belmont had not clearly addressed: rights over tissue removed from one's body, over the data derived from it, and over its commercial value.

**Jesse Gelsinger.** In 1999 an eighteen-year-old with a manageable genetic liver disorder died four days after an injection in a gene therapy safety trial at the University of Pennsylvania. Investigators found that the consent process had understated known risks and that the lead investigator and the university had financial interests in the therapy. The case put conflicts of interest at the center of research ethics.

**He Jiankui.** In 2018 a Chinese researcher announced that he had used CRISPR to edit the embryos of twin girls to make them resistant to HIV. The edit was medically unnecessary, its effects unknown, the consent misleading, and the changes heritable. He was condemned worldwide and sentenced to three years in prison, and the case showed that international norms against heritable editing had no enforcement.

## Principlism and its critics

In 1979, the same year as the Belmont Report, Tom Beauchamp (who had worked for the National Commission) and James Childress published *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*. Now in its eighth edition, its framework, usually called *principlism*, is the closest thing bioethics has to an official theory. It proposes four principles:

1. **Respect for autonomy**: respect the decisions of competent people about their own lives and bodies.
2. **Nonmaleficence**: do not cause harm.
3. **Beneficence**: help; prevent and remove harm; weigh benefits against risks and costs.
4. **Justice**: distribute benefits, risks and costs fairly.

The principles are not derived from one theory. They come, the authors say, from the *common morality* shared by all morally serious people, so a utilitarian, a Kantian and a natural-law theorist can each accept them for their own reasons. They are *prima facie* in W. D. Ross's sense, binding unless outweighed. Conflicts are handled by *specification* (Henry Richardson's term), which makes a principle precise for a context, so that "respect autonomy" becomes "respect the informed refusal of treatment by a competent adult, even if it is likely to lead to death," and by *balancing* when specification runs out, all held together by reflective equilibrium ([Chapter 1](01-what-is-ethics.md)).

### The critics

**It is a checklist, not a theory.** K. Danner Clouser and Bernard Gert (1990) argued that the principles work like chapter headings: they remind you what to consider but give no method for resolving conflicts, and "balancing" names the problem rather than solving it. Two committees can apply them to the same case and reach opposite conclusions. Beauchamp and Childress replied that every theory needs judgment at the point of application.

**Cases come first.** Albert Jonsen and Stephen Toulmin, both of whom worked for the National Commission, reported in *The Abuse of Casuistry* (1988) that commissioners from very different backgrounds could usually agree on particular cases; the disagreement began when they tried to say why. They concluded that moral knowledge lives mainly in judgments about cases and analogies between them, as in the old case-based reasoning ("casuistry") of moral theology. That is how much clinical ethics is actually done: a new case is compared with paradigms (Quinlan, Cruzan, the Jehovah's Witness who refuses blood).

**Autonomy is too thin, or too Western.** Care ethicists ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)) argued that the principles picture the patient as an isolated chooser, when illness is lived in relationships of dependence, and developed accounts of *relational autonomy*. Critics from East Asian, African and Indigenous traditions noted that family-centered decision-making is not obviously a failure of respect. Defenders replied that a patient can autonomously choose to let her family decide; what she cannot be is overridden without her say. Virtue ethicists added that most failures in medicine are failures of character and attention, not of principle, and later editions of the book added a chapter on moral character.

The four principles remain the common vocabulary of clinical ethics; even their critics use them, if only as a list of things not to forget.

## Autonomy and paternalism

The most frequent conflict in clinical ethics is between respecting a patient's choice and protecting her from it. A Jehovah's Witness refuses a transfusion that would save her life; an elderly woman insists on going home to live alone after a fall. *Paternalism* is interfering with a person's liberty for her own good. The classic case against it is Mill's:

The classic answer is Mill's in *On Liberty*:

> That the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty*, chapter I

Mill's conclusion is uncompromising: "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign." His reasons are partly utilitarian (individuals usually know their own interests best; see [Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)) and partly about dignity: being managed for your own good is being treated as a child. Yet Mill allowed some interference, and how he did it shaped the modern debate:

Yet Mill himself allowed some interference, and the way he did it shaped the modern debate:

> If either a public officer or any one else saw a person attempting to cross a bridge which had been ascertained to be unsafe, and there were no time to warn him of his danger, they might seize him and turn him back, without any real infringement of his liberty; for liberty consists in doing what one desires, and he does not desire to fall into the river.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty*, chapter V

The man on the bridge does not know it is unsafe, so stopping him serves what he actually wants. Joel Feinberg called this *soft paternalism*: interfering with conduct that is not fully voluntary (through ignorance, compulsion, incompetence) in order to find out whether it is. *Hard paternalism* overrides an informed, voluntary choice. Mill accepts the first and rejects the second: once warned, the person should be "only warned of the danger; not forcibly prevented from exposing himself to it." So a confused patient refusing treatment may be stopped while her competence is assessed, but a competent Jehovah's Witness who understands the consequences must be allowed to refuse blood, as in most legal systems she is.

Mill also said something that complicates his own view. He would not enforce a contract by which a person sold himself into slavery:

> The principle of freedom cannot require that he should be free not to be free. It is not freedom, to be allowed to alienate his freedom.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty*, chapter V

If liberty is valued because it lets a person shape her future, a choice that ends all future choices is special. Gerald Dworkin ("Paternalism," 1972) built on this to argue that some paternalism is insurance that rational people would accept in advance against their own irreversible and poorly considered choices, as Ulysses had himself tied to the mast. Whether that extends to the choice to die is a question for the final sections.

## Abortion

No question in applied ethics has been argued so long with so little convergence, but the structure of the disagreement is far clearer than it was in 1970. Much public argument runs:

1. It is wrong to kill an innocent person.
2. A fetus is an innocent person from conception.
3. So abortion is wrong.

One can deny premise 2: a fetus, or at least an early one, is not a person in the morally relevant sense. Or one can grant it and deny that the conclusion follows, because the wrongness of killing does not include a duty to keep someone alive with your body. A third approach, from virtue ethics, questions the framing.

### The status of the fetus

**Conception views.** On the view common in religious traditions and in natural-law ethics ([Chapter 4](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md)), a human being with full moral status begins at conception. John Noonan (1970) argued that conception is the only non-arbitrary line, since every later one (quickening, viability, birth) depends on contingencies like medical technology. Critics cite two facts: until about fourteen days the embryo can split into twins, which makes it hard to say a single individual began at conception; and a large share of early embryos are lost unnoticed, a death toll nobody treats as a tragedy.

**Personhood views.** Mary Anne Warren (1973) distinguished being genetically human from being a member of the moral community. A person has some of five traits: consciousness (especially of pain), reasoning, self-motivated activity, communication and self-awareness. An early fetus has none, so it is not a person, and its potential cannot outweigh the rights of the woman who is one. Michael Tooley (1972) argued that a right to life requires the capacity to desire one's own continued existence as a subject of experiences.

Both prove too much: a newborn lacks most of Warren's traits and all of Tooley's requirement. Tooley accepted that infanticide is not the killing of a person; Warren later argued that infanticide is wrong because others value the infant, which most readers find too weak, since it makes killing a baby a wrong to someone else.

**Gradualist views.** Many hold that moral status grows during pregnancy, so early abortion is minor and late abortion serious. Sentience is the favored threshold. Major medical bodies have held that the brain connections needed for conscious pain are not in place before about twenty-four weeks; some researchers argue for earlier. The honest position is uncertainty.

### Marquis: a future like ours

Don Marquis (1989) argued that the personhood debate is stuck because both sides start from contested accounts of who is a person. He started instead from something nobody disputes: what makes it wrong to kill *us*? Killing deprives the victim of a future: all the experiences, projects and enjoyments that would otherwise have made up his life. That explains why killing is among the worst crimes, why the deaths of the young seem especially tragic, and why killing someone whose future would be only agony may not be wrong.

Then the argument closes. A typical fetus has a future like ours, the same kind of future that makes killing us wrong, so killing it is seriously wrong for the same reason. The argument needs neither fetal personhood nor any religious premise.

Critics have pressed three lines. *Contraception*: do sperm and egg also have a future of value? Marquis replied that before conception there is no single subject whose future it is. *Identity*: Jeff McMahan (*The Ethics of Killing*, 2002) argued that how much death harms a being depends on how closely it is psychologically connected to its future; an early fetus, with no mental life, is barely connected to the person it would become, so its "time-relative interest" in living is weak even though the loss is great. *Potentiality*: the argument relies on what the fetus would become, and critics doubt that potential value is actual value.

### Thomson: the violinist

Judith Jarvis Thomson's "A Defense of Abortion" (1971) granted, for argument's sake, that the fetus is a person from conception, and asked whether it follows that abortion is impermissible. Her method was one of the most discussed thought experiments in philosophy:

> You wake up in the morning and find yourself back to back in bed with an unconscious violinist.
>
> — Judith Jarvis Thomson, "A Defense of Abortion" (1971)

The story (paraphrased) continues: the violinist has a fatal kidney ailment, and the Society of Music Lovers, finding that you alone have the right blood type, kidnapped you and plugged his circulatory system into yours. Unplug now and he dies; in nine months he will have recovered. Must you stay? Most people think staying would be very kind, but not required, and that unplugging does him no injustice.

If so, the inference from "the fetus has a right to life" to "abortion is impermissible" fails. A right to life, Thomson argued, is a right not to be killed *unjustly*, not a right to whatever one needs to live, and it does not include a right to use another person's body.

To the objection that the case is like rape, while most pregnancies follow voluntary sex, she offered "people-seeds" that drift like pollen and may take root in your carpets: if you open your windows with the best mesh screens and one gets through anyway, does it have a right to your house? At most, she suggested, responsibility shows that some abortions are wrong, not all. She also distinguished the *Good Samaritan* from the *Minimally Decent Samaritan*: decency may require staying plugged in for an hour, but nine months of pregnancy and the risks of childbirth go far beyond what morality requires of anyone else for a stranger.

Her argument has limits. It gives a right to end the pregnancy, not to the death of a fetus that could survive outside the womb. And it does not make every abortion decent: a woman in her seventh month who aborted to avoid postponing a trip abroad would, Thomson said, act indecently.

The main objections: *killing and letting die* (unplugging lets the violinist die, while most abortions kill; Thomson replied that unplugging is killing too and still permissible); *special obligations* (a fetus is one's own child, not a stranger; Thomson held such obligations are assumed, not imposed by biology, while critics note that a father who did not want a child still owes support); and *responsibility* (in most pregnancies the parents did something that foreseeably created a dependent person, as the kidnap victim did not).

### Hursthouse: the question behind the question

Rosalind Hursthouse (1991) used abortion to show what virtue ethics ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)) adds. The standard debate focuses on fetal status and women's rights, but even where a woman has a right to an abortion, *exercising* it can be callous or light-minded, or wise and responsible. Rights say what others may not prevent, not what it is good to do.

The virtue ethicist starts from facts everyone accepts: pregnancy involves a new human life; parenthood is among the most important things in most lives; pregnancy and childbirth are hard. Treating an abortion as no more significant than a haircut fails to grasp the importance of life and parenthood; treating every abortion as murder ignores the real costs of pregnancy, poverty and unready parenthood. Hursthouse's claim is that "what would a good person do here, and in what spirit?" is a better question than the usual one, and it explains why many who think abortion should be legal also think it serious.

### The law, briefly

The moral and legal questions differ: one can think abortion often wrong and still oppose prohibition, for reasons about bodily integrity, the effects of bans, or the limits of what a pluralist state may impose. In the United States, *Roe v. Wade* (1973) recognized a constitutional right to abortion; *Planned Parenthood v. Casey* (1992) replaced its trimesters with an "undue burden" test; *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overruled both and returned the question to the states. Most European countries allow abortion on request in the first trimester; Ireland joined them after a 2018 referendum repealed its constitutional ban, with legislation following at the end of that year.

## Death and dying

### Refusing treatment

The most settled area of end-of-life ethics is the right of a competent patient to refuse treatment, including life-sustaining treatment. The hard cases involve patients who cannot speak. In 1976 the New Jersey Supreme Court allowed the parents of Karen Ann Quinlan, in a persistent vegetative state, to have her ventilator removed (she breathed on her own and lived nine more years). In *Cruzan* (1990) the US Supreme Court accepted that competent people may refuse unwanted treatment, including artificial nutrition, but let states require clear evidence of what an incompetent patient would have wanted. The Terri Schiavo case, which ended in 2005 after years of litigation and an intervention by Congress, showed how bitter such disputes become when nothing was written down.

For patients who cannot decide, *substituted judgment* asks what this patient would have chosen; *best interests* asks what is best for her when her wishes are unknown. Advance directives and health-care proxies make the first standard workable. Catholic moral theology contributed an older distinction: Pope Pius XII said in 1957 that one must use *ordinary* means to preserve life but not *extraordinary* ones that impose grave burdens or offer no reasonable hope. That established early that refusing treatment is not suicide or killing. The claim was soon challenged.

### Killing and letting die: Rachels

In 1973 the American Medical Association condemned "mercy killing" but left the stopping of extraordinary treatment, when death was imminent, to patients and families. James Rachels's three-page article of 1975 attacked this distinction between active and passive euthanasia.

His first argument concerned mercy. Infants born with Down syndrome and a correctable intestinal blockage were sometimes, with parental agreement, left unoperated, and died of dehydration over days. If the decision that the infant should die is right, a lethal injection would be more humane; the slow route was chosen only because it was "passive." Worse, whether the infant lived depended on whether it happened to have a blockage, an irrelevant ground.

His most famous argument is the pair of cases met in [Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md): Smith drowns his young cousin for an inheritance; Jones, intending the same, watches the boy slip and drown, ready to push his head back under. Smith killed; Jones let die. Is Jones any better? Rachels said no, and most readers agree. So the bare difference between killing and letting die makes no moral difference, and if passive euthanasia is permissible, so is active.

Philippa Foot ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)) defended the distinction as one between stringent negative duties (not to harm) and weaker positive duties (to aid). Shelly Kagan's "The Additive Fallacy" (1988) identified a problem with Rachels's method: a contrast pair shows that a factor makes no difference *in that context*, not that it never does, because moral factors interact. The difference may be swamped when both agents act from murderous greed and still matter when a doctor withdraws treatment at a patient's request. Many defenders of the medical distinction now rest it on rights instead: a patient may refuse invasion of her body, but has no right to demand a lethal act from someone else.

### Double effect

A second tool from natural-law ethics bears directly on medicine. [Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md) introduced the *doctrine of double effect*: Aquinas's distinction (*Summa Theologica* II-II, q. 64, a. 7) between what an act intends and what lies "beside the intention," together with his proportionality proviso and the four conditions that nineteenth-century manuals of moral theology (Jean-Pierre Gury's is the usual citation) distilled from it. Medicine is where the doctrine does most of its practical work.

In palliative care, a doctor may give a dying patient enough morphine to relieve severe pain even if it may hasten death, because she intends the relief and only foresees the hastening, and the relief is not achieved *by* the death. She may not give a dose intended to kill. (Properly adjusted opioids rarely shorten life at all, so the doctrine is needed less often than its fame suggests.) Traditional Catholic teaching likewise permits removing a cancerous pregnant uterus, which foreseeably kills the fetus, but not killing the fetus directly to save the mother.

Critics raise two main problems. The first is *closeness*. In Foot's example, a fat man is stuck in the mouth of a cave with potholers trapped behind him and water rising; they blow him up with dynamite, saying they intend only to clear the opening. If the doctrine allows that redescription it permits everything; if not, it needs a criterion of when an effect is too close to count as merely foreseen, and no one has produced a satisfactory one. The second concerns the *relevance of intention*: Thomson (1999) and T. M. Scanlon (*Moral Dimensions*, 2008) argued that intention bears on how we assess an agent, not on whether the act is permissible. A doctor who gives the right dose of morphine while secretly hoping the patient dies acts permissibly, though her attitude is bad.

### Assisted dying: the arguments

The idea of a chosen death for the incurably suffering is old. Thomas More's *Utopia* (1516) describes how the Utopians care for the sick:

> but when any is taken with a torturing and lingering pain, so that there is no hope either of recovery or ease, the priests and magistrates come and exhort them, that, since they are now unable to go on with the business of life, are become a burden to themselves and to all about them, and they have really out-lived themselves, they should no longer nourish such a rooted distemper, but choose rather to die since they cannot live but in much misery
>
> — Thomas More, *Utopia*, Book II, tr. Gilbert Burnet

More adds at once: "But no man is forced on this way of ending his life," and those who refuse are cared for as before, while anyone who dies "without the approbation of the priests and the senate" is thrown "into a ditch." More was a devout Catholic who died a martyr, and scholars disagree about how far *Utopia* endorses what it describes. But the passage holds the main elements of the modern debate: incurable suffering, voluntariness, official approval, and the phrase critics of assisted dying fear most, "a burden to themselves and to all about them."

Today *euthanasia* usually means a doctor directly ending a patient's life at her request; *physician-assisted suicide* (or *medical aid in dying*) means a doctor providing the means, usually a prescription, which the patient takes herself.

**The case for.** The first argument is from *autonomy*: a patient on a ventilator may choose the time of her death by refusing it, and it seems arbitrary to deny that choice to a cancer patient whom nothing is keeping alive. Ronald Dworkin's *Life's Dominion* (1993) added that people have *critical interests* in their lives having a shape that fits their values. In 1997 six philosophers (Dworkin, Thomas Nagel, Robert Nozick, John Rawls, T. M. Scanlon and Thomson) filed a "Philosophers' Brief" urging the US Supreme Court to recognize a constitutional right to assisted suicide; the Court declined (*Washington v. Glucksberg*) and left the matter to the states. The second argument is from *mercy*: some suffering cannot be relieved. The third is Rachels's: if letting die is permissible and killing is no worse in itself, active euthanasia is permissible when more merciful.

**The case against.** The first argument is from the *value of life*, which on many religious views and on natural law it is always wrong to attack directly, even one's own. Kant reached a similar conclusion without theology:

> He who contemplates suicide should ask himself whether his action can be consistent with the idea of humanity as an end in itself. If he destroys himself in order to escape from painful circumstances, he uses a person merely as a mean to maintain a tolerable condition up to the end of life.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals*, Second Section, tr. T. K. Abbott

David Velleman (1999) developed this. For Kant a person's value is *dignity*, different from the value of her interests: her interests matter because she does, so one cannot destroy the person for the sake of her interests, "to maintain a tolerable condition." Yet Velleman allowed that when illness destroys the capacities on which dignity rests, as in severe dementia, ending one's life may be consistent with respect for the person. Other Kantians argue that a rational choice to die when dignity is threatened honors humanity.

The second argument concerns *the integrity of medicine*. The Hippocratic physician swore to give "no deadly medicine to any one if asked," and Leon Kass has argued that patients must be able to trust that their doctor will never kill them. Defenders reply that trust depends on doctors not abandoning patients at the end.

The third and most discussed concerns *slippery slopes* and *vulnerability*. The logical version says that autonomy and mercy do not stop at terminal illness: autonomy applies to anyone competent and mercy to anyone suffering. The empirical version says that in practice safeguards erode and vulnerable people (the elderly, disabled, poor and depressed) will die who would otherwise have lived. Disability rights groups argue that the "suffering" that qualifies people often consists of dependence and loss of function, the daily life of many disabled people, and that offering death for it judges which lives are worth living. More's priests telling the sick they are a "burden … to all about them" is exactly what they fear.

Mill's slavery argument is sometimes added: if it is "not freedom, to be allowed to alienate his freedom," perhaps the choice to die is not protected by liberty either. Defenders reply that the dying patient is not trading away a free future; disease is already taking it.

### What has happened where it is legal

Oregon's Death with Dignity Act (1997) lets a physician prescribe lethal medication, after waiting periods, to a competent adult with a terminal illness expected to cause death within six months; about a dozen US jurisdictions now have similar laws. Oregon's annual reports show that patients most often cite loss of autonomy, loss of enjoyable activities and loss of dignity; pain, or fear of it, is cited much less often, and fear of being a burden is cited by a substantial share, often close to half. Defenders read this as evidence that the law serves people who want control; critics read the burden figures as the pressure they fear.

The Netherlands and Belgium legalized euthanasia in 2002 for unbearable suffering without prospect of improvement, terminal or not, including psychiatric suffering and, in Belgium since 2014, minors with capacity. Canada legalized medical assistance in dying in 2016 and in 2021 dropped the requirement that death be "reasonably foreseeable"; eligibility for mental illness alone has been repeatedly postponed. In both the Netherlands and Canada assisted deaths are now roughly one in twenty deaths. Defenders see real demand previously met badly or in secret; critics see widening criteria as proof of the logical slope, and point to Canadian cases of disabled people seeking death while saying they could not get adequate housing or support.

Whatever your view, a legal right to die means something different in a society that does not provide good palliative care, disability support and relief from poverty.

## Two further frontiers

Two topics deserve at least a mention. The first is *justice in health care*. Every health system rations, by price, waiting list or rule. Utilitarians ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)) favor maximizing health, measured in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), as England's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence does; critics note that QALYs favor the young and can count a year of life with a disability as worth less. Norman Daniels's *Just Health Care* (1985) extends Rawls ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)): disease restricts the range of opportunities open to a person, so fair equality of opportunity requires access to care, and because people reasonably disagree about priorities, it also requires a fair and public process for setting them. The ventilator-triage protocols of 2020, some withdrawn for discriminating against disabled patients, made these questions concrete. The second is *genetic enhancement*. Julian Savulescu defends "procreative beneficence," the duty to choose the embryo expected to have the best life; Adrienne Asch's expressivist objection holds that selecting against a disability expresses the judgment that people with it should not exist; Michael Sandel's *The Case Against Perfection* (2007) defends an "openness to the unbidden" in parenthood that engineering children would erode.

## Living it

You will face bioethical decisions, as a patient, as a family member, possibly as a research participant, and as a citizen. Some preparation helps.

1. **Write it down.** Fill in an advance directive and appoint a health-care proxy, whatever your age. Then talk with the person you appointed about your values, not just your wishes: what would make a life not worth prolonging for you, and what you would want done when it is unclear. Most of the cases in this chapter became tragedies because nobody had had that conversation.
2. **Decide about organ donation.** Register your choice and tell your family. The decision often falls to them, and they usually follow what they know you wanted.
3. **If you are asked to join a study,** ask four questions. What is the purpose? What are the risks, and what happens if I am harmed? Is any part of this designed to benefit me, or is it purely for knowledge? Can I withdraw at any time without penalty? If you leave unable to answer them, you have not given informed consent, whatever you signed.
4. **When you are the surrogate,** use the two standards in order. First ask what the patient would have chosen; only when you cannot know, ask what is best for her. Notice when you are deciding what you want instead.
5. **On abortion and assisted dying,** state the strongest version of the view you reject, in terms its holders would accept, and identify which premise you disagree with: moral status, bodily autonomy, the value of life, the limits of the state, the reliability of safeguards. Most public argument is between people who have never done this.

## Summary

- Bioethics became a field in the 1960s and 1970s, driven by new medical powers, research scandals (Tuskegee, Beecher's cases) and suspicion of professional authority. The Hippocratic Oath and Percival's *Medical Ethics* are beneficent but paternalist.
- Research ethics runs from the Nuremberg Code through Helsinki to Belmont's three principles (respect for persons, beneficence, justice) and their applications (informed consent, risk-benefit assessment, fair subject selection). The therapeutic misconception, payment and equipoise remain live issues.
- Principlism (autonomy, nonmaleficence, beneficence, justice) is the field's common framework; critics say it lacks a method for conflicts (Clouser and Gert), puts principles before cases (Jonsen and Toulmin), and neglects relationships and character.
- Mill rejects paternalism toward competent adults, accepts soft paternalism toward choices that are not fully informed or voluntary, and refuses to enforce a contract of slavery.
- On abortion, conception, personhood (Warren, Tooley) and gradualist views disagree about moral status; Marquis grounds the wrongness of killing in the loss of a "future like ours"; Thomson argues that a right to life is not a right to use another's body; Hursthouse asks how a virtuous person would think about pregnancy.
- Competent patients may refuse treatment; surrogates use substituted judgment, then best interests. Rachels denies that killing and letting die differ in themselves; Kagan's additive fallacy and rights-based views reply. Double effect faces the closeness problem and doubts about the relevance of intention.
- Assisted dying is defended from autonomy, mercy and consistency, and opposed from the value of life (Kant, Velleman), the integrity of medicine, and slippery slopes and vulnerability; evidence from Oregon, the Benelux countries and Canada is read differently by each side.

**You:** Of course. He's a person.

**Judith Jarvis Thomson:** Exactly. The question is what a person may be required to do for another with her body, and on that question, most of us are less demanding of men and strangers than of pregnant women.

### Dialogue 2 — On choosing to die

**John Stuart Mill:** Your grandmother, mentally clear and dying slowly of cancer, tells you she wants a doctor to help her die before she loses control of her body. Her country now permits it. Your aunt says it would be wrong and that the family should talk her out of it. Whose side are you on?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Mill in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Hers, I think. It's her life and her body. You'd agree: over herself, she's sovereign.

**John Stuart Mill:** I did write that. Is her choice fully informed and voluntary?

**You:** She knows her diagnosis. She's thought about it for months. Nobody's pressuring her. Well, she did say she doesn't want to be a burden.

**John Stuart Mill:** Does that trouble you?

**You:** A bit. If she's doing it for us, that's not quite her own choice.

**John Stuart Mill:** Is it not? People make many choices partly for others. A mother takes a worse job to be near her children. Why should a choice made out of love for one's family be less hers?

**You:** Because she might think she's a burden when we don't think so. Then she'd be dying on the basis of a mistake.

**John Stuart Mill:** Then the remedy is to correct the mistake: to tell her, and show her, that she is not. That is the man on the bridge. You may stop him long enough to tell him the bridge is unsafe. Once he knows, the decision is his.

**You:** And what if she's right that she's a burden? Caring for her is hard on my aunt.

**John Stuart Mill:** Then she has a true belief, and a reason that concerns others. Would you forbid her to act on it?

**You:** I'd want to make it less true first. Better care, help for my aunt. If the only reason she wants to die is that we failed to look after her properly, that's on us.

**John Stuart Mill:** That is a just answer, and it concerns what society owes her, not whether she may choose.

**You:** But wait. You also said a man can't sell himself into slavery, because freedom doesn't include the freedom to end your freedom. Isn't dying the ultimate case of that?

**John Stuart Mill:** That is the fair objection to me. What did the slave give up?

**You:** All his future choices, in exchange for something now.

**John Stuart Mill:** And what does your grandmother give up?

**You:** A few months, most of them in decline she doesn't want. Her future choices are disappearing anyway; the disease is taking them.

**John Stuart Mill:** Then perhaps her choice is the opposite of the slave's: an exercise of control over the end of a life whose freedom is being taken from her, not a sale of a free future. You must decide whether that distinction holds.

**You:** It holds for her. I'm less sure it holds for a depressed twenty-five-year-old with decades ahead.

**John Stuart Mill:** Nor am I. You have found where the principle of liberty needs judgment rather than slogans.

**You:** …

**You:** Yes. I read the forms, I'm an adult, nobody made me. The money is just payment for my time and risk.

**Henry Beecher:** How long were the forms?

**Henry Beecher:** What is the drug for, and what has it done in animals?

**You:** It's for some kind of inflammation. I don't remember the animal part.
