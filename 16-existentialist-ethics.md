# Chapter 16 — Existentialist Ethics

On the evening of 29 October 1945, Jean-Paul Sartre gave a public lecture at the Club Maintenant in Paris. The hall was too small; by the accounts of those present and of the next day's papers, the crowd pushed in from the street, chairs broke and people fainted in the heat. Paris had been liberated fourteen months earlier. Everyone had a story about what they had done, or failed to do, under the Occupation, and everyone wanted to hear the man whose philosophy said that there is no excuse. The lecture, published the next year as *L'existentialisme est un humanisme*, became the most widely read text of existentialism, and it was an answer to a moral charge: Communists said existentialism was bourgeois despair, Catholics said it was license, and both said it made ethics impossible. This chapter asks whether they were right. It follows the line from Kierkegaard, Dostoevsky and Nietzsche to Sartre, then to Simone de Beauvoir's *The Ethics of Ambiguity*, the most careful attempt to build an existentialist ethics, and to Albert Camus's ethics of revolt and limits. The metaphysics of freedom, bad faith and the absurd is treated in depth in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 10](../learn-nihilism/10-camus-and-the-absurd.md) and [Chapter 11](../learn-nihilism/11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md); here the question is narrower and harder: if there is no human nature and no God-given law, can anything be required of you? Previous: [Chapter 15 — Nietzsche and the Critique of Morality](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md). Next: [Chapter 17 — Metaethics](17-metaethics.md).

## The charge sheet

To understand existentialist ethics you have to see what it was accused of. In 1945 the charges were four, and Sartre lists them himself at the start of his lecture.

1. **Quietism.** If nothing has a meaning given in advance, why act at all? Existentialism invites you to sit in a café and contemplate the void.
2. **Morbidity.** It dwells on anguish, nausea and death, on "the ignoble," and ignores the smile of the child.
3. **Isolation.** Its starting point is the solitary subject, the Cartesian *cogito*, so it cannot reach solidarity with others.
4. **Arbitrariness.** If values are chosen, not discovered, then anything goes. Anyone can do whatever they like, and no one can condemn anyone.

The fourth charge is the philosophical one, and it is still alive. In its sharpest form it is a dilemma. Either existentialism gives you no reason to prefer one choice to another, in which case it is not an ethics but a mood; or it does give you reasons, in which case it has smuggled back in the objective values it said did not exist. Every serious existentialist ethics is an attempt to slip between the horns. Keep the dilemma in view: it will return at the end of each section.

Before Sartre, three nineteenth-century writers set up the problem. None of them called himself an existentialist, and two of them would have hated the label.

## Kierkegaard: the single individual against the universal

Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) published *Fear and Trembling* in 1843 under the pseudonym Johannes de silentio, "John of silence." Its subject is Genesis 22: God tells Abraham to take his son Isaac to Mount Moriah and sacrifice him. Abraham goes. At the last moment an angel stops his hand. For the church this was the father of faith; for Kierkegaard's narrator it is a horror that the church has turned into a Sunday sermon. If Abraham had been a modern man who heard such a voice and acted on it, we would call him a murderer or a madman.

The book's argument runs through three *Problemata*, of which the first is the famous one: is there a **teleological suspension of the ethical**? The ethical, for Kierkegaard's narrator (who is thinking of Hegel's *Sittlichkeit*, the ethical life of a community), is *the universal*: what applies to everyone, can be stated, can be justified to others. The tragic hero, such as Agamemnon sacrificing Iphigenia for the sake of the Greek fleet, stays inside the universal: he gives up one ethical duty (to his daughter) for a higher ethical duty (to the state), and everyone can weep with him because everyone understands. Abraham has no such justification. He acts as "the single individual" (*den Enkelte*) in an absolute relation to God that cannot be expressed to anyone. He cannot even explain himself to Sarah. That is why the pseudonym is "of silence."

The narrator admires Abraham and is terrified by him. The most startling passage is the portrait of the "knight of faith," who has made "the movement of infinity" and yet looks entirely ordinary:

> The first moment I lay my eyes on him I push him back, leaping back myself, I hold up my hands in amazement and say to myself: "Good Lord! that person? Is it really he--why, he looks like a parish-beadle!"
>
> — Kierkegaard, *Fear and Trembling*, "Preliminary Expectoration" (tr. Lee M. Hollander, *Selections from the Writings of Kierkegaard*, 1923)

The knight of faith "belongs altogether to this world, no philistine more so"; he takes pleasure in his dinner, the shops, the street. The point is that the highest inwardness has no outward sign. You cannot read a person's relation to the absolute off their behavior.

Why does this matter for ethics? Kant, Hegel and the utilitarians all tie moral seriousness to public justification. Kierkegaard suggests that the deepest commitments of a self may lie beyond that court. In his earlier *Either/Or* (1843) he dramatized the choice between the **aesthetic** life (lived for the interesting moment, ruled by boredom and its cure) and the **ethical** life (lived through commitment, marriage, duty, continuity); the judge who speaks for the ethical in Part II tells the aesthete that what matters is not choosing the right thing first but *choosing* at all, choosing oneself. That move, from *what* is chosen to *how* one chooses, is the seed of every existentialist ethics. For Kierkegaard's account of despair and the self, see [the Nihilism course, Chapter 8](../learn-nihilism/08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md).

The danger is obvious: what distinguishes Abraham from the fanatic who hears a voice telling him to kill? The narrator does not dissolve it; he insists only that faith, if it exists, is this paradox. Sartre will take the danger and turn it around: since you can never be sure the voice is God's, the decision to treat it as God's is yours.

## Dostoevsky: "everything is lawful" and the returned ticket

The most quoted line in existentialist ethics is one that does not appear in the novel it is attributed to. Sartre, in his lecture, says that Dostoevsky once wrote "If God did not exist, everything would be permitted," and calls it the starting point of existentialism. *The Brothers Karamazov* (1880) contains no such sentence. It contains an idea, attributed to Ivan Karamazov and repeated by several characters in several forms. Its first statement comes in a sneer from the seminarian Rakitin:

> And did you hear his stupid theory just now: if there's no immortality of the soul, then there's no virtue, and everything is lawful.
>
> — Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book II, ch. 7 (tr. Constance Garnett)

Later Smerdyakov, the servant who murders old Karamazov, tells Ivan he learned "all things are lawful" from him. The novel does not *assert* the slogan; it tests it, and Ivan's theory, put into practice by someone else, destroys him. The famous "If God does not exist, everything is permitted" is a later compression (it has circulated in that form since at least the early twentieth century) of a thesis the novel examines and arguably refutes.

The more important existentialist text in the novel is the chapter "Rebellion." Ivan tells his brother Alyosha, a novice monk, a series of true stories, taken from newspapers, about cruelty to children. A general sets his hounds on a serf boy in front of the boy's mother. Then Ivan asks whether any future harmony, any heavenly reconciliation in which the mother embraces the torturer, could be worth this:

> I would rather remain with my unavenged suffering and unsatisfied indignation, *even if I were wrong*. Besides, too high a price is asked for harmony; it's beyond our means to pay so much to enter on it. And so I hasten to give back my entrance ticket, and if I am an honest man I am bound to give it back as soon as possible. And that I am doing. It's not God that I don't accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return Him the ticket.
>
> — Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov*, Book V, ch. 4 (tr. Constance Garnett)

This is not atheism. It is a moral protest against the very idea that suffering could be *justified* by an outcome, even a divine one. Ivan's refusal is the refusal that Camus will call revolt: a "no" that already contains a "yes," because to say "this is not to be borne" is to affirm the worth of what is violated. Notice, too, that Ivan's protest cuts against consequentialist reasoning (compare [Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)): no aggregate of future happiness can buy the child's tears.

The second Dostoevsky text, *Notes from the Underground* (1864), attacks the rationalist utopia of his contemporary Nikolai Chernyshevsky, whose novel *What Is to Be Done?* (1863) imagined a society of enlightened egoists who would always do what reason showed to be in their interest. The Underground Man replies that people will smash the Crystal Palace just to prove they are not piano keys:

> One's own free unfettered choice, one's own caprice, however wild it may be, one's own fancy worked up at times to frenzy—is that very "most advantageous advantage" which we have overlooked, which comes under no classification and against which all systems and theories are continually being shattered to atoms.
>
> — Dostoevsky, *Notes from the Underground*, Part I, §7 (tr. Constance Garnett)

> Twice two makes four seems to me simply a piece of insolence. Twice two makes four is a pert coxcomb who stands with arms akimbo barring your path and spitting.
>
> — Dostoevsky, *Notes from the Underground*, Part I, §9 (tr. Constance Garnett)

The Underground Man is spiteful and miserable, a diagnosis rather than a hero. But his point survives his character: an ethics that treats persons as optimizers of a calculable good leaves out the one thing they most insist on, which is that the choice be *theirs*.

## Nietzsche: giving style to one's character

Nietzsche's critique of morality is the subject of [Chapter 15](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md), and the death of God is treated in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 6](../learn-nihilism/06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md). What existentialist ethics took from him was a positive program: after the death of the old values, the self becomes a work of art.

> To "give style" to one's character—that is a grand and a rare art! He who surveys all that his nature presents in its strength and in its weakness, and then fashions it into an ingenious plan, until everything appears artistic and rational, and even the weaknesses enchant the eye—exercises that admirable art.
>
> — Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §290 (tr. Thomas Common)

The ideal is not self-invention from nothing. It is the patient shaping of a given nature, "with long exercise and daily labour at the task," as the same section says. Nietzsche also gives the program its motto, borrowed from Pindar:

> We, however, *would seek to become what we are*,—the new, the unique, the incomparable, making laws for ourselves and creating ourselves!
>
> — Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §335 (tr. Thomas Common)

And he offers a test, the thought of eternal recurrence, which he calls "the heaviest burden":

> If that thought acquired power over thee, as thou art, it would transform thee, and perhaps crush thee; the question with regard to all and everything: "Dost thou want this once more, and also for innumerable times?" would lie as the heaviest burden upon thy activity!
>
> — Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom*, §341 (tr. Thomas Common)

Bernard Reginster (*The Affirmation of Life*, 2006) and others read the recurrence not as a cosmological claim but as a practical test of whether you affirm your life. It is a test of *form*, not content: it does not say what to do, only how to stand toward what you do. That is the strength and the weakness that existentialist ethics inherits.

## Sartre: from freedom to responsibility

Sartre's lecture compresses the ontology of *Being and Nothingness* (1943) into a handful of slogans. The central one is **existence precedes essence**. A paper knife (Sartre's example) is made by an artisan who has its concept in mind first: its essence precedes its existence. If there is no God who conceived human nature in advance, then for human beings the order is reversed. We exist first, turn up in the world, and only then define ourselves by what we do. That a human being is nothing other than what he makes of himself is, on Sartre's telling, the "first principle" of existentialism.

From this he derives three moods that critics had taken for morbidity, and he insists they are simply honesty.

- **Anguish** (*angoisse*): the awareness that in choosing, you are responsible not only for yourself. Sartre's example is Abraham. If an angel commands you, you must still decide that it is an angel and that it is speaking to you. A woman in a psychiatric hospital hears voices on the telephone giving her orders; how does she know they are God's? Whatever signs you receive, *you* interpret them.
- **Abandonment** (*délaissement*): there is no God and no human nature to fall back on, so no excuse. Here he cites the Dostoevsky misquotation. Sartre's gloss is the line most remembered from the lecture: man is "condemned to be free" (tr. Mairet). Condemned, because we did not create ourselves; free, because once thrown into the world we are responsible for everything we do.
- **Despair** (*désespoir*): we can count only on what lies within our will and within the probabilities that make our action feasible. Act without hope, not in the sense of gloom but in the sense of not relying on others, or on history, to complete what you do.

### The universalization argument

To answer the charge of arbitrariness Sartre makes a surprising move. In choosing myself, he says, I choose an image of the human being as I believe it ought to be. If I marry and have children, I commit not only myself but humanity to monogamy. Every act is a kind of legislation. Put as an argument:

1. In every choice, I affirm the value of what I choose; I cannot choose something as worse.
2. Nothing can be good for me without being good for all, because there is no human nature that would make my case special.
3. So in choosing for myself I choose for all: every choice proposes an image of humanity.
4. So I am responsible for everyone, and I should always ask: what would happen if everyone did as I am doing?

The echo of Kant ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)) is deliberate. But the argument is fragile. Premise 1 is a claim about the logic of choosing (to choose one option over another is to affirm its value), and it can be granted. Premise 2 is the weak one: it trades on the absence of a fixed human nature to conclude that there are no morally relevant differences between persons, which does not follow. And even if we grant the conclusion, it does not tell us *which* image of humanity to choose. Kant derived content from universalizability because he thought some maxims, universalized, contradict themselves. Sartre has no such test. He says only that a man who lies to himself by saying "not everyone does this" is in bad faith.

### The student's dilemma

The most famous passage in the lecture is a case. During the Occupation a former student came to Sartre with a choice. His brother had been killed in the German offensive of 1940; his father was inclined to collaborate. He lived alone with his mother, whose only consolation he was. He wanted to escape to England and join the Free French. If he went, his mother might be plunged into despair, and his action, routed through Spain, might amount to nothing. If he stayed, he would be certain to help one person but would do nothing for the war.

Sartre runs through the available theories. Christian doctrine says act with charity and love your neighbor; but which neighbor, the mother or the fighters? Kant says treat no one merely as a means; but whichever he chooses, he treats someone that way. Feelings? He can only discover how strong his feelings are by acting on them. Advice? In choosing whom to ask (a collaborationist priest, a resistant priest) he has already chosen the answer. Sartre's verdict: no rule of general morality can tell him what to do. He told the student that he was free, and so must choose, which is to say invent.

Three responses to the case are worth knowing.

- **The particularist reply.** Perhaps Sartre is right that no *rule* settles the case, but wrong that nothing does. A wise agent could perceive which consideration weighs most here, as Aristotle's *phronimos* would ([Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md); see also Jonathan Dancy, *Ethics Without Principles*, 2004). The absence of an algorithm is not the absence of a right answer.
- **Taylor's reply.** Charles Taylor ("What Is Human Agency?", 1977) argues that the student's predicament makes sense only because both options already matter to him in a way he did not choose: he "strongly evaluates" filial love and patriotic resistance. The dilemma is real because it is between values he finds, not values he invents. Radical choice without prior strong evaluation would not be agonizing; it would be a coin toss.
- **The tragic-dilemma reply.** Bernard Williams ("Ethical Consistency", 1965) argues that in real dilemmas whatever you do leaves a "remainder" of regret, and that this is a sign that both obligations were real. That is closer to Sartre's anguish than his critics allow, but it locates the anguish in the plurality of values, not in their absence.

### Bad faith and authenticity

Sartre's most original ethical concept comes from *Being and Nothingness*: **bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*). It is a lie to oneself about one's own freedom. The café waiter whose movements are a little too precise is playing at being a waiter, as if he were a waiter the way an inkwell is an inkwell. The woman on a first date who lets her hand rest in her companion's without noticing that she has let it, treats her hand as a thing, to postpone the moment of decision. In both cases the person flees the tension between **facticity** (the given facts of one's situation and past) and **transcendence** (the capacity to go beyond them). The ontology is laid out in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 11](../learn-nihilism/11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md).

The ethical question is whether bad faith is a *moral* fault or only a failure of lucidity. Sartre writes as if it were both. But then he owes an account of why lucidity matters. The answer that seems to be implicit is that **authenticity** (*authenticité*), lucid recognition of one's freedom and responsibility, is the one value that existentialism cannot reject without contradicting itself. A footnote in *Being and Nothingness* promises an ethics of "deliverance and salvation" achieved by a "radical conversion," and the book's final pages promise a future work on ethics. Sartre filled hundreds of pages of notebooks in 1947–48 trying to write it. They were published only after his death, as *Notebooks for an Ethics* (1983). In the 1960s he drafted a second, more Marxist ethics centered on human need (the 1964 Rome lecture, discussed by Robert Stone and Elizabeth Bowman and in Thomas Anderson's *Sartre's Two Ethics*). He never completed either. Sartre also came to regard the 1945 lecture as an oversimplification, and the reasons are visible in it.

### The arbitrariness objection, stated properly

Here is the objection in its strongest form. Suppose a lucid collaborator in 1942 says: "I know I am free. I am not hiding behind my nature or my orders. I choose a Europe ordered by the Reich, and I accept responsibility for that image of humanity." Has he satisfied every condition Sartre lays down? He is not in bad faith. He universalizes. He accepts anguish. Yet Sartre plainly thought him wrong, not merely different.

Sartre's answer in the lecture is that one can still judge: a person who hides behind determinism is a coward, and a person who claims his existence is necessary is a "scum" (*salaud*). But these are judgments of bad faith, not of the content of a choice. Sartre also asserts, without much argument, that once we see that values depend on us, we can will only freedom as the foundation of all values, and that we cannot will our own freedom without willing that of others. That is the right move, but he does not show why it follows. Beauvoir tried to.

## Beauvoir: the ethics of ambiguity

Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986) wrote *The Ethics of Ambiguity* in 1946 and published it in 1947, two years before *The Second Sex*. For decades it was read as an application of Sartre. Scholars since the 1990s, including Margaret Simons, Kate and Edward Fullbrook, Sonia Kruks, Debra Bergoffen and Kristana Arp, have shown that Beauvoir's thinking diverged early and sharply: she put situation, the body and other people at the center, where Sartre had the solitary for-itself. Beauvoir had already published *Pyrrhus and Cineas* (1944), a short ethical essay organized around a story from Plutarch: King Pyrrhus tells his adviser Cineas his plans to conquer Greece, then Africa, then the world. "And then?" asks Cineas. "Then I shall rest." "Why not rest now?" Beauvoir sides with Pyrrhus: a human life is a series of projects that go beyond the present, and rest is not their goal.

### Ambiguity as the human condition

The book opens with Montaigne's remark that the continuous work of our life is to build our death. Human beings, Beauvoir argues, are fundamentally **ambiguous**: we are free subjects and also objects for others; we are minds and also bodies; we are singular and also members of a collective; we live toward a future and are weighed down by a past. Philosophers have tried to escape this ambiguity by reducing us to one side, pure mind or pure matter. Ethics has done the same, imagining either a pure will or a set of fixed goods. Beauvoir's claim is that ethics begins from accepting ambiguity, not from fleeing it.

She agrees with Sartre that existence has no ready-made justification. Existentialism, she says, is a philosophy of ambiguity, and it is because no external value redeems our existence that we must give it value ourselves. But she adds that freedom is not a static property. Freedom is the movement by which we disclose a world and give it meaning, and it has to *will itself*. To will oneself free is to will the movement of freedom, and this turns out to require others.

### The argument from interdependence

The central argument of Part II can be stated like this.

1. My freedom exists only as projects that go beyond me toward a future and a world.
2. That future and that world are opened up and sustained only by other free beings, who take up, extend or refuse my projects. A book needs readers; a cause needs successors; a discovery needs a community that will carry it further.
3. If others are oppressed, ignorant or in misery, the field in which my freedom can act is narrowed: my projects lose their future.
4. So to will my freedom genuinely is to will a world in which others are free too.

Her own formulation is the one most often quoted from the book:

> To will oneself free is also to will others free.
>
> — Simone de Beauvoir, *The Ethics of Ambiguity*, Part II (tr. Bernard Frechtman)

The next sentences insist that this is "not an abstract formula" but points each person toward concrete action. This argument does what Sartre's universalization did not. It does not assume a human nature, and it does not claim that every choice legislates for all. It claims that freedom, understood as transcendence, is materially dependent on the freedom of others. It is closer to Hegel's account of recognition than to Kant's universalization. Kristana Arp calls the result a theory of "the bonds of freedom." The weak point is premise 2: a solitary hermit, or a tyrant who uses slaves to carry out his projects, might seem to have a future without others' freedom. Beauvoir's reply is that the tyrant's projects are frozen into objects; he wants obedience, not the free taking-up of his acts, and so he has cut himself off from the only thing that gives a project lasting meaning. Whether that reply works is one of the live questions in the scholarship.

### A gallery of evasions

Part II is also a typology of ways to fail at freedom, and it is the most vivid part of the book.

- The **sub-man** refuses to engage at all, out of fear. His world is flat and dull. He is easy prey for anyone who offers ready-made values.
- The **serious man** is Beauvoir's chief target. He subordinates his freedom to a value he treats as absolute and given: the Nation, the Party, the Army, the Family, Science, Revolution. Because the value seems to come from outside, he feels justified in sacrificing others to it, and he never has to ask whether he chose it. The serious man is the most dangerous, because he can be both sincere and ruthless.
- The **nihilist** is a disappointed serious man. Having discovered that his idol was not absolute, he concludes that nothing is worth anything. Beauvoir (anticipating many later critics of nihilism) sees this as a continuation of seriousness: he still thinks value must be *given* or it is nothing.
- The **adventurer** loves action for its own sake and uses causes as occasions for exploits. He is closer to genuine freedom, but indifferent to the ends and so often ends by serving tyrants.
- The **passionate man** devotes himself to a single object (a woman, a work, a collection) and makes it absolute, closing himself off from the rest of the world.

Against these, the genuinely free person commits to concrete ends while knowing that the ends get their worth from freedom and must serve it. The free person can be serious about a cause without being the serious man.

Her account of the child is underrated. The child lives in a "serious world" where values are given by adults like the sky. Adolescence is the crisis in which this world collapses and freedom appears. Some people never leave the child's world, and some are kept in it: Beauvoir mentions slaves and many women of her time, whose situation makes the experience of their freedom almost impossible. They are not in bad faith; they are **oppressed**. This is the crucial difference from early Sartre. For Beauvoir, freedom has material and social conditions, and ethics must address the situations that suppress it. That thought becomes the method of *The Second Sex* ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)).

### The antinomies of action

Part III takes on the hardest problem: political violence. Liberation struggles may require violence against oppressors, and even against bystanders. Beauvoir refuses both pacifist purity and revolutionary ruthlessness. She says that every action that treats people as means is a failure in some degree, that it can be justified only by the concrete freedom it opens up, and that it must be constantly re-justified, never placed beyond question by a sacred End. Ends do not justify means once and for all; they must be "lived" in the means. This is an ethics without a formula, and Beauvoir says so plainly: ambiguity means that we cannot calculate the right answer in advance, but must take the risk and accept responsibility for failure.

Beauvoir later judged the book too abstract (in her memoir *Force of Circumstance*, 1963) and regretted trying to define a morality independent of social context. Many readers now think she was too hard on it. It remains the closest thing existentialism has to a systematic ethics.

## Camus: revolt and its limits

Albert Camus (1913–1960) refused the label "existentialist," and he came at ethics from a different direction. His first question, in *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), was whether the absurd (the confrontation between our demand for meaning and a world that gives none) justifies suicide. His answer was no: one must live the absurd lucidly, in revolt, freedom and passion. The book ends with Sisyphus pushing his rock and the famous line that one must imagine him happy. That argument is examined in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 10](../learn-nihilism/10-camus-and-the-absurd.md).

*The Rebel* (1951) asks the political version: does the absurd justify murder? Camus saw his century as an age of "logical crime," in which murder was justified by philosophies: Nazi racial theory, and also the Marxist philosophy of history that excused the Soviet camps for the sake of a classless future. If nothing has meaning, then killing is a matter of indifference; but if history has a direction that only the Party understands, then killing for history is a duty. Camus wanted a third position.

He finds it in the act of rebellion. A slave who has obeyed orders all his life suddenly refuses a new one. In saying "no," he draws a line: thus far and no further. That line implies a "yes": there is something in him that is worth more than the master's command. And it is not only his own. The rebel acts in the name of something that he shares with other people, even with the master. Camus compresses this into a parody of Descartes:

> I rebel—therefore we exist.
>
> — Albert Camus, *The Rebel*, Part One (tr. Anthony Bower)

Revolt, then, is the source of a value that is neither given by God nor invented by fiat: human solidarity, the common dignity that the rebel discovers in the act of refusing its violation. Camus's argument:

1. Rebellion is a refusal of an intolerable violation.
2. To refuse a violation as intolerable is to affirm a limit, and a value that the limit protects.
3. That value is not peculiar to the rebel; he rebels also on behalf of others, and he would rebel at the same treatment of others.
4. So rebellion presupposes a shared human value.
5. A rebellion that murders or enslaves in the name of that value contradicts its own origin.
6. So genuine rebellion must respect limits: it may never make itself into a new tyranny.

The model of limited revolt in *The Rebel* is the group of Russian revolutionaries of 1905 around Ivan Kalyayev, whom Camus had dramatized in *The Just Assassins* (*Les Justes*, 1949). Kalyayev was to throw a bomb at the carriage of the Grand Duke Sergei. When he saw that the Grand Duke's young niece and nephew were in the carriage, he did not throw it. Days later he killed the Grand Duke alone, and accepted execution. Camus admired the "scrupulous assassins" for holding that if killing is ever necessary, it remains a crime, it must be limited, and the killer must pay with his own life. Ends do not sanctify means. This is close to Beauvoir's "antinomies of action," but Camus is more willing than Beauvoir to say *no* in advance to certain means.

Part Five calls this "thought at the meridian": the Mediterranean virtue of measure (*mesure*), opposed to history as an absolute. Critics mocked it as sunny moralism.

### The quarrel

In May 1952, Francis Jeanson reviewed *The Rebel* in Sartre's journal *Les Temps modernes*, accusing Camus of a "Red Cross morality" that refused to dirty its hands in history and so objectively served the bourgeoisie. Camus replied with a letter addressed to "Monsieur le Directeur" (Sartre), and Sartre answered in August with a personal demolition. The friendship ended. At issue were the Soviet camps and whether some moral limits hold regardless of historical consequences. Ronald Aronson argues that both men were partly right: Camus about the limits, Sartre about the need to act within history rather than above it. After Soviet tanks entered Budapest in 1956, Sartre broke with the French Communist Party. During the Algerian War, Camus's refusal to endorse the FLN's violence while also condemning French torture left him isolated. In Stockholm in 1957 he was reported as saying, in answer to a student, that if justice meant bombs in the trams of Algiers where his mother might be riding, he preferred his mother. The remark is often compressed as "I believe in justice, but I will defend my mother before justice"; the wording varies across reports, and it should be treated as a paraphrase.

## Objections to existentialist ethics, and replies

### 1. Empty formalism

Authenticity, lucidity, choosing oneself: these are forms, the objection goes, not contents. The lucid torturer and the lucid nurse are equally authentic. Hegel had made the same objection to Kant's categorical imperative; it applies with more force here.

*Reply.* Beauvoir's interdependence argument gives the form content: willing freedom means willing others' freedom, which rules out oppression. Camus's revolt gives content too: the limit discovered in rebellion. The more content these arguments provide, though, the more they look like a version of a familiar view: Kantian respect for persons, or a natural-rights theory. Existentialist ethics may be best understood as a way of *inhabiting* such commitments (choosing them, refusing to hide behind them), rather than as an alternative source for them.

### 2. The self is not a blank

Iris Murdoch, who had met Sartre and wrote the first English book on him (*Sartre: Romantic Rationalist*, 1953), became his sharpest critic. In "The Idea of Perfection" (1964, in *The Sovereignty of Good*), she attacks the picture of the moral agent as a pure will that surveys facts and leaps. Most of morality, she argues, happens before the leap, in how we *see*. Her example is a mother-in-law, M, who finds her son's wife, D, vulgar and juvenile, and who, by patient and just attention over years, comes to see D as refreshingly simple and spontaneous. Nothing outward changes. Yet M has done moral work. Choice is the tip of an iceberg of perception, and "attention" is the moral faculty that Sartre's model leaves out. Charles Taylor's strong evaluation, mentioned above, makes a parallel point. Merleau-Ponty, in the chapter "Freedom" of the *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945), had already argued that freedom is always situated in a field of habits and meanings that are not chosen at each instant.

*Reply.* Beauvoir accepts most of this. Her freedom is embodied, situated and historical. Murdoch's critique hits early Sartre; it hits Beauvoir much less.

### 3. Decisionism and the emotivist self

Alasdair MacIntyre (*After Virtue*, 1981, chs. 2–3) treats Sartre as a symptom of "emotivist" culture: a self with no given ends, whose choices can only be expressions of preference. He links this to the metaethical view discussed in [Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md), and argues that this self cannot sustain the traditions in which virtues make sense ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)).

*Reply.* Sartre's self is not simply preference-driven: anguish is precisely the felt weight of choosing *for others*. But the reply concedes that Sartre has no account of how practices and communities shape what we can choose, and MacIntyre's point stands against the 1945 lecture.

### 4. The social and the political

The Marxist objection: existentialism addresses an individual who is in fact shaped by class, colonialism and gender, and it blames the oppressed for their "bad faith." Frantz Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), used Sartrean tools against this blind spot, and Sartre himself spent his later decades trying to fold Marxism into existentialism (*Critique of Dialectical Reason*, 1960).

*Reply.* Beauvoir's distinction between bad faith and oppression is the answer, and it is already in the 1947 book.

## The legacy in contemporary ethics

Existentialist ethics did not survive as a school, but its questions moved into the mainstream:

- **Authenticity.** Charles Taylor's *The Ethics of Authenticity* (1991) defends the ideal against its degenerate forms (self-indulgent "being true to oneself") by arguing that authenticity only makes sense against "horizons of significance" one does not choose.
- **Identification and wholeheartedness.** Harry Frankfurt's account of caring and identifying with one's desires ([Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md)) is a non-existentialist version of choosing oneself.
- **Ground projects.** Bernard Williams's critique of impartial morality ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)) insists that a person's deepest projects give them reasons to live at all, and that morality cannot simply override them. Williams's Gauguin is a Kierkegaardian figure.
- **Practical identity.** Christine Korsgaard's constructivism ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)) begins from the reflective structure of human consciousness, the fact that we must choose and need reasons, and derives obligations from the identities we cannot help valuing. It reads at times like a Kantian answer to Sartre.
- **Feminist and decolonial ethics.** Beauvoir's ethics of situated freedom shaped *The Second Sex* and, through it, a whole tradition ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)); Fanon's work did the same for critical philosophy of race.

For the wider existentialist movement and its phenomenological roots, see [the Philosophy course, Chapter 19](../learn-philosophy/19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md).

## Living it

Existentialist ethics is supposed to be lived; it is unintelligible otherwise. Try the following practice for a month. It combines four tests from the texts.

1. **The serious-man audit (Beauvoir).** Write down the cause, institution or ideal you would most readily sacrifice other things to: a career, a party, a religion, a family's reputation, a theory. Then ask the two questions Beauvoir's serious man never asks: *When did I choose this?* and *Whose freedom have I been willing to spend on it?* You may keep the commitment. The point is to hold it as yours, which means you can be asked to justify it.
2. **The Abraham check (Kierkegaard, Sartre).** When you feel certain that something is demanded of you, by conscience, by a leader, by "the facts," notice that you have still decided to treat the demand as authoritative. Name the decision. Sartre's madwoman with the telephone is not only a joke about fanatics; it is a description of every certainty.
3. **The recurrence test (Nietzsche).** Pick one recurring practice of yours, such as the way you argue with your partner, or the way you spend the first hour of the day. Would you want it "once more, and also for innumerable times"? If not, what would you need to change for the answer to be yes?
4. **The limit (Camus).** For the cause you named in step 1, write down one thing you would not do for it, whatever the consequences. If you cannot find such a limit, Camus would say that you are not yet a rebel but a potential executioner. If you can, ask what value the limit protects, and whether you would grant the same limit to your opponents.

Keep a short weekly note. The aim is not to discover your "true self" (existentialists deny there is one waiting to be found) but to replace inherited excuses with commitments you can own.

## Summary

- Existentialist ethics answers the charge that a philosophy of radical freedom makes morality arbitrary. The deep problem is a dilemma: either it gives no reasons, or it smuggles in objective values.
- Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* separates the ethical as *the universal* from the single individual's absolute commitment, and moves the emphasis from *what* is chosen to *how*.
- "If God does not exist, everything is permitted" is not a line in *The Brothers Karamazov*. The novel tests the thesis "everything is lawful" and shows its catastrophe; Ivan's "returned ticket" is a moral protest against justifying suffering by outcomes.
- The Underground Man and Nietzsche's "give style to one's character" supply existentialism's defense of choice against calculation and its ideal of self-shaping.
- Sartre: existence precedes essence; anguish, abandonment and despair; every choice proposes an image of humanity; the student's dilemma shows the limits of moral rules; bad faith is self-deception about freedom. His universalization argument is weak, and he never finished his ethics.
- Beauvoir grounds an ethics in ambiguity: freedom depends on others' freedom, so to will oneself free is to will others free. The serious man, who idolizes a cause, is the chief danger; oppression is distinct from bad faith; ends must be justified in their means.
- Camus finds in revolt a shared value and a limit: "I rebel—therefore we exist." Revolution that murders in the name of the future betrays its origin. The Sartre–Camus quarrel of 1952 was about whether such limits hold within history.
- The main objections (formalism, the empty self, decisionism, social blindness) are serious against early Sartre and much weaker against Beauvoir. The legacy lives on in Taylor, Frankfurt, Williams, Korsgaard and feminist ethics.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether my freedom needs yours

**Simone de Beauvoir:** You told me that you value your freedom above everything. Do you value mine?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Beauvoir in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what she will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I respect it, but honestly I value mine more. Your freedom is your business.

**Beauvoir:** Then tell me what your freedom is for. What do you do with it?

**You:** I write music. I want to make something new.

**Beauvoir:** For whom?

**You:** For myself first. But yes, I want people to hear it.

**Beauvoir:** Free people, or people who have been ordered to applaud?

**You:** Free people, of course. Applause on command is worthless.

**Beauvoir:** So the value of your project depends on listeners who can refuse it. Your music needs a future in which others freely take it up, argue with it, perhaps surpass it. If they are hungry, illiterate or terrified, what happens to that future?

**You:** It shrinks. But a composer under a tyranny can still compose.

**Beauvoir:** He can, and he writes for a freer world he hopes for. That is my point. A project is a bet on other freedoms. When I wrote that to will oneself free is also to will others free, I did not mean a moral rule added to freedom from outside. I meant that freedom, understood properly, already reaches toward others.

**Sartre:** (*joining*) And in choosing, you also propose an image of humanity. If you choose to ignore the oppressed, you legislate that indifference for all.

**You:** That argument seems weaker, Jean-Paul. I am not legislating for anyone when I choose coffee over tea.

**Beauvoir:** He is too quick, I agree. My argument does not need every choice to be legislation. It needs only that your deepest projects depend on others being free.

**You:** But then a selfish person who has no projects that involve others escapes your conclusion.

**Beauvoir:** Show me such a person. Even the miser counts his gold in a world where gold means something because others value it. The question is whether he admits his dependence or lives as the serious man, as though his values fell from the sky.

**You:** So my freedom is not a possession but a relation.

**Beauvoir:** Yes. And that is why oppression is not someone else's problem. It narrows the world in which you are free.

**You:** Then the position I can defend is this: I cannot coherently value my own creative freedom while being indifferent to the conditions that make free reception possible. That falls short of an obligation to everyone, but it is more than self-interest.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether ethics can help the student

**Socrates:** Sartre's student must choose between his mother and the Free French. Sartre says no ethical theory can tell him what to do. Do you agree?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Yes. Both options are good, and nothing decides between them. He just has to choose.

**Socrates:** If nothing decides between them, why is the student suffering? Do you agonize over which of two identical coins to pick up?

**You:** No. He suffers because both matter a great deal.

**Socrates:** So they matter before he chooses. Did he choose to love his mother?

**You:** Not really. He found that he loved her.

**Charles Taylor:** That is my objection. The dilemma is agonizing only because he already strongly evaluates both: filial devotion and resistance to tyranny. These are not values he invents in the moment of choice.

**You:** But Sartre's point is that even granting both values, nothing weighs them for him.

**Socrates:** Nothing? Suppose he learned that his mother had other children nearby who would look after her. Would that change the weighing?

**You:** Yes, it would make leaving easier.

**Socrates:** And if he learned that the route through Spain was almost certainly fatal and useless?

**You:** Then he should stay.

**Socrates:** So considerations can weigh. What Sartre has shown is that no *rule* settles the case as described. Has he shown that there is no better and worse answer?

**You:** No. Perhaps only that the answer can't be read off a formula.

**Aristotle:** That is what I said of the mean: it is relative to us, and fixed as the person of practical wisdom would fix it. Not a formula, but not a coin toss.

**Sartre:** But who is the person of practical wisdom? He chose me as his adviser, knowing roughly what I would say.

**You:** That's a good point. Choosing the adviser is part of the choice.

**Socrates:** Then what follows? That advice is useless, or that he is responsible even for the advice he seeks?

**You:** The second. I'll revise my view: ethics can help the student. It can clarify what matters and what the facts are. But it can't take the responsibility for the final judgment away from him. Sartre was right about the responsibility and overstated the arbitrariness.

### Dialogue 3 — On ends and means

**Albert Camus:** You support a cause you believe is just. Suppose that its success required killing two children who happened to be in the way. Would you do it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Camus in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** No. Killing children is never acceptable.

**Camus:** Why not, if the cause would save thousands of children later?

**You:** Because… I'm not sure. A consequentialist would say I should.

**Camus:** Kalyayev did not throw the bomb when he saw the Grand Duke's niece and nephew in the carriage. His comrades argued with him. One said that the revolution was worth more than two children. What would you say to that comrade?

**You:** That a revolution willing to kill children for its success has already become what it is fighting.

**Camus:** Why has it?

**You:** Because the revolution began as a refusal of treating people as disposable. If it treats children as disposable, it contradicts its own reason for existing.

**Camus:** That is the logic of revolt. The slave who says "no" affirms a value that he shares with others. A revolt that kills in the name of that value destroys what it affirms.

**Sartre:** Fine words. But history does not wait for clean hands. To refuse all violence is to accept the violence that already exists. The colonized cannot afford your measure.

**You:** That's a fair point, Albert. Your limit could leave the oppressed defenseless.

**Camus:** I did not say no violence ever. I said that if one kills, one must know it is a crime, limit it strictly, and accept the price. Kalyayev went to the gallows. What I refuse is the murder justified in advance by a theory of history, so that the killer sleeps well.

**Beauvoir:** I would put it differently: means must be justified by the concrete freedom they open up, here and now, and re-justified continually. No sacred End can absolve them.

**You:** So there are three positions. Sartre's (history may require it), Beauvoir's (justify it concretely, never once and for all), and Camus's (some limits hold regardless).

**Camus:** And which do you hold?

**You:** Something between Beauvoir and you. Some acts, like killing children as a tactic, are off the table whatever the payoff. For others, there's no formula, but there's always the duty to justify them to the people they harm.

**Camus:** Then you have found a limit. Ask yourself whether you would grant it to your enemies too.

</details>
