# Chapter 8 — Hume and the Sentimentalists

Where does morality come from: the head or the heart? For most of the history covered so far, the answer was the head. Plato made reason the rightful ruler of the soul, the Stoics held that virtue is knowledge, and Aquinas held that natural law is grasped by practical reason. In early eighteenth-century Britain, a group of philosophers argued the opposite. Moral distinctions, they said, are not discovered by reason the way geometry is. We *feel* them, through a "moral sense" or through sympathy with the joys and pains of others. The most radical of these sentimentalists was a young Scot named David Hume, who declared that reason "is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions" and noticed that moralists keep sliding from "is" to "ought" without explaining how. His friend Adam Smith built on Hume's ideas a rich theory of conscience as an "impartial spectator" within. This chapter follows the argument from Hutcheson to Hume to Smith, asks whether sentimentalism can explain moral authority, and traces its descendants in contemporary philosophy and psychology. Previous: [Chapter 7 — Confucian, Buddhist, and Other Traditions](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md). Next: [Chapter 9 — Kant: Duty, Dignity, and the Moral Law](09-kant.md).

## The rationalists and their critics

To see what the sentimentalists were rejecting, start with their opponents. Late seventeenth- and early eighteenth-century **rationalists**, such as Ralph Cudworth and Samuel Clarke in England, held that moral truths are like mathematical truths: eternal, necessary, and knowable by reason alone. Clarke argued that there are "fitnesses" and "unfitnesses" in the relations between things. It is fitting that a creature should worship its creator and that equals should treat each other equally, just as it is fitting that the angles of a triangle sum to two right angles. To act wrongly is to act against the reason of things, which is a kind of absurdity. William Wollaston went further: every wrong act, he argued, is a kind of lie, because it treats things as other than they are. Stealing a horse "says" that the horse is yours.

The rationalists were reacting to a different threat: the egoism of Hobbes ([Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md)), which made morality a matter of self-interest and the sovereign's command. The rationalists wanted to show that morality is objective and binding independent of anyone's will, whether God's or the king's.

The scandal that provoked the sentimentalists came from the other direction. In 1714, the Dutch-born London physician Bernard Mandeville published an expanded edition of a satirical poem, *The Grumbling Hive*, with long prose commentaries, under the title *The Fable of the Bees: Or, Private Vices, Public Benefits*. The poem describes a prosperous hive full of fraud, luxury, and vanity, where "every part was full of vice, / Yet the whole mass a paradise." When the bees pray to be made honest and the gods grant their wish, the economy collapses: no more lawyers, no more luxury trades, no more war, and soon no more prosperity. Mandeville's prose went further. Moral virtue itself, he argued, is a confidence trick that clever politicians played on vain human beings, praising self-denial so that people would restrain themselves for the reward of flattery:

> the nearer we search into human nature, the more we shall be convinced, that the moral virtues are the political offspring which flattery begot upon pride.
>
> — Bernard Mandeville, *The Fable of the Bees*, "An Enquiry into the Origin of Moral Virtue"

Mandeville was condemned by grand juries and denounced from pulpits. But he raised a hard question. If all our apparently moral motives are disguised self-love, is there anything left of virtue? The rationalists answered by appeal to reason. The sentimentalists answered by appeal to feeling: there are, they said, genuinely benevolent affections in human nature, and a special sense by which we approve of them.

## Shaftesbury and Hutcheson: the moral sense

The third Earl of Shaftesbury, a pupil of John Locke, introduced the idea of a **moral sense** in his *Characteristics* (1711). Human beings, he argued, have "natural affections" for the good of others, and a reflective capacity to approve of these affections and disapprove of their opposites. Our approval of kindness is as natural and immediate as our pleasure in a well-proportioned building.

Francis Hutcheson (1694–1746), an Irish Presbyterian minister who became professor of moral philosophy at Glasgow, turned this suggestion into a systematic theory. His *Inquiry into the Original of Our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue* (1725) was written explicitly against Mandeville and, behind him, Hobbes. (Hutcheson's works are in the public domain but not on Project Gutenberg, so this section paraphrases them; the Liberty Fund editions are free online.)

Hutcheson's central argument is about the *phenomenology* of moral approval: what it is like. When we read about a generous act performed long ago, in a distant country, by someone who can do us no good, we approve of it. When we read of an act of treachery in similar circumstances, we disapprove. This approval cannot be self-interest, because the act has no effect on us. It is not reasoning either, because it is immediate: we do not first calculate and then approve. And it cannot be produced by bribes: offer someone a fortune to approve of a cruel act, and they may *say* they approve, but they cannot make themselves feel it. Hutcheson concluded that we have a distinct sense, analogous to the senses of sight and hearing, that perceives virtue and vice. Like the sense of beauty, it is a natural part of human constitution, given by God for our good.

What does the moral sense approve of? Hutcheson's answer was **benevolence**: the desire for the happiness of others. The more extensive the benevolence, the more we approve. This led him to a formula that would later become famous. In comparing actions, he wrote, that action is best which procures "the greatest happiness for the greatest numbers" (*Inquiry*, II.iii.8). Jeremy Bentham would make the phrase the slogan of utilitarianism ([Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)). But for Hutcheson it was not a theory of rightness built on calculation. It described what an affection-sensitive moral sense, working well, approves of.

Hutcheson's theory faced an obvious objection. Senses can differ from person to person and can be corrupted. If morality is just what our moral sense approves, then a person whose moral sense approves of cruelty is not *mistaken*, any more than a person who dislikes the taste of coffee is mistaken. Hutcheson answered that the moral sense is uniform in human nature, though it can be distorted by false beliefs, bad habits, and partial interests. But the objection would follow sentimentalism through all its later forms.

## Hume: reason and the passions

David Hume (1711–1776) published *A Treatise of Human Nature* in three books in 1739–1740, when he was in his late twenties. It "fell dead-born from the press," as he later put it, and he recast its arguments in two shorter *Enquiries*, the second of which, *An Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751), he considered "of all my writings... incomparably the best." Hume's aim was to apply the experimental method of Newton to the study of human nature. His ethics is part of that project: an explanation of morality as a natural phenomenon.

### Reason is the slave of the passions

Hume begins with a claim about motivation. Philosophers, he says, commonly speak of the combat between reason and passion and urge us to side with reason. Hume thinks this picture is confused. Reason, by itself, cannot move us to act at all. It does two things: it compares ideas (as in mathematics), and it discovers matters of fact and causal relations (as in science). Neither produces a motive. Learning that a fire is hot does not move you, unless you already care about not being burned. Reason can tell you *how* to get what you want, but the wanting comes from elsewhere: from passions, desires, and feelings.

> We speak not strictly and philosophically when we talk of the combat of passion and of reason. Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* 2.3.3.4

The phrase "ought only to be" is deliberately provocative. Hume is not saying that we *should* follow our impulses blindly. He is saying that the role of reason is to serve ends that it does not set. Reason is a navigator, not a captain.

A corollary follows: a passion cannot be unreasonable unless it rests on a false belief, about what exists or about the means to an end. If I want a fruit because I think it is delicious and you show me it is not, my desire disappears. But if no false belief is involved, reason has nothing to say:

> Where a passion is neither founded on false suppositions, nor chuses means insufficient for the end, the understanding can neither justify nor condemn it. It is not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger. It is not contrary to reason for me to chuse my total ruin, to prevent the least uneasiness of an Indian or person wholly unknown to me.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* 2.3.3.6

This is one of the most quoted passages in moral philosophy. Note that Hume is not saying preferring the world's destruction is *acceptable*. It is monstrous. His point is that what makes it monstrous is not a failure of reasoning. The person who prefers it has made no logical error and need hold no false belief. What is wrong with them is a defect of feeling.

### Morality is more properly felt

From the motivational claim Hume derives an argument about morality. It runs:

1. Moral judgments influence action: they "excite passions, and produce or prevent actions."
2. Reason alone cannot influence action.
3. Therefore moral judgments are not conclusions of reason alone.

> Morals excite passions, and produce or prevent actions. Reason of itself is utterly impotent in this particular. The rules of morality therefore, are not conclusions of our reason.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* 3.1.1.6

If moral distinctions are not discovered by reason, how are they discovered? Hume invites us to look for the vice in a clear case:

> Take any action allowed to be vicious: Wilful murder, for instance. Examine it in all lights, and see if you can find that matter of fact, or real existence, which you call vice. In which-ever way you take it, you find only certain passions, motives, volitions and thoughts. There is no other matter of fact in the case. The vice entirely escapes you, as long as you consider the object. You never can find it, till you turn your reflection into your own breast, and find a sentiment of disapprobation, which arises in you, towards this action. Here is a matter of fact; but it is the object of feeling, not of reason.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* 3.1.1.26

Vice and virtue, Hume concludes, are like colors, sounds, heat, and cold, which "according to modern philosophy, are not qualities in objects, but perceptions in the mind." The comparison is to Locke's secondary qualities. Redness is not "in" the apple the way its shape is; it is a power the apple has to produce a certain experience in normal observers. Likewise, viciousness is a power that murder has to produce a sentiment of disapproval in normal observers. Hence his summary: "Morality, therefore, is more properly felt than judged of."

In the later *Enquiry*, Hume softened the opposition. He granted that reason has a large role to play in morals: to determine the facts, trace consequences, and draw distinctions. But the final verdict still belongs to feeling:

> The one discovers objects as they really stand in nature, without addition and diminution: the other has a productive faculty, and gilding or staining all natural objects with the colours, borrowed from internal sentiment, raises in a manner a new creation.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals*, Appendix I

"The one" is reason; "the other" is taste or sentiment. The image of the mind "gilding or staining" the world with its own colors has become the standard metaphor for **projectivism**, the view that moral properties are projections of our attitudes onto the world ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)).

### Is and ought

At the end of the same section of the *Treatise*, Hume added what he called an "observation, which may, perhaps, be found of some importance." It became the most famous paragraph he ever wrote:

> In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remarked, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surprized to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not. This change is imperceptible; but is, however, of the last consequence. For as this ought, or ought not, expresses some new relation or affirmation, it is necessary that it should be observed and explained; and at the same time that a reason should be given, for what seems altogether inconceivable, how this new relation can be a deduction from others, which are entirely different from it.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* 3.1.1.27

The standard reading, sometimes called **Hume's Law**, is that no set of purely factual ("is") premises can logically entail a normative ("ought") conclusion. You cannot validly derive "you ought to help him" from "he is in pain" alone. You need a normative premise, such as "you ought to relieve pain when you can." [Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md) applied this as a tool for spotting hidden premises in moral arguments.

The principle has been hugely influential. It underlies G. E. Moore's attack on "naturalistic" ethics, the non-cognitivism of the twentieth century, and many critiques of appeals to "nature" ([Chapter 4](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md) and [Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)). But it is also contested in three ways.

- **What did Hume mean?** Alasdair MacIntyre argued in 1959 that Hume was not stating a logical gap at all. Hume himself moves from facts about human nature to moral conclusions throughout Book 3. On MacIntyre's reading, the paragraph merely asks for an *explanation* of how the transition works, and Hume's answer is that it works through the passions. The paragraph's conclusion supports this: the point is that vice and virtue are "not perceived by reason," not that no ought follows from any is.
- **Counterexamples.** John Searle (1964) argued that "Jones uttered the words 'I hereby promise to pay you five dollars'" leads, through facts about the institution of promising, to "Jones ought to pay Smith five dollars." Critics replied that the derivation smuggles in a normative premise about honoring institutional obligations. A. N. Prior produced logical puzzles ("Tea-drinking is common in England; therefore either tea-drinking is common in England or all New Zealanders ought to be shot") that show the principle needs careful formulation.
- **Thick concepts.** Philippa Foot and Bernard Williams pointed to concepts like "rude," "cruel," and "courageous," which seem to combine description and evaluation inextricably ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)). If "he is cruel" is a factual statement, it already carries an evaluation.

Even so, most philosophers accept a version of the gap: descriptive premises alone do not settle what to do. The question is what fills it, and Hume's answer, sentiment, is only one option.

## Hume's moral theory

Hume did not stop at saying that morality is a matter of feeling. He offered an account of *which* feelings, and why we share them.

### Sympathy

The central mechanism is **sympathy**. Hume did not mean pity but something more like what we now call emotional contagion or empathy: the tendency of feelings to pass from one person to another.

> As in strings equally wound up, the motion of one communicates itself to the rest; so all the affections readily pass from one person to another, and beget correspondent movements in every human creature.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* 3.3.1.7

When we see someone in pain, we form an idea of their pain, which becomes so lively that it turns into a faint version of the pain itself. When we contemplate a character trait that tends to make its possessor or others happy, sympathy makes us share that happiness, and this shared pleasure is the feeling of moral approval. Elsewhere Hume puts the point in an image: "the minds of men are mirrors to one another."

### Useful and agreeable

What do we approve of? In the second *Enquiry*, Hume surveys the qualities that people everywhere praise and finds that they fall into four classes: qualities **useful** to others (justice, benevolence), **useful** to oneself (prudence, industry), **agreeable** to others (wit, good manners, modesty), and **agreeable** to oneself (cheerfulness, tranquility). His conclusion is that "Personal Merit consists altogether in the possession of mental qualities, useful or agreeable to the person himself or to others."

Notice how wide this is. For Hume, virtue includes cleanliness, wit, and eloquence alongside justice and kindness. Modern readers trained on Kant may find this odd: surely being witty is not a *moral* virtue? Hume would reply that the line between moral and non-moral excellences is artificial, and that we in fact admire people for all of these. This is closer to the Greek idea of *aretē* ([Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md)) than to the modern idea of morality.

The theory also gave Hume a weapon. Qualities that are neither useful nor agreeable to anyone are not virtues, whatever religion says:

> Celibacy, fasting, penance, mortification, self-denial, humility, silence, solitude, and the whole train of monkish virtues; for what reason are they everywhere rejected by men of sense, but because they serve to no manner of purpose; neither advance a man's fortune in the world, nor render him a more valuable member of society; neither qualify him for the entertainment of company, nor increase his power of self-enjoyment?
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* IX.1

Hume goes on to say that we should "transfer them to the opposite column, and place them in the catalogue of vices." This was a deliberate provocation in an age when those virtues were still preached from every pulpit.

Against the egoists, Hume insisted that the theory requires only a little benevolence. There must be "some particle of the dove kneaded into our frame, along with the elements of the wolf and serpent." However weak, this particle is enough to produce a general preference for what benefits humanity, and moral distinctions arise from it.

### The general point of view

An obvious objection is that sympathy is biased. We sympathize more with those near to us, those like us, those we see. I feel more for my friend's minor troubles than for the sufferings of strangers far away. Yet our moral judgments do not vary this way: we do not think that a virtuous person who lived in ancient Greece was less virtuous than a friend because we feel less for them. How can a theory based on sympathy explain this?

Hume's answer is that we correct our sentiments by adopting a **general point of view**. Because each of us has a peculiar position, and our sentiments vary as our positions change, we could never talk to each other about character on reasonable terms if each judged only from where they stood. So, to avoid "continual contradictions," we fix on "some steady and general points of view," and judge characters by their effects on those who actually deal with the person. This is like the way we judge that a distant building is large, even though it looks small from here: we correct for perspective.

This move is important. It turns Hume's theory from a description of raw feeling into an account of **corrected** feeling, and so gives it a standard of correctness. A moral judgment is not just any sentiment I happen to have, but the sentiment I would have from the general point of view. Whether this is enough to answer the objection to Hutcheson (what if someone's corrected sentiments are monstrous?) is a question to which we will return.

### Justice: an artificial virtue

Hume divides the virtues into **natural** ones, like benevolence and generosity, which we would approve of in any society, and **artificial** ones, which depend on human conventions. The chief artificial virtue is **justice**, meaning respect for property and keeping promises.

Hume's argument is subtle. Benevolence cannot be the motive for justice, because justice sometimes requires returning money to a rich miser or a vicious person, which does no one any good. Nor can self-interest, directly, because unrestrained self-interest is what justice restrains. So justice must be an invention: a set of conventions that arose because they serve everyone's interest when generally followed. Hume illustrates the idea of a convention without a promise:

> Two men, who pull the oars of a boat, do it by an agreement or convention, though they have never given promises to each other.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* 3.2.2.10

The rowers fall into a rhythm because each sees that it pays to keep it, as long as the other does. Property, promising, and government grow the same way. Hume also shows what happens without such conventions:

> Your corn is ripe to-day; mine will be so tomorrow. It is profitable for us both, that I should labour with you to-day, and that you should aid me to-morrow. I have no kindness for you, and know you have as little for me. I will not, therefore, take any pains upon your account; and should I labour with you upon my own account, in expectation of a return, I know I should be disappointed, and that I should in vain depend upon your gratitude. Here then I leave you to labour alone: You treat me in the same manner. The seasons change; and both of us lose our harvests for want of mutual confidence and security.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* 3.2.5.8

This is the structure game theorists call the **assurance problem** or a trust game, and Hume's solution, the convention of promising, anticipates the analysis in David Lewis's *Convention* (1969) and modern work on the evolution of cooperation. [Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md) compares it with Hobbes' sovereign and the social contract.

Why, then, do we *morally approve* of justice, and not just find it convenient? Because of sympathy again. We see that justice serves the public good, and sympathy with the public makes us approve of just conduct even when it does not benefit us. The *Enquiry* adds a thought experiment: imagine a world of such abundance that everyone has more than enough of everything. In such a world, "the cautious, jealous virtue of justice would never once have been dreamed of." Justice exists because goods are scarce and human generosity is limited. This idea, the "circumstances of justice," was taken over by Rawls ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)).

### The sensible knave

[Chapter 1](01-what-is-ethics.md) asked, with Plato's ring of Gyges, why be moral if you could get away with injustice. Hume poses the same question in his own terms, in the figure of the **sensible knave**:

> And though it is allowed that, without a regard to property, no society could subsist; yet according to the imperfect way in which human affairs are conducted, a sensible knave, in particular incidents, may think that an act of iniquity or infidelity will make a considerable addition to his fortune, without causing any considerable breach in the social union and confederacy.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* IX.2

Hume's response is candid. If someone's heart does not rebel against the knave's reasoning, he admits, "it would be a little difficult to find any" answer that would satisfy him. For the rest of us, the feelings of integrity and the peace of a good conscience outweigh the gains of cheating, and knaves tend to be caught by their own cleverness. Notice that this is a sentimentalist answer. It does not show that knavery is *irrational*. It shows that most of us do not want to be knaves, and that is all there is to show. To Kant ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)) this would look like a failure: morality should bind the knave, too. To Hume, the demand that morality bind *everyone*, whatever they care about, is a philosopher's fantasy.

## Adam Smith: the impartial spectator

Adam Smith (1723–1790) is remembered today as the father of economics for *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). But he was professor of moral philosophy at Glasgow, a student of Hutcheson, and a close friend of Hume, and he regarded his first book, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), as the better of the two. He revised it until the year of his death. (Section numbers below follow the standard Glasgow edition. The Gutenberg text reproduces the 1777 edition, which lacks the material Smith added in 1790.)

### Sympathy as imagination

The book opens by rejecting the egoism of Hobbes and Mandeville:

> How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it, except the pleasure of seeing it.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* I.i.1.1

Smith's account of sympathy differs from Hume's. For Hume, feelings pass from person to person almost mechanically, like vibrations between strings. For Smith, sympathy is an act of **imagination**: we put ourselves in the other's situation and ask how we would feel.

> As we have no immediate experience of what other men feel, we can form no idea of the manner in which they are affected, but by conceiving what we ourselves should feel in the like situation. Though our brother is upon the rack, as long as we ourselves are at our ease, our senses will never inform us of what he suffers.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* I.i.1.2

This allows Smith to explain something Hume's model could not. We can sympathize with feelings the other person does not have. We feel embarrassment for someone who is behaving foolishly and does not know it, and we feel grief for a person who has lost their reason, though they are cheerful. We are responding not to their feelings but to their situation, as imagined.

### Propriety

Sympathy is the basis of moral judgment. When I imagine myself in your situation and find that I would feel what you feel, I approve of your feeling as **proper**. When your grief or anger is greater than what I can enter into, I find it excessive; when it is less, I find it cold. Moral approval, at its root, is this correspondence of sentiments.

The theory is two-sided. The spectator tries to enter into the feelings of the person principally concerned, and the person concerned, wanting sympathy, tries to lower their feelings to a pitch the spectator can share. Smith built a theory of the virtues on this mutual adjustment. The "amiable" virtues of sensitivity and humanity come from the spectator's effort; the "awful" virtues of self-command come from the sufferer's. Their ideal combination is the perfection of human nature:

> And hence it is, that to feel much for others and little for ourselves, that to restrain our selfish, and to indulge our benevolent affections, constitutes the perfection of human nature; and can alone produce among mankind that harmony of sentiments and passions in which consist their whole grace and propriety.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* I.i.5.5

The Stoic tone of the "awful" virtues is no accident; Smith admired the Stoics, and the [Stoicism course](../learn-stoicism/07-the-passions.md) treats their theory of the passions in detail.

### The mirror of society

How do we come to judge *ourselves*? Smith's answer is that we learn to see ourselves through others' eyes. A person raised entirely alone, he argues, could no more think about the propriety of his own conduct than about the beauty of his own face:

> Bring him into society, and he is immediately provided with the mirror which he wanted before. It is placed in the countenance and behaviour of those he lives with, which always mark when they enter into, and when they disapprove of his sentiments; and it is here that he first views the propriety and impropriety of his own passions, the beauty and deformity of his own mind.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.1.3

We first care about praise. But actual spectators are often ignorant, biased, or absent. So we gradually learn to imagine a spectator who is well-informed and impartial, and to judge ourselves as such a person would. This is the **impartial spectator**, "the man within the breast."

The crucial step is that the impartial spectator can overrule the real ones. We come to desire not just praise but *praiseworthiness*, and to dread not just blame but *blameworthiness*:

> On the contrary, if the man within condemns us, the loudest acclamations of mankind appear but as the noise of ignorance and folly, and whenever we assume the character of this impartial judge, we cannot avoid viewing our own actions with this distaste and dissatisfaction.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.2.32

Here Smith goes beyond Hume. Hume's general point of view was mainly a device for correcting judgments of others. Smith's impartial spectator is the source of **conscience**: an internalized judge that can stand against the crowd.

### The earthquake in China

Smith's most famous passage puts the impartial spectator to work. Suppose, he says, that the great empire of China were swallowed up by an earthquake, and consider how a humane European with no connection to China would react. He would express sorrow, reflect on the precariousness of life, perhaps speculate about the effects on trade, and then go back to his business and sleep soundly:

> If he was to lose his little finger to-morrow, he would not sleep to-night; but provided he never saw them, he will snore with the most profound security over the ruin of a hundred millions of his brethren, and the destruction of that immense multitude seems plainly an object less interesting to him, than this paultry misfortune of his own.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.3.4

Our *passive* feelings, Smith admits, are this selfish. But would such a man sacrifice a hundred million lives to save his finger? "Human nature startles with horror at the thought." What makes the difference, Smith says, is not "the soft power of humanity" or "that feeble spark of benevolence which Nature has lighted up in the human heart." It is something stronger:

> It is reason, principle, conscience, the inhabitant of the breast, the man within, the great judge and arbiter of our conduct. It is he, who, whenever we are about to act so as to affect the happiness of others, calls to us with a voice capable of astonishing the most presumptuous of our passions, that we are but one of the multitude, in no respect better than any other in it; ...
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.3.4

The passage is a sentimentalist's answer to the problem of distance that Mencius faced with the ox and the sheep ([Chapter 7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md)) and that Singer would raise with the drowning child ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)). Smith accepts that our spontaneous feelings are partial, and locates morality's corrective not in a separate faculty of pure reason but in an imaginative perspective we build out of social experience. Notice, though, that Smith calls the man within "reason, principle, conscience." The line between sentimentalism and rationalism is blurrier in Smith than in Hume.

### Justice and beneficence

Smith also offered a distinction that Kant and the contractualists would echo. **Beneficence** (kindness, generosity) is praiseworthy but cannot be demanded by force. **Justice** (not harming others) may be enforced, because its violation provokes resentment that the impartial spectator shares. And justice matters more:

> Justice, on the contrary, is the main pillar that upholds the whole edifice. If it is removed, the great, the immense fabric of human society, that fabric which to raise and support seems in this world, if I may say so, to have been the peculiar and darling care of Nature, must in a moment crumble into atoms.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* II.ii.3.4

Beneficence, by contrast, is "the ornament which embellishes, not the foundation which supports the building."

### The Adam Smith problem

Nineteenth-century German scholars posed what they called "das Adam Smith Problem": how could the same author write a book founded on sympathy and another, *The Wealth of Nations*, founded on self-interest? ("It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.") Most scholars now think the problem is spurious. Smith never claimed that sympathy is our *strongest* motive, only that it exists and grounds moral judgment. And the market he described works within a framework of justice that the moral sentiments support. In markets, we deal with strangers, and self-interest within the rules of justice does well enough; with family and friends, we expect more. As [Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md) noted, Smith was no egoist.

## Butler, conscience, and authority

Before turning to objections, note one rival to the sentimentalist picture that came from within the same circle. Joseph Butler, whose *Sermons* (1726) refuted psychological egoism ([Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md)), agreed with Hutcheson that human nature includes benevolence. But he insisted that conscience is not just one sense among others. It has **authority**: it claims to judge all our other motives.

> Had it strength, as it had right; had it power, as it had manifest authority, it would absolutely govern the world.
>
> — Joseph Butler, *Fifteen Sermons* II

Butler's distinction between strength and authority is exactly the point at which sentimentalism is most often attacked. A feeling has strength; it can be strong or weak. But what gives one feeling *authority* over another? If morality is a matter of sentiment, why should the moral sentiment win when it conflicts with greed?

## Objections and replies

### The authority problem

This is the deepest objection. If moral judgments express feelings, then:

- A person who lacks the relevant feelings, like the sensible knave or a psychopath, is not *wrong* in any sense that should move them. They just have a different constitution.
- Moral disagreement becomes a clash of tastes. When we say slavery is wrong and slaveholders said it was right, are we just reporting different feelings?
- Morality loses its categorical force. It binds only those who happen to care.

Kant built his ethics on this objection ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)). Moral requirements, he argued, must bind every rational being as such, whatever they feel, or they are not moral requirements at all.

Sentimentalists have several replies. First, Hume's general point of view and Smith's impartial spectator are **standards of correction**. Not every feeling counts; only feelings that would survive full information and impartiality. This turns sentimentalism into something like an **ideal observer theory**: what is right is what an informed, impartial, sympathetic observer would approve. Roderick Firth developed this view explicitly in 1952. It is not obvious that a slaveholder's approval would survive the impartial spectator.

Second, sentimentalists can bite the bullet on the knave. Perhaps there is no argument that will move someone with no moral feelings. But then, as Hume suggested, there is also no argument that will move someone who does not care about consistency to stop contradicting himself. All reasoning starts from something the reasoner already accepts.

Third, contemporary sentimentalists argue that the alternative is worse. If moral judgments are beliefs about mind-independent facts, it is mysterious how they motivate, and mysterious how we could know them ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)). Sentimentalism at least explains why morality matters to us.

### Instrumental reason and its critics

Hume's claim that reason only serves the passions has become the dominant view of practical rationality in economics and decision theory, often called the **instrumental** or **Humean** theory: rationality consists in taking effective means to whatever ends you have. Bernard Williams' influential "internal reasons" view (1979) holds that you have a reason to do something only if there is a "sound deliberative route" to it from your existing motivations.

Critics argue that this is too thin. Derek Parfit asked us to imagine a man with **Future-Tuesday Indifference**: he cares about his future pains, except those that will occur on future Tuesdays. He would choose agony next Tuesday over a mild pain on Wednesday. His preference involves no false belief and no mistake about means. On the Humean view, it is not irrational. Parfit thought it obviously is, and concluded that some ends are themselves irrational. Christine Korsgaard argued, in "Skepticism about Practical Reason" (1986), that the Humean cannot even account for instrumental reason itself: why *must* I take the means to my ends, if not because reason requires it? Hume's own example, preferring the destruction of the world to the scratching of one's finger, is for these critics a reductio of his view.

### Is empathy a good guide?

A more recent objection comes from psychology. If sympathy (empathy) is the basis of morality, morality inherits its biases. Empathy is strongest for people who are near, similar, attractive, and identifiable: the "identifiable victim effect" means that one named child in a well draws millions in donations while statistical thousands draw little. Paul Bloom, in *Against Empathy* (2016), argues that empathy is "a spotlight" that focuses on some and leaves others in darkness, and that good moral decisions need cool reasoning to correct it.

But this objection was anticipated. Smith's earthquake passage *is* the argument that our raw sympathies are parochial, and the impartial spectator is his remedy. Hume's general point of view serves the same purpose. The question is whether a corrective built out of sentiment can do the job, or whether, as Bloom and the Kantians suggest, at that point reason has taken over. Smith's own word for the man within, "reason," hints that the line is not as sharp as the debate assumes.

## The sentimentalist legacy

Sentimentalism shaped much of what came later.

- **Utilitarianism** inherited Hutcheson's formula and Hume's emphasis on utility, while dropping the moral sense in favor of calculation ([Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)).
- **Non-cognitivism** in twentieth-century metaethics, from A. J. Ayer's emotivism to Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism, claims Hume as its ancestor. Blackburn explicitly calls his view "projectivism," after Hume's image of gilding and staining ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)). The [Nihilism course](../learn-nihilism/12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) discusses Ayer and Mackie in more detail.
- **Care ethics** shares the sentimentalists' focus on feeling and relationship. Annette Baier called Hume "the women's moral theorist," and Michael Slote has developed a sentimentalist ethics of care based on empathy ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)).
- **Moral psychology** has largely vindicated Hume's picture of motivation. Jonathan Haidt's "social intuitionist model" holds that moral judgments are mostly quick intuitions, with reasoning coming afterward to justify them: "the emotional dog and its rational tail." Neuroscientific studies of patients with damage to emotional brain regions suggest that emotion is necessary for normal moral judgment and decision-making ([Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md)).

Whether those findings support Hume's *normative* view is another matter. That moral judgments are driven by feeling does not show that they *should* be, or that they are not responsive to reasons. That is exactly the gap between is and ought that Hume himself taught us to watch for.

## Living it

- **Find the vice.** Next time you feel strong moral disapproval of something, try Hume's test: describe the act purely in terms of facts. Where is the wrongness? Then ask whether locating it in your reaction makes it any less serious.
- **Consult the man within.** Before a decision you might be ashamed of, imagine a well-informed, fair-minded stranger watching. Not your friends, who will excuse you, or your critics, who will not. What would they feel?
- **Correct for distance.** Pick one distant tragedy in the news. Notice how little you feel compared with a minor problem of your own. Then ask what the impartial spectator would think of that ratio.
- **Audit your virtues.** Make a list of qualities you admire in others. Sort them by Hume's four categories: useful to others, useful to self, agreeable to others, agreeable to self. Do any fit none? What does that tell you?
- **Spot the slide.** In the next opinion piece you read, find a paragraph that moves from "is" to "ought." What hidden premise is doing the work?

## Summary

- **Rationalists** (Clarke, Wollaston) held that morality is discovered by reason like mathematics. **Mandeville** argued that virtue is the "offspring which flattery begot upon pride." The **sentimentalists** answered both by grounding morality in natural feeling.
- **Shaftesbury** and **Hutcheson** posited a **moral sense** that immediately approves of benevolence. Hutcheson's formula, "the greatest happiness for the greatest numbers," was later borrowed by the utilitarians.
- **Hume** held that **reason is the slave of the passions**: reason alone cannot motivate, so moral judgments, which motivate, cannot come from reason alone. Vice is found not in the act but in the **sentiment** of disapproval it causes. Morality is "more properly felt than judged of."
- **Hume's Law**: no "ought" follows from premises that contain only "is." Its meaning and scope remain debated (MacIntyre, Searle, thick concepts).
- Hume's theory: **sympathy** spreads feelings; we approve of qualities **useful or agreeable** to self or others; we correct bias by a **general point of view**. **Justice** is an **artificial virtue**, a convention that solves problems of trust. The **sensible knave** cannot be argued out of knavery.
- **Adam Smith** made sympathy imaginative, grounded judgment in **propriety**, and explained conscience as an internalized **impartial spectator**, the "man within the breast," who can overrule actual opinion and who rebukes our partiality, as in the **earthquake in China**.
- Objections: the **authority problem** (Butler, Kant), critiques of **instrumental reason** (Parfit, Korsgaard), and the **biases of empathy** (Bloom). Replies appeal to correction, ideal observers, and the limits of argument.
- Sentimentalism's descendants include utilitarianism, expressivism, care ethics, and the intuitionist moral psychology of Haidt.

### Dialogue 1 — Hume and the expense report

**Hume:** You look like a man who has found a loophole and is wondering whether to use it. Come, have some claret, and tell me.

**You:** …

*Your turn: describe a small dishonesty you could get away with, and the reasons you have for and against it.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** My company reimburses travel expenses with almost no checking. I could add a couple hundred dollars a month in meals I never ate. Nobody would ever know. The company makes billions. It wouldn't hurt anyone.

**Hume:** A sensible knave, then. I described you once. You admit honesty is a good general rule, but you think the wise man follows the rule and takes advantage of the exceptions.

**You:** Pretty much. Can you give me a reason not to?

**Hume:** I confess I may not be able to give you one that you would find convincing. If your heart does not rebel against the idea, reason alone will not move you. Reason can tell you the risk of being caught. It cannot tell you to care about integrity.

**You:** That's a strange thing for a philosopher to admit.

**Hume:** It is an honest thing. But let me ask you a question of fact. When you imagine filing the false report, what do you feel?

**You:** A little uncomfortable, I suppose. Like I'd be the kind of person I don't respect.

**Hume:** There. That discomfort is not an argument; it is the thing arguments are about. You disapprove of a character, and you see that you would have it. Now ask: is the discomfort founded on any false belief?

**You:** No. It's true that I'd be cheating.

**Hume:** Then reason cannot condemn it either. You are left weighing two feelings: the pleasure of two hundred dollars and the uneasiness of being a cheat. Which is greater?

**You:** Honestly? Right now, the money feels bigger.

**Hume:** Right now. But consider the long run. The knave who means to cheat with moderation finds that a tempting occasion arises, nature is frail, and he goes further than he meant. And consider the other side of the ledger: a quiet mind, a satisfactory review of your own conduct. These are pleasures too, and they last longer than a few dinners.

**You:** You're telling me honesty pays.

**Hume:** I am telling you that, for a person with your feelings, it probably does. For a person without them, I have little to say. But you are not such a person, or you would not be asking me.

### Dialogue 2 — Adam Smith and the news feed

**Smith:** You have been scrolling that little glass for an hour. What has it shown you?

**You:** …

*Your turn: describe a time when news of a distant disaster affected you less than a small problem of your own, and how you felt about that.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** A flood somewhere. Thousands dead, a million displaced. I read it, felt bad for a minute, and then spent the next half hour stressing about a rude comment my boss made in a meeting.

**Smith:** You will not be surprised to hear that I predicted this. A man of humanity hears of an earthquake in China, makes some melancholy reflections, and sleeps soundly. If he lost his little finger, he would lie awake.

**You:** So you're saying this is normal. That makes me feel a bit better.

**Smith:** Normal, yes. But I did not say it was proper. Imagine a spectator who knew everything about you and the flood, and cared no more for you than for any victim. What would he think of a man who spends half an hour on a rude comment and one minute on a million homeless?

**You:** He'd think I was self-absorbed.

**Smith:** He would think you were one of the multitude, and no better than any of them. That is the voice I call the man within. He does not ask you to feel the flood as you feel your boss's insult. Nature has not made us capable of that. He asks you to *act* as if you knew your proper size.

**You:** Meaning what, practically? I can't fix a flood.

**Smith:** You could send something to those who can. But more than that, you could let the knowledge correct your proportions. Next time the rude comment keeps you awake, remember that the impartial spectator would find your grievance very small, and that you would be ashamed to let it outweigh the flood if anyone could see the scales.

**You:** That sounds like it would make me feel guilty all the time.

**Smith:** Not guilty. Steady. The person who has learned to see himself as the spectator sees him is not tormented by every slight, because he knows how little it is, nor indifferent to the world's sorrows, because he knows how much they are. That is the harmony I meant: to feel much for others and little for ourselves.

**You:** And if I can't get there?

**Smith:** No one gets there entirely. But you have already begun. You noticed the disproportion and it bothered you. A man who felt nothing would not have mentioned it.

</details>
