# Chapter 11 — Contemporary Consequentialism

In 1972 a young Australian philosopher published an article arguing that most people in rich countries are doing something seriously wrong every time they buy clothes they do not need. Half a century later, Peter Singer's "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" is among the most assigned essays in philosophy, and it helped start a social movement that has moved billions of dollars, recruited thousands of careers, and suffered a spectacular scandal. This chapter follows the utilitarian tradition into the present. It covers how "utilitarianism" became the broader family called *consequentialism*; Singer's drowning child and the problem of demandingness; Bernard Williams's charge that consequentialism destroys integrity; Derek Parfit's puzzles about future people, including the Repugnant Conclusion; and the rise, achievements and troubles of effective altruism and longtermism. Throughout, the question is the same one Sidgwick left open: how much can impartial reason ask of a person who has a life of their own? Previous: [Chapter 10 — Classical Utilitarianism: Bentham, Mill, Sidgwick](10-classical-utilitarianism.md). Next: [Chapter 12 — Contractualism and Justice](12-contractualism-and-justice.md).

## From utilitarianism to consequentialism

The word *consequentialism* was coined by an enemy. Elizabeth Anscombe introduced it in "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) to name what she thought was the shared corruption of every English moral philosopher since Sidgwick: the view that, given good enough consequences, any act might be permissible ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)). Its defenders adopted the label because it marks the structural core of the theory while leaving the theory of value open.

The core is this: **the right act is determined by the value of outcomes.** Everything else is a parameter. Contemporary consequentialists vary the classical package along several dimensions:

- **Theory of the good.** Hedonism (pleasure), preference satisfaction (getting what you want, or would want if informed), or an objective list (knowledge, friendship, achievement, autonomy, alongside pleasure). Some include non-welfare goods, such as equality or beauty.
- **Actual or expected.** Most modern consequentialists judge acts by *expected* value: the value of each possible outcome weighted by its probability, given the agent's evidence. A doctor who prescribes the best-supported drug has acted rightly even if a rare allergy kills the patient.
- **Maximizing, satisficing or scalar.** Classical theory demands the best. Michael Slote proposed *satisficing* consequentialism (do enough good). Alastair Norcross proposed *scalar* consequentialism, which drops "right" and "wrong" altogether: acts are simply better or worse.
- **Act, rule or global.** Evaluate acts, rules, or (on "global" views) everything, including motives, character traits and institutions, by their consequences.
- **Agent-neutral or agent-relative.** Classical theory is agent-neutral: everyone has the same goal, the best world. Some philosophers allow agent-relative goals (for example, *my* not killing), but whether the result is still consequentialist is disputed.

Peter Railton's "sophisticated consequentialism" (1984) adds a point already in Mill and Sidgwick: the *criterion* of right action need not be the agent's *decision procedure*. Sidgwick put it plainly:

> For, as we have before observed, it is not necessary that the end which gives the criterion of rightness should always be the end at which we consciously aim: and if experience shows that the general happiness will be more satisfactorily attained if men frequently act from other motives than pure universal philanthropy, it is obvious that these other motives are reasonably to be preferred on Utilitarian principles.
>
> — Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, IV.i.1

Keep this passage in mind. It is the consequentialist's main reply to nearly every objection in this chapter.

## The drowning child

Singer wrote in November 1971, during the famine and refugee crisis in East Bengal (soon to become Bangladesh). Nine million refugees were in camps in India; rich governments had given a small fraction of what Britain was spending on the Concorde. His argument is short.

**The premises.** Suffering and death from lack of food, shelter and medical care are bad. And:

> if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it.
>
> — Peter Singer, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972)

**The example.** If you are walking past a shallow pond and see a small child drowning in it, you ought to wade in and pull the child out, even though your clothes will get muddy. The muddy clothes are insignificant compared to the child's death.

**The conclusion.** Most people in affluent countries could prevent serious suffering and death by giving money to effective relief organizations, without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance. So they ought to give, and failing to give is wrong, not merely less than admirable. The traditional line between duty and charity (giving as praiseworthy but optional, "supererogatory") should be redrawn.

Singer also offers a weaker, "moderate" principle: prevent very bad things if you can do so without sacrificing anything *morally significant*. Even this version, he argues, requires most of us to give far more than we do.

Notice two features. First, the strong principle does not mention happiness or utility. A Kantian who accepts a duty of beneficence ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)) or a Christian who takes the parable of the Good Samaritan seriously could accept it. The argument's power is that it starts from a judgment almost everyone shares (save the child) and asks what principle could explain it. Second, Singer addresses two relevant differences between the pond and the famine directly:

- **Distance.** The child in the pond is near; the famine victim is far. But physical distance, Singer argues, cannot be what matters morally. It may affect whether you *can* help, but modern communications and aid agencies have made it possible to help at a distance.
- **Numbers.** At the pond, only you can help. In a famine, millions could help but do not. But if five other adults stood by the pond doing nothing, you would not be excused.

### Smith's earthquake and Mrs. Jellyby

The puzzle about distance is old. Adam Smith imagined a "man of humanity in Europe" hearing that the whole empire of China had been swallowed up by an earthquake:

> If he was to lose his little finger to-morrow, he would not sleep to-night; but provided he never saw them, he will snore with the most profound security over the ruin of a hundred millions of his brethren, and the destruction of that immense multitude seems plainly an object less interesting to him, than this paultry misfortune of his own.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, III.3

Smith's point is not that this indifference is right. He goes on to ask whether such a man would *sacrifice* a hundred million lives to save his finger, and answers that "Human nature startles with horror at the thought." What restrains us, he says, is "the impartial spectator," the voice of conscience that tells us "that we are but one of the multitude, in no respect better than any other in it" ([Chapter 8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md)). But notice what Smith's spectator requires: that we not harm distant strangers for small gains. Singer asks whether it also requires us to *help* them at small cost. That gap between not harming and helping is the doing/allowing distinction ([Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md)), and consequentialism denies it any fundamental significance.

Victorian fiction offers the opposite warning. Dickens's Mrs. Jellyby, in *Bleak House*, is devoted to a colonial project in Africa while her own children fall down the stairs. Her eyes "had a curious habit of seeming to look a long way off. As if … they could see nothing nearer than Africa!" Dickens titled the chapter "Telescopic Philanthropy." Critics of distant giving still reach for her. The consequentialist answers that the problem with Mrs. Jellyby is not that she cares about distant people but that she neglects nearby ones she is better placed to help, and that her project is useless. Both are failures of consequentialist reasoning, not of impartiality.

### Replies to Singer

Philosophers have pushed back in several ways.

1. **Fair share.** Liam Murphy (*Moral Demands in Nonideal Theory*, 2000) argues that your duty is to do your *fair share*: what would be required of you if everyone did theirs. Others' failure to help does not double your burden. Critics reply that this gets the pond wrong: if two children are drowning and the other bystander walks away, you must save both.
2. **Justice, not charity.** Thomas Pogge (*World Poverty and Human Rights*, 2002) argues that the strongest duty of the affluent is *negative*: to stop supporting institutions (trade rules, the international borrowing and resource "privileges" of dictators) that harm the poor. On this view the drowning child is the wrong model; the rich are closer to the people who pushed the child in.
3. **Institutions, not individuals.** Many critics argue that poverty is a political problem requiring political solutions, and that individual giving can let governments off the hook.
4. **Unger's challenge.** Peter Unger's *Living High and Letting Die* (1996) argues the opposite: our intuitions that distinguish the pond from the famine are artifacts of *salience* and other psychological quirks, and once we see this, the demands are even greater than Singer said.

## Demandingness

Singer's strong principle has a startling implication. You must keep giving until the next dollar would cost you something of comparable importance, that is, roughly, until you would be as badly off as those you are helping. This is the **demandingness objection**: a moral theory that forbids nearly every personal project cannot be correct.

The classical utilitarians anticipated the worry. Mill denied that utilitarianism requires constant concern with the whole world:

> The multiplication of happiness is, according to the utilitarian ethics, the object of virtue: the occasions on which any person (except one in a thousand) has it in his power to do this on an extended scale, in other words, to be a public benefactor, are but exceptional; and on these occasions alone is he called on to consider public utility …
>
> — Mill, *Utilitarianism*, ch. 2

But Mill wrote before global aid agencies and online donations. Today, almost everyone with a bank account has the power to be a public benefactor on an extended scale. Sidgwick's reply was subtler: each person is "better able to provide for his own happiness than for that of other persons," and the energies of most people are drawn out by self-interest, so

> it would not under actual circumstances promote the universal happiness if each man were to concern himself with the happiness of others as much as with his own.
>
> — Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, IV.iii.3

That argument works when the others are your neighbors. It is much weaker when a few hundred dollars can provide antimalarial bed nets to people whose needs are far more urgent than yours.

Contemporary responses fall into four groups:

- **Accept it.** Shelly Kagan (*The Limits of Morality*, 1989) argues that morality really is extremely demanding and that the arguments for limits fail. Singer himself holds that the strong principle is correct but that a public standard should be set lower, since asking too much may produce less giving.
- **Build in a prerogative.** Samuel Scheffler (*The Rejection of Consequentialism*, 1982) proposed an *agent-centered prerogative*: you may give your own interests extra weight (some multiple of their impartial value), but you may not harm others to promote them.
- **Change the scope of morality.** Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" argues that a person who was as morally good as possible would be unattractive: no time for the oboe, for wit, for gourmet cooking. Perhaps moral reasons are not always the overriding ones.
- **Go scalar or satisficing.** If there is no sharp line between right and wrong, only better and worse, then the theory never "forbids" your hobbies; it just tells you that giving more would be better.

## Integrity: George and Jim

Bernard Williams's "A Critique of Utilitarianism" (1973) is the most influential attack on consequentialism of the last century. Its two cases are now standard.

**George** is an unemployed chemist with poor health and a family who are suffering from his unemployment. He is offered a job in a laboratory doing research into chemical and biological warfare. He is opposed to such weapons. But if he refuses, the job will go to a rival who will pursue the research with much greater zeal.

**Jim** is a botanist who arrives in a South American town where an army captain, Pedro, is about to execute twenty randomly chosen villagers as a reprisal for protests. Pedro offers Jim a guest's privilege: if Jim shoots one of the villagers himself, the other nineteen will go free. If he refuses, all twenty die. The villagers beg Jim to accept.

Williams's surprising concession is that in Jim's case utilitarianism probably gives the *right answer*: Jim should shoot. His complaint is about *how* utilitarianism reaches it. For the utilitarian the answer is obvious, and what Jim feels about it is merely another factor to be weighed. Williams presses two points:

1. **Negative responsibility.** Utilitarianism holds that you are as responsible for what you allow others to do as for what you do yourself. If George refuses and his rival builds the weapons, George is answerable for the result. This erases the moral significance of the fact that it is Pedro who kills, not Jim.
2. **Integrity.** A person's *ground projects*, the commitments that give his life meaning (George's pacifism, an artist's work, a love), are treated by utilitarianism as just one input among millions. Asking George to drop his convictions whenever the sum comes out the other way is, as Williams puts it, to alienate him from his own actions and from the convictions they spring from. It is an attack on his integrity in the literal sense: his being one, whole person.

Williams predicted that the day could not be far off when we would hear no more of utilitarianism. (It is still very much heard of.)

### The consequentialist replies

Railton's reply in "Alienation, Consequentialism, and the Demands of Morality" uses the Sidgwick passage quoted above. A *sophisticated* consequentialist is committed to leading an objectively consequentialist life but does not run every decision through calculation. Railton's Juan loves his wife Linda directly; he does not visit her because it maximizes utility. But he would be willing, if he found out that his way of life was doing great harm, to change it. Railton's claim is that this *counterfactual* commitment, standing in the background, is compatible with real love and real projects.

The question that remains is whether that background commitment is itself the problem. Williams's earlier "one thought too many" (discussed in [Chapter 9](09-kant.md)) applies here: a husband who saves his wife because she is his wife *and* because it is permissible to do so has a thought too many; a husband who keeps her in his life because the alternative would be worse for the world has one more.

## Parfit and the future

Derek Parfit (1942–2017) spent almost his entire career at All Souls College, Oxford, and was known for photographing the same few buildings in Venice and St. Petersburg, year after year. His *Reasons and Persons* (1984) reshaped several debates at once. Two are important here.

### Personal identity and the separateness of persons

Rawls objected that utilitarianism ignores "the separateness of persons" ([Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md), [Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)). Parfit argued, using cases of teleportation and gradual replacement of brain cells, that personal identity is not what matters in survival: what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness, which come in degrees. If he is right, the boundary between persons is less deep than Rawls assumes, and the boundaries between earlier and later stages of one person are deeper. Parfit said that accepting this view made him less concerned about his own death and more concerned about others. Whether it supports utilitarianism or only weakens one objection to it is disputed.

### The non-identity problem

Suppose a 14-year-old girl decides to have a child now rather than in ten years. The child will have a worse start in life than a child she would have had later. Has she wronged the child? The child she actually has could not have had a better life; had she waited, a *different* child would have been born. So the choice is worse for no one. Yet it seems wrong.

The same structure applies to policy. Suppose a society chooses a depletion policy, using up resources for present comfort, that makes life worse for people centuries from now. Because the choice changes who meets whom and when, it changes which people will exist. So the future people are not worse off than *they* would otherwise have been. This is Parfit's **non-identity problem**. It suggests that a morality built entirely on what is good or bad *for particular people* (a "person-affecting" view) cannot explain what is wrong with careless climate policy ([Chapter 20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md)). Impersonal views, on which outcomes are better or worse regardless of who exists in them, avoid the problem. But impersonal views lead to the next puzzle.

### The Repugnant Conclusion

Recall Sidgwick's verdict ([Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)): utilitarians should maximize total happiness, the number of people times their average happiness. Parfit drew out the implication. Start with a world of ten billion people with very high quality of life. Now imagine a vastly larger population whose lives are barely worth living, but whose numbers are so great that the total is bigger. On the total view the second world is better. Parfit called this the **Repugnant Conclusion**.

Abandoning the total view does not escape cleanly:

- **Averagism** (maximize average well-being) implies that it would be wrong to add a person whose life is wonderful, if the average is even higher elsewhere (including among long-dead Egyptians). Worse, it implies that adding people in agony could improve a world, if their agony is less than the existing average agony.
- **Person-affecting views** run into the non-identity problem.
- **The Mere Addition Paradox** shows that a series of individually plausible steps (adding happy people who do not affect anyone else; then equalizing at a higher total) leads to the Repugnant Conclusion anyway.

Later "impossibility theorems" (Gustaf Arrhenius and others) show that no theory of population can satisfy every condition most people find intuitive. Something must be given up. In 2021, twenty-nine philosophers published a joint statement in *Utilitas* arguing that avoiding the Repugnant Conclusion should no longer be treated as a necessary condition for an adequate theory. Parfit himself never accepted it.

## Effective altruism

Singer's argument stayed largely academic until the 2000s. Then three things converged: the charity evaluator GiveWell (founded 2007 by former hedge-fund analysts Holden Karnofsky and Elie Hassenfeld), Giving What We Can (founded 2009 in Oxford by Toby Ord and William MacAskill, whose members pledge at least 10 percent of their income), and online communities of people who wanted to use evidence to do the most good. The name **effective altruism** (EA) was chosen in 2011.

MacAskill defines EA as a project, not a theory: using evidence and careful reasoning to work out how to benefit others as much as possible, and acting on that. It is not identical to utilitarianism. It does not require that you maximize; it asks that, given whatever you choose to spend on others, you spend it well. Its tools include:

- **Cost-effectiveness.** The best health charities may be hundreds of times more effective per dollar than average ones. GiveWell's estimates in the early 2020s put the cost of saving a life through its top charities (antimalarial bed nets, seasonal malaria medicine) at several thousand dollars.
- **Cause prioritization by scale, neglectedness and tractability.** How big is the problem? How few resources already address it? How much progress can more resources make?
- **Career choice.** The organization 80,000 Hours (named for the hours in a working life) advises on high-impact careers. Its early advice included **earning to give**: taking a lucrative job in finance in order to donate much of the income.
- **Animals.** Because the numbers are enormous (tens of billions of land animals a year in factory farms), many effective altruists prioritize animal welfare, following Bentham ([Chapter 20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md)).

### Critiques

EA has attracted critics from the left, the right and the center:

1. **Systemic change.** Charity treats symptoms and leaves structures intact. EA's measurement tools favor interventions with countable outcomes, such as bed nets, over political change, whose effects cannot be measured in advance. (Iason Gabriel, "Effective Altruism and Its Critics," 2017.)
2. **Moral narrowness.** The essays in *The Good It Promises, the Harm It Does* (ed. Carol J. Adams, Alice Crary and Lori Gruen, 2023) argue that EA's quantitative frame cannot see injustice, solidarity or the knowledge of the communities it claims to help. This continues the care-ethics critique of impartialism ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)).
3. **Hubris and the ends justifying the means.** In November 2022, the cryptocurrency exchange FTX collapsed. Its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, had been the movement's most famous practitioner of earning to give. In 2023 he was convicted of fraud and in 2024 he was sentenced to twenty-five years in prison. EA leaders condemned the fraud, and MacAskill and others had written explicitly against violating common-sense moral constraints for the greater good. Critics asked whether a culture that treats expected-value reasoning as authoritative makes such rationalization easier. The episode is a live test of the Mill–Sidgwick–Hare thesis that good consequentialists should follow firm rules at the everyday level ([Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)).

Defenders reply that the core claims (some charities are far better than others; distant people count; giving more is better than giving less) survive all three critiques, and that the alternative to measurement is often just not checking.

## Longtermism

Sidgwick had already stated the principle that now drives the most controversial wing of effective altruism:

> It seems, however, clear that the time at which a man exists cannot affect the value of his happiness from a universal point of view; and that the interests of posterity must concern a Utilitarian as much as those of his contemporaries, except in so far as the effect of his actions on posterity--and even the existence of human beings to be affected--must necessarily be more uncertain.
>
> — Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, IV.i.2

Add two empirical premises: the future could be extremely long and populous (if humanity survives, there could be vastly more people in the future than have ever lived), and some present actions can affect that future, above all by reducing the risk of **existential catastrophe**, an event that destroys humanity or permanently curtails its potential. The conclusion is **longtermism**: positively influencing the long-term future is a key moral priority of our time (MacAskill's "weak" version) or *the* key priority (the "strong" version defended by Hilary Greaves and MacAskill).

The intellectual seed is in the last pages of *Reasons and Persons*. Parfit compares three outcomes: peace; a nuclear war that kills 99 percent of the world's population; and a war that kills 100 percent. Most people think the gap between peace and 99 percent is far larger than the gap between 99 and 100. Parfit argued the reverse: the last one percent also destroys the whole future of humanity. Toby Ord's *The Precipice* (2020) estimated the total risk of existential catastrophe in this century at about one in six, with unaligned artificial intelligence and engineered pandemics as the largest contributors ([Chapter 21](21-technology-and-ai-ethics.md)).

Objections:

- **Discounting.** Economists usually discount future welfare. A "pure" time discount (valuing future well-being less *because* it is later) is what Sidgwick denies, and the economist Frank Ramsey (1928) called the practice "ethically indefensible," arising from "the weakness of the imagination." But discounting for *uncertainty* and for the likelihood that future people will be richer is defensible, and it greatly weakens longtermist conclusions.
- **Cluelessness.** We cannot predict the long-run effects of our actions. James Lenman (2000) argued that their long-run effects swamp the short-run ones and are unknowable, so consequentialism gives no guidance at all. Longtermists reply that some interventions (preventing extinction) have predictable long-run effects.
- **Fanaticism.** Expected-value reasoning about tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs leads to what Nick Bostrom's 2009 paper calls "Pascal's mugging": a stranger promises you a trillion happy lives in return for your wallet; even if he is almost certainly lying, the expected value says pay. Most people think a decision theory with this result has gone wrong.
- **Population ethics.** Longtermism's urgency depends largely on the value of *future people existing at all*. On person-affecting views, preventing extinction is not a benefit to the never-born; the Repugnant Conclusion and its rivals return.
- **Priorities.** Critics argue that longtermism directs resources away from present suffering, especially among the global poor, and toward speculative risks that interest technologists.

## Living it

**A giving audit.** Look up your annual income and calculate 1 percent, 10 percent, and the amount that would leave you at the median income of your country. Then look up one top-rated charity's cost-effectiveness estimate (GiveWell publishes its reasoning) and translate each figure into outcomes. You are not being asked to give anything. You are being asked to notice which number you flinch at, and why. Is the flinch a reason, or just a feeling? Singer's argument runs through that question.

**The Jellyby check.** Choose one cause you support at a distance (a charity, a political movement, an online campaign). Then list three people near you who need something you could give this month (time, attention, practical help). Are the near and the far competing for the same resources? If so, how do you decide? Write down the principle you used.

**Williams's test.** Name one ground project of yours, something you would hate to give up and that partly makes you who you are. Imagine a credible argument that abandoning it would make the world measurably better. What would you do? Does your answer make you a worse person or a better one?

## Summary

- *Consequentialism* (Anscombe's coinage) holds that rightness depends on the value of outcomes; its variants differ on value theory, expected versus actual consequences, maximizing versus satisficing or scalar, and act versus rule versus global evaluation. Railton's sophisticated consequentialism separates criterion from decision procedure, as Sidgwick had.
- Singer's "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" argues from the drowning child to a duty to give to the global poor; distance and the number of potential helpers do not matter. Smith's "man of humanity" and Dickens's Mrs. Jellyby frame the old debate about distant and near concern.
- Replies: fair share (Murphy), negative duties of justice (Pogge), institutional change, and Unger's argument for even greater demands.
- The demandingness objection, answered by acceptance (Kagan), agent-centered prerogatives (Scheffler), limits on morality's authority (Wolf), or scalar views.
- Williams's George and Jim: negative responsibility and integrity. Railton replies that consequentialism need not alienate us from our commitments.
- Parfit: personal identity is not what matters; the non-identity problem undermines person-affecting views; the Repugnant Conclusion and the Mere Addition Paradox show there is no painless population ethics.
- Effective altruism uses evidence to do as much good as possible; critiques concern systemic change, moral narrowness and ends-justify-means reasoning (FTX).
- Longtermism follows Sidgwick's time-neutrality to prioritize the far future and existential risk (Parfit's three outcomes, Ord's one-in-six estimate); objections include discounting, cluelessness, fanaticism and population ethics.

**You:** Not as much as Pedro's. He's the one who set this up and who would do the killing.

**Williams:** The utilitarian disagrees. For him, what you allow is as much yours as what you do. That is negative responsibility.

**Railton (stepping in):** But the sophisticated consequentialist need not think about Jim's choice that way at all. He can say: people with a strong aversion to killing produce better worlds than people who calculate. Jim's horror is not a mere cost; it is part of a good character.

**Williams:** Then, Peter, in this case the good character tells Jim not to shoot, and the theory tells him to shoot. Which does he obey?

**Railton:** The theory, in this case. That is why it is the theory.

**You:** So the character is only there because it's useful. If I really cared about not killing, I'd care about it for its own sake.

**Williams:** Yes. The utilitarian asks you to have commitments and to hold them at arm's length. I think that is impossible, or a kind of self-alienation.

**You:** Then what does Jim do, in your view?

**Williams:** Probably shoot. But not because the sums are obvious. He does it as someone who is doing a terrible thing, and who must live with it afterward. The theory cannot see that remainder.

**You:** So you don't disagree about the act. You disagree about what kind of person makes the choice.

**Williams:** And whether moral philosophy should be in the business of making such choices easy.

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On whether more is better

**Parfit:** Imagine two possible futures. In A, ten billion people live wonderful lives. In Z, a hundred trillion people live lives barely worth living: muzak and potatoes, but no suffering. Which is better?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Parfit in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** A. Obviously. Z sounds dreary.

**Parfit:** Then you are not a total utilitarian, since Z contains more total well-being. Are you an averagist?

**You:** Maybe. It's the quality of lives that matters.

**Parfit:** Then compare A with A+: the same ten billion wonderful lives, plus a billion extra people, on a distant planet, whose lives are good but less wonderful. Their existence harms no one. Is A+ worse than A?

**You:** No. How could adding happy people who hurt no one make things worse?

**Parfit:** Yet the average in A+ is lower. So you are not an averagist either. Now compare A+ with B: the same total population, but equal, with everyone slightly better off on average than in A+. Is B better than A+?

**You:** Yes. More equal and better on average.

**Parfit:** Repeat these steps many times. Each one seemed an improvement. Where do you end?

**You:** At Z. That's the trick, isn't it.

**Parfit:** It is not a trick. It is an argument, and I could not find the flaw in it, though I tried for decades.

**Sidgwick (from the past):** I accepted the total view without seeing where it led. I confess I would have been troubled.

**You:** Maybe the mistake is in comparing worlds at all, rather than asking what we owe the people who will actually exist.

**Parfit:** That is a person-affecting view. Then tell me what is wrong with the depletion policy, which makes life worse for future people who would not have existed under any other policy.

**You:** I don't know. Maybe I have to accept something counterintuitive somewhere.

**Parfit:** That is the conclusion of the impossibility theorems. The question is only which counterintuitive thing you accept.
