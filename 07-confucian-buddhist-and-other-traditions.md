# Chapter 7 — Confucian, Buddhist, and Other Traditions

Around the same centuries that Socrates questioned Athenians in the marketplace, a failed civil servant from the small state of Lu was teaching a circle of students that the way to fix a disordered world was to get the rituals right. Further south and west, a prince who had left his palace was teaching that suffering comes from craving and can be ended. Neither had read Plato, and Plato had never heard of either. Yet both asked the questions this course has been asking: what a good person is, how one becomes one, and why it matters. This chapter surveys some of the most influential ethical traditions that grew up outside the Greek and Christian line. It covers Confucius on humaneness and ritual; the great Chinese debate between Mencius and Xunzi over whether human nature is good; the Mohists, who were consequentialists two thousand years before Bentham; the Daoists, who thought all this moralizing was the problem; Buddhist ethics; and the southern African ethic of Ubuntu. The aim is not a tour of exotic wisdom. These are rival theories, with arguments, and they put pressure on assumptions that Western ethics has tended to take for granted. Previous: [Chapter 6 — Hellenistic Ethics](06-hellenistic-ethics.md). Next: [Chapter 8 — Hume and the Sentimentalists](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md).

## A word about comparison

Two easy mistakes lie in wait for anyone who studies ethics across cultures. The first is to treat everything outside Europe as "wisdom literature," lovely sayings rather than philosophy. The Mohists built arguments with explicit criteria for testing claims; Mencius and Xunzi wrote essays that anticipate objections; Buddhist philosophers wrote treatises on the self and the logic of compassion. The *Analects* is a collection of sayings, but so are the fragments of Heraclitus.

The second mistake is the opposite: to translate everything instantly into Western boxes. "Confucius was a virtue ethicist." "Mozi was a utilitarian." These labels capture something, and the chapter will use them where they help, but the traditions grew from different questions. Chinese thinkers rarely asked "what makes an act right?" in the abstract way Kant or Mill did; they asked how to become a certain kind of person and how to order a family and a state. Buddhist ethics is one strand of a path to liberation, not a free-standing theory of obligation. The rule from [Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md) applies: read charitably, reconstruct carefully, then evaluate. And notice what your own tradition takes for granted. Modern Western ethics has largely assumed that the moral unit is the individual, that morality is mainly about duties to strangers, and that rules are its core. Each tradition here questions at least one of those assumptions.

## Confucius: humaneness and ritual

Kong Qiu, known in the West by the Latinized name Confucius (551–479 BCE), lived in a period when the old Zhou dynasty's authority had collapsed and warring states competed for power. He held minor office, failed to find a ruler who would use his advice, and spent his later years teaching. The *Analects* (*Lunyu*, "collected sayings") was compiled by his students and their students over several generations. It is short, unsystematic, and often cryptic, but two concepts sit at its center: *ren* and *li*.

### Ren: the humane heart

*Ren* is the supreme virtue in the *Analects*. Legge translates it as "perfect virtue" or "benevolence"; others use "humaneness" or "goodness." The character combines the signs for "person" and "two," and many readers take this to suggest that one becomes fully human only in relation to others. Asked about it directly, Confucius gave one of his shortest answers:

> Fan Ch'ih asked about benevolence. The Master said, 'It is to love all men.' He asked about knowledge. The Master said, 'It is to know all men.'
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* XII.22 (tr. James Legge)

When a student asked for a single word to live by, Confucius chose *shu*, "reciprocity" or "sympathetic understanding":

> Tsze-kung asked, saying, 'Is there one word which may serve as a rule of practice for all one's life?' The Master said, 'Is not RECIPROCITY such a word? What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others.'
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* XV.23 (tr. James Legge)

Notice the negative form. Not "do unto others what you would have them do unto you," but "do not do to others what you do not want done to yourself." The negative version is more modest. It does not license imposing your preferences on others (the person who loves surprise parties throwing one for a friend who hates them). It asks only that you put yourself in the other's place before acting.

### Li: ritual and propriety

If *ren* is the heart, *li* is the form. *Li* originally meant the rites of ancestral sacrifice, but Confucius extended it to the whole body of customs governing how people treat one another: how to greet, mourn, give gifts, serve a parent, address a superior. Legge translates it as "propriety." The famous exchange with his favorite student, Yan Hui (Yen Yuan), joins the two concepts:

> Yen Yuan asked about perfect virtue. The Master said, 'To subdue one's self and return to propriety, is perfect virtue. If a man can for one day subdue himself and return to propriety, all under heaven will ascribe perfect virtue to him.'
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* XII.1 (tr. James Legge)

Asked for details, Confucius answered with nothing but *li*: "Look not at what is contrary to propriety; listen not to what is contrary to propriety; speak not what is contrary to propriety; make no movement which is contrary to propriety."

This can sound like etiquette dressed up as ethics. But there are good reasons to take it seriously. Rituals make values concrete: a wedding, a funeral, a thank-you note each turns an attitude into something done together, where everyone knows the steps. Herbert Fingarette's *Confucius: The Secular as Sacred* (1972) showed how much of human life runs smoothly through such shared forms, without force or calculation. Rituals also *train* feeling, much as Aristotle held that we become just by doing just acts ([Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md)). And Confucius insisted that the form without the feeling is empty: "If a man be without the virtues proper to humanity, what has he to do with the rites of propriety?" (III.3).

The best illustration is his dispute with the student Zai Wo (Tsai Wo), who argued that three years' mourning for a parent was too long. Confucius did not cite a rule. He asked whether Zai Wo would feel at ease eating fine rice and wearing embroidered clothes after one year. Zai Wo said he would. "If you can feel at ease, do it," said Confucius. After Zai Wo left, he explained what was missing:

> It is not till a child is three years old that it is allowed to leave the arms of its parents. And the three years' mourning is universally observed throughout the empire. Did Yu enjoy the three years' love of his parents?
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* XVII.21 (tr. James Legge)

The ritual is right not because tradition says so but because it fits what a decent child feels: three years of grief answering three years of being carried. Zai Wo's failure is not that he broke a rule but that he did not feel what he should.

### Ruling by example

Confucius applied the same thinking to politics. Laws and punishments can make people comply, but they cannot make people good:

> The Master said, 'If the people be led by laws, and uniformity sought to be given them by punishments, they will try to avoid the punishment, but have no sense of shame. ... If they be led by virtue, and uniformity sought to be given them by the rules of propriety, they will have the sense of shame, and moreover will become good.'
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* II.3 (tr. James Legge)

Modern research on "crowding out" partly supports him. In a well-known study of Israeli day-care centers, introducing a fine for late pickup *increased* lateness. A sense of obligation to the teachers had been replaced by a price. Confucius also held that roles carry obligations: there is good government "when the prince is prince, and the minister is minister; when the father is father, and the son is son" (XII.11). This is the "rectification of names": a "father" who neglects his children, or a "ruler" who robs his people, no longer fits the name, and order decays.

### The sheep-stealing father

The most provocative passage in the *Analects* concerns loyalty and justice:

> The Duke of Sheh informed Confucius, saying, 'Among us here there are those who may be styled upright in their conduct. If their father have stolen a sheep, they will bear witness to the fact.' ... Confucius said, 'Among us, in our part of the country, those who are upright are different from this. The father conceals the misconduct of the son, and the son conceals the misconduct of the father. Uprightness is to be found in this.'
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* XIII.18 (tr. James Legge)

Compare Euthyphro ([Chapter 4](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md)), who prosecuted his own father and called it piety. Confucius takes the opposite side. There is something to be said for him. Many legal systems recognize spousal privilege, on the Confucian thought that a state which turns family members into informants corrodes something valuable; twentieth-century regimes that celebrated children who denounced their parents are a warning. But concealing a stolen sheep is not concealing a murder, and the sheep's owner has a claim too. How much partiality toward those close to us is permitted, or required? [Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md) returns to the question, and [Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md) shows care ethicists defending partiality on grounds a Confucian would recognize.

### The cultivated person

The ideal of the *Analects* is the *junzi*, the "superior man" or "exemplary person." Originally the word meant a nobleman by birth; Confucius made it a matter of character. The *junzi* has internalized *ren* and *li* so thoroughly that right action is no longer a struggle:

> At seventy, I could follow what my heart desired, without transgressing what was right.
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* II.4 (tr. James Legge)

This is Aristotle's distinction between the merely self-controlled person and the virtuous one, whose desires and judgment agree ([Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md)). Confucius got there at seventy, which suggests how long the training takes. The opposite of the *junzi* is not the criminal but the counterfeit: "Your good, careful people of the villages are the thieves of virtue" (XVII.13). The "village worthy" offends no one and follows every fashion, and because he looks so much like the real thing, he steals the credit that belongs to genuine goodness.

### Objections to Confucianism

Confucian ethics has been sharply criticized, in China as well as the West. Its five relationships (ruler–minister, father–son, husband–wife, elder–younger brother, friend–friend) are mostly unequal, and later state Confucianism used them to justify autocracy and the subordination of women; the May Fourth reformers of 1919 called for overthrowing "Confucius and Sons." If *li* is the standard, it is unclear how to criticize the *li*, although Confucius did accept some changes (a cheaper silk cap for the linen one, IX.3) while rejecting others. And the emphasis on family can shade into nepotism, as the Mohists complained. Defenders reply that Confucius' view was reciprocal (the father must be a father before the son owes a son's duties) and that the hierarchies can be revised while keeping the core idea that roles carry duties. Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont go further, reading Confucianism as **role ethics**: there is no self prior to one's roles as daughter, teacher, friend, or citizen, and morality consists in living those roles well. That is a real alternative to the Western picture of an autonomous individual who chooses relationships after the fact.

## Mencius versus Xunzi: is human nature good?

A century after Confucius, his followers split over a question every ethical theory must eventually face. If people need cultivation to become good, what is the raw material? Is goodness growing something already present, or imposing something from outside?

### Mencius: the four sprouts

Mengzi, Latinized as Mencius (c. 372–289 BCE), answered that human nature is good. He did not mean that people always act well, but that everyone has the beginnings of virtue, "sprouts" that will grow if not stunted.

His most famous argument (2A6, paraphrased here because the public-domain English selection used in this course omits Book II) is a thought experiment. Suppose anyone suddenly sees a small child about to fall into a well. Everyone, Mencius says, would feel alarm and distress, and not because they want to get in good with the child's parents, or want praise from their neighbors, or dislike the child's cries. The reaction is immediate. So the heart of compassion is part of human nature, and so are a sense of shame, a sense of deference, and a sense of right and wrong. These four hearts are the sprouts of the four virtues: benevolence (*ren*), righteousness (*yi*), propriety (*li*), and wisdom (*zhi*). The argument does not say that everyone would *save* the child, only that everyone would *feel* the alarm, and that the feeling is not self-interested. This is the point Butler made against Hobbes ([Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md)).

The Legge selection does contain Mencius' other great case. King Xuan of Qi asked whether someone like him could become a true king who protects his people. Mencius said yes and reminded him of an incident:

> The king,' said he, 'was sitting aloft in the hall, when some people appeared leading a bull past below it. The king saw it, and asked where the bull was going, and being answered that they were going to consecrate a bell with its blood, he said, "Let it go, I cannot bear its frightened appearance--as if it were an innocent person going to the place of death." They asked in reply whether, if they did so, they should omit the consecration of the bell, but the king said, "How can that be omitted? Change it for a sheep."
>
> — Mencius, *The Sayings of Mencius* I.1.7 (1A7) (tr. James Legge)

People had mocked the king as stingy for swapping a big animal for a small one. Mencius saw compassion for a suffering creature that was in view; the sheep, out of sight, did not trigger it. "You saw the bull, and had not seen the sheep." The task was to *extend* the feeling:

> Now here is kindness sufficient to reach to animals, and yet no benefits are extended from it to the people--how is this? is an exception to be made here?
>
> — Mencius, *The Sayings of Mencius* I.1.7 (1A7) (tr. James Legge)

Here is Mencius' ethics in miniature. Morality is not acquiring new motives but **extension**: carrying the compassion you already feel in vivid, nearby cases over to similar cases that are less vivid. "By weighing we know what things are light, and what heavy," he told the king; the mind must be weighed too. Peter Singer's drowning-child argument ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)) has exactly this structure, and faces the same reply: perhaps nearness and visibility really do matter. The king's swap is either a moral failure or an accurate picture of how compassion works.

Mencius' images are agricultural. Ox Mountain near Qi is bare, and people think it never had trees; in fact axes cut them down and cattle ate the new shoots. The farmer of Song, worried that his sprouts were slow, pulled on them to help, and by morning they had withered. Virtue cannot be forced; it must be nourished, and the conditions matter. In the same dialogue Mencius tells the king that ordinary people without a secure livelihood cannot keep a "fixed heart," and that punishing them for crimes poverty drove them to "is to entrap the people." He also distrusted the language of profit. When a king asked what counsel he brought "to profit my kingdom," he replied:

> What I am likewise provided with are counsels to benevolence and righteousness; and these are my only topics.
>
> — Mencius, *The Sayings of Mencius* I.1.1 (1A1) (tr. James Legge)

If the king asks what profits the kingdom, the ministers will ask what profits their families and the people what profits themselves, and everyone will try to take from everyone else. The target, as we will see, was the Mohists.

### Xunzi: nature is bad

Xunzi (c. 310–c. 235 BCE), the third great classical Confucian, flatly disagreed. His essay "Human Nature Is Bad" (paraphrased here from the Ivanhoe and Van Norden reader) argues that people are born with a love of profit, with envy and hatred, and with appetites of the eyes and ears. Following them leads to strife and chaos. Goodness comes from deliberate effort (*wei*, "artifice"), under the transforming influence of teachers, ritual, and righteousness.

His images are technological. Warped wood must be steamed and pressed against a straightening board; blunt metal must be ground on a whetstone; clay becomes a pot only through a potter. In his essay on ritual he offers a genealogy: people have desires; unsatisfied desires lead to seeking; seeking without limit leads to conflict and chaos; the ancient kings hated chaos, so they set up ritual to give each a share and to train desire. This is close to Hobbes ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)), with a key difference. Hobbes' solution is a sovereign with a sword, which restrains desire. Xunzi's is ritual and education, which transform it: the gentleman comes to love the rituals, as a musician who once practiced scales from duty comes to love music. Against Mencius, Xunzi asked what use sage kings and ritual would be if people were already good, and argued that the very fact that people *want* goodness shows they lack it, as only the poor long for wealth.

### Who is right?

The question is partly empirical. Infants of a few months prefer puppets that help to puppets that hinder, and toddlers spontaneously help adults pick up dropped objects: a Mencian sprout. But children also show strong in-group bias, and their sharing is selective and fragile. Haidt's picture of intuitive moral "foundations" shaped by culture ([Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md)) is something of a synthesis. The two Confucians also agreed on much: both held that people need teachers, ritual, and long practice, and that anyone can become a sage. They differed over the direction of cultivation, drawing out what is in us or putting in what is not. A Mencian teacher looks for the sprout of compassion in a cruel child; a Xunzian teacher drills good habits and trusts that feeling will follow. Most parents do both.

## Mohism: impartial caring

The Confucians' most formidable early opponents were the followers of Mo Di, or Mozi (traditionally c. 470–c. 391 BCE): an organized movement, half school and half brotherhood of engineers who hired themselves out to defend small states against siege. They were the first Chinese thinkers to argue systematically. (No public-domain English translation of the *Mozi* is on Project Gutenberg, so this section paraphrases the Ivanhoe and Van Norden reader.)

### The standard of benefit

Mozi proposed three tests for any doctrine: does it agree with the deeds of the ancient sage kings, with the evidence of ordinary people's eyes and ears, and, when put into practice, does it **benefit** the state and the people? The third did most of the work. What is right is what promotes the benefit of the world and removes harm, where benefit means concrete social goods: the people fed, clothed, and at rest, the population growing, order replacing chaos. This is recognizably **consequentialism** ([Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)), with social goods in place of Bentham's pleasure.

### Caring for all alike

From this standard Mozi derived *jian ai*, "impartial caring." What are the greatest harms? Great states attacking small ones, strong families overthrowing weak ones, the many abusing the few. Their source is partiality: caring for one's own state, family, or person and not for others. If people regarded others' states and families as their own, who would attack or steal? To the objection that this is too hard for human beings, Mozi replied that people have done harder things when rulers demanded them (one king's ministers starved themselves because he admired slim waists). And impartial caring serves everyone, including its critics. Suppose you had to leave your parents, wife, and children in someone's care during a long journey. Would you choose a friend who held to partiality or one who held to impartiality? Everyone, Mozi said, would choose the impartial friend.

### Against offensive war, and against waste

His argument against aggressive war is a classic of reasoning by consistency. Stealing peaches from an orchard is condemned; stealing pigs and chickens is worse; stealing horses and oxen worse still; killing an innocent man for his clothes worst of all. Yet when a state attacks another, killing thousands, people praise it as righteous. This, Mozi said, is like calling a little black "black" and a lot of black "white." He applied the benefit standard just as ruthlessly to Confucian ritual. Elaborate funerals bury wealth; three years' mourning keeps officials from governing and farmers from farming; music costs instruments and labor and feeds no one. What Confucius saw in the Zai Wo passage as the fitting expression of love, Mozi saw as waste.

### Mencius' counterattack

Mencius regarded Mohism as a menace and said that its impartial caring amounted to "having no father": a doctrine that asks you to care for a stranger's father as much as your own denies the relationship that makes you a son. In one exchange (3A5), a Mohist named Yi Zhi buried his parents lavishly and explained that love has no gradations but its practice *begins* with one's parents. Mencius asked whether Yi Zhi really believed that a man loves his brother's child no more than a neighbor's baby. Either he loved his parents specially, and was a Confucian, or he did not, and was inhuman.

This is the oldest recorded version of a debate that still divides ethicists. The impartialist (Mozi, Bentham, Godwin, Singer) holds that everyone counts equally from the moral point of view. The partialist (Mencius, Bernard Williams, the care ethicists) holds that special relationships are part of morality's foundation, not exceptions to it. Williams' complaint that a man who saves his drowning wife rather than a stranger, after reasoning that this is permissible, has had "one thought too many" is pure Mencius ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)). Mohism vanished as a school after China's unification in 221 BCE. Whether Confucianism won the argument or only the political struggle is worth asking.

## Daoism: against moralizing

Both Confucians and Mohists were trying to fix society by getting people to adopt the right standards. The Daoists thought the trying was the problem.

### Laozi: when the Way is lost

The *Daodejing* (*Tao Teh King*), traditionally attributed to a sage named Laozi, is a short, poetic text probably compiled in the fourth or third century BCE. It describes the Dao, the "Way," as the nameless source and pattern of things, and recommends acting in accord with it through *wu wei*, "non-action" or "effortless action." Its ethics is largely a critique of ethics:

> When the Great Tao (Way or Method) ceased to be observed, benevolence and righteousness came into vogue. (Then) appeared wisdom and shrewdness, and there ensued great hypocrisy.
>
> — Laozi, *Tao Teh King* 18 (tr. James Legge)

Moral concepts appear when something has already gone wrong. Nobody talks about "filial piety" in a family where children simply love their parents. And once there are virtues to display, people display them, and hypocrisy follows. Chapter 38 ranks the decline:

> Thus it was that when the Tao was lost, its attributes appeared; when its attributes were lost, benevolence appeared; when benevolence was lost, righteousness appeared; and when righteousness was lost, the proprieties appeared. ... Now propriety is the attenuated form of leal-heartedness and good faith, and is also the commencement of disorder; ...
>
> — Laozi, *Tao Teh King* 38 (tr. James Legge)

At the bottom sit *ren*, *yi*, and *li*, the Confucian virtues. Chapter 19 draws the conclusion: "If we could renounce our benevolence and discard our righteousness, the people would again become filial and kindly." The same logic applies to law: "the more display there is of legislation, the more thieves and robbers there are" (ch. 57). Every new rule creates a new loophole and a new class of people who are technically compliant while violating the spirit, as anyone who has worked in a bureaucracy knows. The positive ideal is water:

> The highest excellence is like (that of) water. The excellence of water appears in its benefiting all things, and in its occupying, without striving (to the contrary), the low place which all men dislike. Hence (its way) is near to (that of) the Tao.
>
> — Laozi, *Tao Teh King* 8 (tr. James Legge)

One contrast with Confucius is pointed. Asked whether injury should be returned with kindness, Confucius replied, "With what then will you recompense kindness? Recompense injury with justice, and recompense kindness with kindness" (*Analects* XIV.36). The *Daodejing* lists among the sage's practices "to recompense injury with kindness" (ch. 63). Here the Daoist and the Christian ("love your enemies") stand together against the Confucian, who thought that returning kindness for injury erases the difference between those who deserve it and those who do not.

### Zhuangzi: the cook and the ox

Zhuangzi (traditionally c. 369–c. 286 BCE; the book that bears his name is partly by later hands) is the wittiest writer in the Chinese tradition, full of talking animals, cripples wiser than kings, and Confucius himself sometimes made to speak as a Daoist. His most famous ethical image is Prince Hui's cook, whose carving of an ox is "in perfect harmony,--rhythmical like the dance of the Mulberry Grove." When the prince praises his skill, the cook says that what he follows is the Dao, which goes beyond skill:

> When I first began to cut up bullocks, I saw before me simply whole bullocks. After three years' practice, I saw no more whole animals. ... A good cook changes his chopper once a year,--because he cuts. An ordinary cook, once a month,--because he hacks. But I have had this chopper nineteen years, and although I have cut up many thousand bullocks, its edge is as if fresh from the whetstone.
>
> — Zhuangzi, *Chuang Tzu* ch. III (tr. Herbert A. Giles)

The prince says he has learned from the cook how to take care of his life. The best action is not applying rules or calculating outcomes but the effortless responsiveness of someone so trained that they perceive the "joints" of a situation and move through them without friction. This is *wu wei*, close to Aristotle's practical wisdom ([Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md)) and to what psychologists call "flow." Edward Slingerland's *Trying Not to Try* (2014) argues that Confucians and Daoists shared this ideal of spontaneity and its paradox: how can you *try* to be spontaneous? Zhuangzi's second chapter goes further, arguing that "right" and "wrong" depend on the standpoint from which they are judged and that the Confucians and Mohists simply affirm what the other denies. Readers dispute whether this is relativism ([Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md)), skepticism like the Pyrrhonists' ([Chapter 6](06-hellenistic-ethics.md)), or a call to a higher view.

His attitude to death is a test case. When his wife died, his friend Hui Shi found him sitting on the ground, drumming on a bowl and singing. Zhuangzi explained that he had grieved at first, then reflected that she had come from formlessness and returned to it:

> And now, by virtue of a further change, she is dead, passing from one phase to another like the sequence of spring, summer, autumn, and winter. And while she is thus lying asleep in Eternity, for me to go about weeping and wailing would be to proclaim myself ignorant of these natural laws. Therefore I refrain.
>
> — Zhuangzi, *Chuang Tzu* ch. XVIII (tr. Herbert A. Giles)

Compare Epicurus and the Stoics ([Chapter 6](06-hellenistic-ethics.md)): all argue that grief rests on a mistake about death, and all face the objection that someone who stops grieving for a spouse has lost something human. Confucius, rebuked for weeping too much when Yan Hui died, answered: "If I am not to mourn bitterly for this man, for whom should I mourn?" (*Analects* XI.9).

### Is Daoism an ethics?

The Daoists were not amoral. They valued simplicity, humility, and gentleness ("I have three precious things," says chapter 67: gentleness, economy, and "shrinking from taking precedence of others"), and they opposed violence. Their target was *moralism*: the self-conscious pursuit and display of virtue and the multiplication of rules. The point deserves a hearing in any ethics. The person who constantly thinks about being good is often less good than the one who simply acts well.

## Buddhist ethics

Buddhist ethics developed in India and spread across Asia, and its sources run to thousands of pages. This section concentrates on the early teaching as preserved in the Pali Canon and on one later argument from the Mahayana tradition.

### The path

The Buddha, Siddhartha Gautama, lived in northern India, probably in the fifth century BCE. Tradition says he was a prince who left his palace after encountering old age, sickness, and death, tried and rejected both luxury and extreme asceticism, and attained awakening through meditation. The *Dhammapada*, 423 verses from the Pali Canon, is the best-known short ethical text in the Buddhist world.

Buddhist ethics is embedded in a diagnosis. The Four Noble Truths say that life as ordinarily lived is marked by unsatisfactoriness (*dukkha*); that its cause is craving (*tanha*); that it can end (*nirvana*); and that the way is the Noble Eightfold Path: right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration. These are grouped as wisdom, ethical conduct (*sila*), and meditation, so ethics is one strand of a single practice. For laypeople, conduct is summarized in the **Five Precepts**: to refrain from killing, from taking what is not given, from sexual misconduct, from false speech, and from intoxicants. They are training commitments one undertakes, not commandments from a god. The *Dhammapada* compresses it all:

> Not to commit any sin, to do good, and to purify one's mind, that is the teaching of (all) the Awakened.
>
> — *The Dhammapada* 183 (tr. F. Max Müller)

### Intention and karma

The *Dhammapada* opens with the primacy of mind:

> All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage.
>
> — *The Dhammapada* 1 (tr. F. Max Müller)

In Brahmanical religion *karma* had meant ritual action. The Buddha redefined it as intentional action; in a well-known passage of the Pali Canon he says that it is intention (*cetana*) that he calls karma. Acts rooted in the "three poisons" of greed, hatred, and delusion produce suffering for the agent; acts rooted in generosity, kindness, and wisdom produce well-being. Whatever one thinks of rebirth, the psychological claim stands on its own:

> For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule.
>
> — *The Dhammapada* 5 (tr. F. Max Müller)

### Non-self and compassion

The most distinctive Buddhist doctrine is *anatman* (Pali *anatta*), "non-self." What we call a self is a changing bundle of bodily form, sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness, with no permanent owner behind them, and clinging to the illusion of a fixed self is a root of suffering.

The eighth-century philosopher Shantideva drew an ethical conclusion in *The Way of the Bodhisattva* (ch. 8). If there is no enduring self, pains do not really "belong" to anyone. My future pain is not mine in any deep sense, any more than yours is; it is just pain. So whatever reason I have to prevent my own future suffering is equally a reason to prevent anyone's. Self-interest is not more rational than altruism; the line between them rests on a metaphysical error. Derek Parfit reached a similar conclusion in *Reasons and Persons* (1984) and noted the parallel with Buddhism ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)).

The emotional counterpart is the cultivation of the four "divine abodes": loving-kindness (*metta*), compassion (*karuna*), sympathetic joy (*mudita*), and equanimity (*upekkha*). Meditators direct *metta* first to themselves, then to a friend, a neutral person, an enemy, and all beings: Mencian extension made into a discipline. The *Dhammapada* states a principle that echoes Confucius' *shu*:

> All men tremble at punishment, all men love life; remember that thou art like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter.
>
> — *The Dhammapada* 130 (tr. F. Max Müller)

In Buddhist tradition the principle reaches all sentient beings, which is why the first precept covers animals and many Buddhists are vegetarian ([Chapter 20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md)).

### What kind of ethics?

Damien Keown argues that Buddhist ethics is closest to Aristotelian **virtue ethics**: the goal is a perfected state of character, and right actions express and lead to it. Charles Goodman argues that much of it, especially Shantideva's Mahayana, is **consequentialist**: the bodhisattva aims to reduce the suffering of all beings and may break ordinary rules when compassion requires. Others hold that the categories do not fit a path that aims to transform perception itself.

Two objections are common. If the goal is the extinction of craving, does Buddhism counsel detachment from those we love? (The Buddha did leave his wife and newborn son.) Defenders reply that what is renounced is *clinging* love, not love; *metta* is cultivated precisely as non-possessive care. And karma can seem to blame victims: if suffering results from past deeds, do the poor deserve their lot? Many teachers reject the inference. In the twentieth century, "engaged Buddhists" such as Thich Nhat Hanh, and B. R. Ambedkar, who led hundreds of thousands of Dalits out of the caste system into Buddhism, applied Buddhist ethics to war, poverty, and injustice.

## Ubuntu: a person through persons

The final tradition comes from sub-Saharan Africa, where moral thought was carried mostly in proverbs, customs, and ceremonies and was systematized by philosophers only in the twentieth century. Its best-known expression is the Nguni (Zulu and Xhosa) maxim *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*, "a person is a person through other persons"; *ubuntu* names the humanness this implies. The Kenyan philosopher John Mbiti gave the most quoted English formulation: "I am, because we are; and since we are, therefore I am" (*African Religions and Philosophy*, 1969). The line deliberately inverts Descartes, who made the solitary thinking self the foundation of knowledge.

### Personhood as achievement

The Nigerian philosopher Ifeanyi Menkiti drew a radical conclusion in a 1984 essay. On the African view, he argued, personhood is not given at birth but *achieved*, by growing into one's community, taking on its obligations, and acting well. An elder who has lived a life of service is more fully a person than a newborn, and someone who acts cruelly may be said, in many African languages, to be "not a person," a moral verdict rather than a biological one. This is close to Confucius' rectification of names and to Aristotle's view that the human function is fulfilled only in a good life. The Ghanaian philosopher Kwame Gyekye objected that Menkiti's "radical communitarianism" left too little room for rights and dissent, and defended a "moderate communitarianism" in which the community shapes the self but the individual can still question and change its values. The disagreement mirrors the Western debate between liberals and communitarians ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)).

### Metz's principle

Thaddeus Metz has tried to formulate Ubuntu as a moral theory to stand beside utilitarianism and Kantianism. Roughly, he proposes that an act is right insofar as it honors *harmonious* relationships, and wrong insofar as it is discordant. Harmony has two parts: **identity**, sharing a way of life and thinking of oneself as part of a "we," and **solidarity**, caring for others' quality of life. Metz argues that the principle explains judgments that other theories struggle with: why it is wrong to kill one patient to harvest organs for five (killing is the ultimate discord), why consensus is valued, and why reconciliation can be a better response to wrongdoing than retribution.

That last point became world-historical. After apartheid, South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, offered amnesty to perpetrators who fully disclosed politically motivated crimes. Tutu defended it in *No Future Without Forgiveness* (1999) by appeal to *ubuntu*: justice should restore the humanity of victim and offender, not merely punish. The model has influenced restorative justice worldwide, and has been criticized by some victims' families for asking them to forgo a justice they were owed.

### Objections to Ubuntu

If one becomes a person by fitting into the community, what of the dissenter or the reformer whose community is unjust? Metz replies that harmony includes caring for others' *good*, which may require opposing communal practice, but the worry about conformism remains. As with Confucianism, traditional practices associated with *ubuntu* included inequalities of gender and age, and political appeals to it have sometimes been used to silence critics. And "harmony" can be stretched to fit almost any conclusion; Metz's project is partly an attempt to make it precise enough to test.

## What comparison teaches

Several themes run through this chapter. **The relational self**: Confucians and Ubuntu thinkers hold that one becomes a self through relationships, and Buddhists that the separate self is an illusion, whereas Western ethics since Hobbes has tended to start with individuals and ask how they can be brought to cooperate ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)). **Cultivation over rules**: nearly every tradition here, like the Greeks, puts the training of character and perception ahead of decision procedures. **Partiality**: the Mozi–Mencius debate is the earliest clear statement of a problem every theory must solve. **Suspicion of morality**: Laozi and Zhuangzi anticipate Nietzsche ([Chapter 15](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md)) in warning that too much explicit attention to goodness can make people worse.

The diversity might seem to support relativism ([Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md)), but the overlap is as striking: a negative Golden Rule in Confucius and the *Dhammapada*, compassion and its extension in Mencius and the Buddha, persons bound to one another in Confucius and *ubuntu*. David Wong's "pluralistic relativism" holds that there is more than one true morality, but that the range is constrained by human nature and the needs of cooperation. At the least, the reflective equilibrium of [Chapter 1](01-what-is-ethics.md) gains from a wider pool of considered judgments; your intuitions about family, ritual, or the self may be less universal than they feel. For these traditions beyond ethics, see [Classical India and China](../learn-philosophy/08-classical-india-and-china.md); for Buddhism as a response to meaninglessness, see [Beyond Nihilism](../learn-nihilism/15-beyond-nihilism.md).

## Living it

- **Audit your rituals.** List the small rituals of your week: greetings, meals, messages, ways of saying goodbye. Which ones still carry feeling? Which have become empty forms? Confucius would ask you to revive the first kind, not abolish them.
- **Practice extension.** Recall a time when you felt real compassion for someone in front of you. Then deliberately picture a similar person far away, in the same situation. Notice what happens to the feeling. Mencius thought the gap could be closed with practice.
- **Drop one rule.** Pick an area where you rely on rigid personal rules. For a week, try acting from attention to the situation instead, like Zhuangzi's cook. Notice whether you do better or worse.
- **Loving-kindness.** For five minutes a day, silently wish well to yourself, a friend, a stranger, and someone you dislike, in that order. Notice which is hardest.
- **Ask the Ubuntu question.** Before a difficult conversation, ask not "what am I owed?" but "what would leave this relationship more whole?"

## Summary

- Non-Western traditions are **philosophy**, with arguments, and should be read charitably without being forced into Western boxes.
- **Confucius** centers ethics on *ren* (humaneness) expressed through *li* (ritual propriety), with *shu*, a negative Golden Rule. Rituals give feeling a shape and train it. He prefers leading by virtue to leading by law, and holds that a son may conceal his father's theft. The ideal *junzi* desires what is right. Critics cite hierarchy, conservatism, and nepotism; defenders propose **role ethics**.
- **Mencius**: human nature is good. The child at the well and the king's pity for the ox show four **sprouts** of virtue, and ethics is their **extension**. **Xunzi**: nature is bad, and ritual straightens it as a press straightens wood.
- **Mozi** judged everything by **benefit**, taught **impartial caring**, condemned offensive war by consistency, and attacked costly ritual. Mencius replied that impartial caring "has no father." The **partiality** debate continues.
- **Daoists** held that benevolence, righteousness, and ritual arise when the Way is lost and breed hypocrisy; their ideal is **wu wei**, like water or Cook Ding's knife.
- **Buddhist ethics** is one strand of a path to end suffering, with the Five Precepts. Karma is **intention**. **Non-self** grounds impartial compassion (Shantideva), trained through the *brahmaviharas*. It has been read as virtue ethics (Keown) and as consequentialism (Goodman).
- **Ubuntu**: "a person is a person through other persons." Menkiti treats personhood as an achievement, Gyekye defends moderate communitarianism, and **Metz** proposes a principle of **harmony** (identity and solidarity). Tutu's Truth and Reconciliation Commission applied it.

### Dialogue 1 — Confucius and the family secret

**Confucius:** You have the look of someone carrying a weight that is not yours. Sit. What is it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: tell Confucius about a time when loyalty to family or friends pulled against what you thought was fair to others.*

**You:** My brother runs a small business. I do his books on weekends. I found out he's been underreporting his income for years. Not millions, but a lot. Part of me thinks I should report him. It's not fair to everyone who pays.

**Confucius:** The Duke of She once told me that in his country, an upright son would testify against his father for stealing a sheep. Do you know what I told him?

**You:** That in your country, father and son cover for each other. I've read it. Honestly, it bothers me. It sounds like the Mafia.

**Confucius:** Does it? Tell me: if you report your brother, what becomes of him?

**You:** Fines, maybe worse. He'd probably never speak to me again. Our mother would be devastated.

**Confucius:** And what is gained?

**You:** The government gets its money. Justice is done.

**Confucius:** Justice for whom? The tax office is not a person who was wronged in the way a neighbor robbed of a sheep is wronged. But set that aside. Even if the wrong were to a neighbor, I did not say the son should *approve* of the theft. I said he should not be the one to bear witness. There are other paths.

**You:** Like what?

**Confucius:** You do his books. You are not a stranger reading about him in the newspaper. You are in the position of a younger brother who can speak to him. Have you?

**You:** No. I've been avoiding it.

**Confucius:** Then you have skipped the first duty and gone straight to the last. The son who covers for his father is not the son who says nothing. He is the son who, in private, gently and persistently, urges his father to make it right. That is uprightness within the family. Turning to the magistrate is what you do when there is no family left.

**You:** And if he refuses?

**Confucius:** Then you must decide whether you can go on keeping his books. You need not join in the wrong to remain his brother. But consider: a country where brothers inform on brothers is not a country where people become good. It is a country where they become afraid.

**You:** And a country where everyone covers for family is a country where the powerful never pay.

**Confucius:** Yes. That is why the ruler must be a ruler first. When those above are upright, those below are ashamed to cheat. You are asking me to solve with one sheep what can only be solved by the whole flock. Begin with the conversation you have been avoiding.

### Dialogue 2 — Mozi and the birthday party

**Mozi:** I hear you are planning a celebration. Tell me what it will cost and what it will do.

**You:** …

*Your turn: describe something you spend money on mainly to honor a relationship or a tradition, and say why it matters to you.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It's my parents' fortieth anniversary. My siblings and I are throwing a party. Venue, catering, a band. Maybe eight thousand dollars split four ways.

**Mozi:** Eight thousand. And what does it produce? Will anyone be fed who would otherwise go hungry? Clothed who would otherwise be cold?

**You:** Well, the guests will be fed.

**Mozi:** Guests who already eat every day. Meanwhile there are people in the world who do not. How many could eight thousand dollars keep alive?

**You:** Depending on the charity, maybe one or two lives, if you believe the effective altruism numbers. I see where this is going.

**Mozi:** Then follow it. If a stranger took eight thousand dollars from a starving family to hire a band, you would call it theft. When you do the same with your own money, you call it love. Is that not calling a little black "black" and a lot of black "white"?

**You:** It's not the same. I'm not taking anything from them.

**Mozi:** You are choosing your parents' pleasure over their lives. Ask yourself: if you were starving and someone could choose between saving you and throwing a party for their parents, which kind of person would you want them to be?

**You:** Okay. But my parents would be hurt if we did nothing. Forty years is a big deal. The party isn't about the band. It's about telling them, in front of everyone they love, that their marriage mattered.

**Mozi:** Could you not tell them with a meal at home and a letter from each of you?

**You:** We could. But Confucius would say the ritual is how the feeling becomes real. You don't mourn three months for a parent who carried you three years.

**Mozi:** Confucius would bury a year's harvest in a coffin and call it filial piety. I ask only that you count the cost. Spend half. Give the other half where it saves a life, and tell your parents you did it in their name. If they are good people, they will be prouder of that than of the band.

**You:** That's actually not a bad idea. Though I notice you've just used a ritual to make the gift mean something.

**Mozi:** If it benefits the world, I have no objection to rituals. I object only to those that do not.

</details>
