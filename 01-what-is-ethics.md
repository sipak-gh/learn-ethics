# Chapter 1 — What Is Ethics?

Ethics is the part of philosophy that asks how you should live, what you owe to others, and what makes a life go well. This chapter draws the map you will use for the rest of the course: the three levels of the subject (normative ethics, metaethics, applied ethics), the raw material moral philosophers work with (considered judgments, sometimes called intuitions), and the method most of them use to test those judgments against theories (reflective equilibrium). It ends with the oldest challenge in the field, Glaucon's story of the ring of Gyges, which asks the question every later chapter has to answer in its own way: if you could do wrong and never be caught, why would you be good? You need no background to read it. Previous: [Course overview](README.md). Next: [Chapter 2 — Moral Reasoning](02-moral-reasoning.md).

## A question you cannot avoid

Most philosophical questions can be postponed. You can live a full life without settling whether numbers exist or whether the past is real. You cannot postpone ethics, because every day you already answer its questions by acting. You tell the truth or shade it; you give money away or keep it; you forgive or hold a grudge; you eat what you eat. Doing nothing is also an answer. Ethics is the attempt to answer these questions *well*: deliberately, with reasons you could defend to someone who disagrees.

Socrates put the stakes plainly near the end of Book I of the *Republic*, after a long and bad-tempered argument with the sophist Thrasymachus:

> But whether the just have a better and happier life than the unjust is a further question which we also proposed to consider. I think that they have, and for the reasons which I have given; but still I should like to examine further, for no light matter is at stake, nothing less than the rule of human life.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* I, 352d (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

"The rule of human life" is Jowett's rendering of a Greek phrase that means, literally, "in what way one ought to live" (*hontina tropon chrē zēn*). That question, not "what is the definition of good?" and not "which acts are forbidden?", is where Western ethics, by the usual reckoning, begins. At his trial in 399 BCE Socrates told the Athenian jury that he would rather die than stop asking it:

> … if I say again that daily to discourse about virtue, and of those other things about which you hear me examining myself and others, is the greatest good of man, and that the unexamined life is not worth living, you are still less likely to believe me.
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 38a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Notice what Socrates does *not* claim. He does not say that an unexamined life is wicked, or that everyone must become a professional philosopher. He says that examining your life (testing your beliefs about how to live against reasons and against other people's challenges) is itself part of living well. This course is built on that wager.

### Where the words come from

"Ethics" comes from the Greek *ēthikos*, "having to do with character" (*ēthos*), a word related to *ethos*, "custom" or "habit"; Aristotle built his theory of habituation on the connection (Chapter 5). Cicero coined the Latin equivalent, *moralis*, from *mores*, "customs," as he says at the start of *On Fate*. So "ethics" and "morality" began as synonyms. Some later philosophers pull them apart: Hegel contrasted *Moralität* (individual conscience) with *Sittlichkeit* (the ethical life of a community), and Bernard Williams (*Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*, 1985) used "morality" for a narrow modern system of obligation and blame that he thought distorted the broad Socratic question. In this course "ethics" is the broad inquiry and "morality" mostly means the part of it concerned with what we owe each other.

## The map: three levels of ethical inquiry

Suppose a friend has a serious illness and asks whether the latest scan looks bad. It does. Should you tell her the truth now, or wait until her doctor can? You can think about this at three levels.

**Applied ethics** asks the concrete question: should you, here, tell her? It draws on the details (what she wants to know, what the doctor advised, your relationship) and on general principles. Applied ethics includes bioethics (Chapter 19), the ethics of animals and the environment (Chapter 20) and technology ethics (Chapter 21). It is not a lesser branch; since about 1970 (the journal *Philosophy & Public Affairs* was founded in 1971) much of the best work in the field has been applied.

**Normative ethics** asks what *makes* an action right or wrong, a character good or bad, a life worth living. Is lying wrong because of its effects (it damages trust and causes pain), because of what it is (it treats the other person's reason as a tool), because an honest person would not do it, or because nobody could reasonably accept a principle allowing it? Those four answers are, roughly, the four great families of normative theory:

| Family | What fundamentally matters | Chapters |
|--------|----------------------------|----------|
| Consequentialism | The goodness of outcomes | 10–11 |
| Deontology | Duties, rights and constraints on what you may do | 9 |
| Virtue ethics | Character and the shape of a good life | 5, 13 |
| Contractualism | Principles that people could justify to each other | 12 |

Other approaches (natural law, care ethics, the Confucian and Buddhist traditions, sentimentalism, existentialism) cut across this table in instructive ways. You will meet each of them.

Within normative ethics philosophers also separate the **theory of the good** (axiology: what is valuable, for instance pleasure, knowledge, friendship, flourishing) from the **theory of the right** (what you ought to do, which may or may not be "produce as much good as possible"). How these two relate is one of the deepest structural questions in the subject, and much of the disagreement between consequentialists and deontologists lives here.

**Metaethics** steps back and asks what moral claims *are*. When you say "lying to her would be wrong," are you describing a fact, the way you describe the scan? If so, what kind of fact, and how could you know it? Or are you expressing an attitude, issuing a command, or making a claim that is systematically false? Metaethics divides into a semantic question (what do moral words mean?), a metaphysical one (are there moral facts or properties?), an epistemological one (how could we know them?) and a psychological one (why do moral judgments move us to act?). Chapter 17 covers the field; the nihilism course treats the skeptical options in depth in [its chapter on moral nihilism and error theory](../learn-nihilism/12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md).

The levels are connected: your verdicts about cases are the main evidence for or against normative theories, and your metaethics constrains which theories you can hold. Keep all three apart from **descriptive** ethics, the empirical study of what people actually believe and do. That a practice is common never by itself settles whether it is right (Chapter 3; Hume's "is" and "ought" in Chapter 8).

## What makes a question a moral one?

Morality is not the only system of norms in your life. There are laws, rules of etiquette, codes of honor, club rules, the norms of prudence (look after your own interests) and religious commandments. What distinguishes moral norms? Philosophers have proposed several marks, none uncontroversial:

1. **Normativity.** Moral claims tell you what you *ought* to do, not merely what people do. Sidgwick, whose *Methods of Ethics* (1874) is still the most careful survey of the field, defines a "method of ethics" this way:

> … a 'Method of Ethics' is explained to mean any rational procedure by which we determine what individual human beings 'ought'--or what it is 'right' for them--to do, or to seek to realise by voluntary action.
>
> — Henry Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, Introduction §1 (7th ed., 1907)

2. **Universalizability.** If an act is wrong for you, it is wrong for anyone relevantly like you in relevantly similar circumstances. "It's fine when I do it" is not a moral reason. (R. M. Hare made this the cornerstone of his theory; Kant's categorical imperative is an earlier relative, Chapter 9.)
3. **Impartiality.** Morality, many think, takes everyone's interests into account, not just yours or your group's. Others (care ethicists, Confucians, many virtue ethicists) think partiality toward family and friends is part of morality, not a departure from it (Chapters 7 and 14).
4. **Overridingness.** Moral reasons are supposed to beat other reasons when they conflict: you may not break a promise to save on a parking fee. Whether they *always* win is disputed; Susan Wolf's essay "Moral Saints" (1982) argues that a life governed entirely by morality would be unattractive.
5. **Content.** Some philosophers think morality must concern harm, welfare, fairness or respect; a "moral" rule about which hand to eat with would be a confusion. Others think any norm a person treats as categorical and universal counts.

Sidgwick noticed how easily these lines blur. Codes of honor and etiquette *feel* from the inside just like morality: you see at a glance what is polite as clearly as you see what is right. He drew a sobering conclusion:

> And even in the case of rules regarded as distinctly moral, we can generally find an element that seems to us as clearly conventional as the codes just mentioned, when we contemplate the morality of other men, even in our own age and country. Hence we may reasonably suspect a similar element in our own moral code …
>
> — Henry Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics* III.xi.2 (7th ed., 1907)

That suspicion is healthy. It is also the first step toward relativism, which Chapter 3 examines and, mostly, rejects.

## The data of ethics: considered judgments

Every science starts from observations. What does ethics start from? Mostly from **moral judgments about cases and principles** that strike you, after reflection, as clearly true: that torturing a child for fun is wrong; that you should not break a serious promise for a trivial gain; that the suffering of a stranger matters; that a person who risks her life to save others is admirable. Philosophers often call these **intuitions**. The word suggests a mysterious sixth sense, which is misleading. In current usage an intuition is simply an intellectual *seeming*: the proposition strikes you as true, independently of any argument you could give for it, in the way that "if A is taller than B and B taller than C, then A is taller than C" strikes you as true.

Some philosophers (W. D. Ross in *The Right and the Good*, 1930; more recently Robert Audi and Michael Huemer) defend **ethical intuitionism**: some moral propositions are self-evident, knowable by adequate understanding of them, though not obvious to everyone. Others treat intuitions more modestly, as defeasible evidence: a seeming that *p* gives you some reason to believe *p*, unless you have reason to think the seeming unreliable. Even philosophers who distrust intuitions use them all the time; a utilitarian who says "but surely the suffering itself is what matters" is appealing to one.

Intuitions are fallible. People once found slavery, the subjection of women and cruelty to animals intuitively acceptable. So the question is how to separate the judgments worth trusting from prejudice dressed up as insight. Sidgwick proposed four tests for a proposition that seems self-evident:

1. Its terms must be **clear and precise**.
2. Its self-evidence must be ascertained by **careful reflection**, not by impulse or habit.
3. The propositions you accept as self-evident must be **mutually consistent**.
4. They should not be contradicted by the judgment of other careful thinkers (**consensus of experts**).

On the second test he wrote one of the most useful warnings in moral philosophy:

> For, on the one hand, it cannot be denied that any strong sentiment, however purely subjective, is apt to transform itself into the semblance of an intuition; and it requires careful contemplation to detect the illusion. Whatever we desire we are apt to pronounce desirable …
>
> — Henry Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics* III.xi.2 (7th ed., 1907)

And on the fourth, why disagreement should shake you:

> Since it is implied in the very notion of Truth that it is essentially the same for all minds, the denial by another of a proposition that I have affirmed has a tendency to impair my confidence in its validity.
>
> — Henry Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics* III.xi.2 (7th ed., 1907)

Sidgwick's tests do not tell you what to believe. They tell you how to hold what you believe: with clarity, after reflection, consistently, and with an ear open to dissent. Chapter 2 takes up the problem of disagreement in more detail, and Chapter 18 looks at psychological evidence that some intuitions are shaped by factors (framing, disgust, the order in which cases are presented) that have nothing to do with their truth.

### How precise can ethics be?

Before the method, a warning about expectations. Aristotle opens the *Nicomachean Ethics* by telling his students not to expect geometry:

> We must be content then, in speaking of such things and from such data, to set forth the truth roughly and in outline; in other words, since we are speaking of general matter and from general data, to draw also conclusions merely general. And in the same spirit should each person receive what we say: for the man of education will seek exactness so far in each subject as the nature of the thing admits …
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.3, 1094b19–25 (tr. D. P. Chase)

This is not an excuse for vagueness. Aristotle thought that general ethical rules hold "for the most part," and that applying them to particular cases takes practical wisdom (*phronēsis*), which no rulebook supplies. He adds a remark about who should study ethics that is less flattering:

> Hence the young man is not a fit student of Moral Philosophy, for he has no experience in the actions of life, while all that is said presupposes and is concerned with these …
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.3, 1095a2–4 (tr. D. P. Chase)

He goes on to say that the problem is not age in years but living by passion rather than reason. The point stands for readers of any age: ethics is tested against your experience of life. Bring that experience to every chapter. And keep in mind Aristotle's statement of purpose, which this course shares:

> … for we are enquiring not merely that we may know what virtue is but that we may become virtuous, else it would have been useless …
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* II.2, 1103b27–29 (tr. D. P. Chase)

## Reflective equilibrium: the working method

How do you bring theory and considered judgments together? The method most moral philosophers use, whether or not they name it, is **reflective equilibrium**. The name is John Rawls's (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971, §4 and §9), building on his 1951 paper "Outline of a Decision Procedure for Ethics" and on Nelson Goodman's account of how we justify rules of inference in logic: we amend a rule if it yields an inference we are unwilling to accept, and reject an inference if it violates a rule we are unwilling to amend. The idea itself is much older.

The method, in steps:

1. **Collect considered judgments**: judgments you make with confidence, when you are calm, informed, and have no personal stake (Rawls's filter). Include judgments about particular cases ("it was wrong of him to sell the stolen car to his nephew") and about principles ("people should not profit from wrongdoing").
2. **Propose principles** that would explain and systematize those judgments.
3. **Test**: where principle and judgment conflict, revise one or the other. Sometimes a powerful principle shows that a judgment was a prejudice; sometimes a firm judgment shows that a principle is too crude.
4. **Iterate** until the principles and judgments support each other. The resulting state is the "equilibrium." It is "reflective" because you know which principles your judgments conform to and how you got there.

Rawls admits that the equilibrium is never final; new cases and arguments can upset it.

### Aristotle did it first

Aristotle states a version of the same method at the start of Book VII of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, before discussing weakness of will:

> And we should adopt the same course as before, i.e. state the phenomena, and, after raising and discussing difficulties which suggest themselves, then exhibit, if possible, all the opinions afloat respecting these affections of the moral character; or, if not all, the greater part and the most important: for we may consider we have illustrated the matter sufficiently when the difficulties have been solved, and such theories as are most approved are left as a residuum.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* VII.1, 1145b2–7 (tr. D. P. Chase)

The "phenomena" here are the *phainomena* ("appearances"), and the "opinions afloat" are *endoxa*, the reputable views held by most people or by the wise. G. E. L. Owen's classic paper "Tithenai ta phainomena" (1961) made this "method of appearances" famous; Jonathan Barnes and Martha Nussbaum have debated how conservative it is. Like reflective equilibrium, it treats both common opinion and theory as revisable, and counts it a success when the puzzles dissolve and the most defensible views survive.

### Narrow and wide

Norman Daniels (1979) distinguished **narrow** reflective equilibrium (coherence between your principles and your case judgments) from **wide** reflective equilibrium, which also brings in background theories: of human nature, of society, of rationality, of the function of morality, and the main rival normative theories with the arguments for each. Wide equilibrium is harder to reach and much harder to fake. You cannot simply build a theory that flatters your existing verdicts; it also has to fit what you know about psychology, economics, history and the other theories' best arguments.

### A worked example

Suppose you start with two confident judgments:

- (J1) It is wrong to let a child drown in front of you to avoid ruining your shoes.
- (J2) It is not wrong to spend money on new shoes rather than donating it to an effective charity.

And suppose you try the principle:

- (P) If you can prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, you ought to do it.

(P) explains (J1) beautifully. But (P) also seems to imply that (J2) is false, because the price of shoes could, through a well-chosen charity, prevent something very bad for a child far away. Now you must adjust. You could revise (J2) (Peter Singer's response, Chapter 11). You could restrict (P) to emergencies, or to people physically near you, and then you owe an account of why distance or immediacy matters morally. Or you could look for a deeper principle that explains why rescue cases are special (for instance, that you are the only one who can help). Each move has costs elsewhere in the system. That is what moral reasoning feels like from the inside, and it is the subject of the next chapter.

### Objections

Reflective equilibrium is popular, but it has serious critics.

- **Garbage in, garbage out.** R. M. Hare (reviewing Rawls in 1973) and Peter Singer ("Sidgwick and Reflective Equilibrium," *The Monist*, 1974) argued that the method gives authority to intuitions that may be products of upbringing, self-interest or evolutionary accident. Coherence among prejudices is still prejudice. Singer urged that we should be willing to discard common intuitions wholesale in favor of a self-evident axiom, as Sidgwick did with his principle of impartial benevolence.
- **Conservatism.** If you start from today's considered judgments, can you ever reach a radically better morality? Abolitionists did not get there by adjusting the received views of 1750 at the margins. Thomas Kelly and Sarah McGrath ("Is Reflective Equilibrium Enough?", 2010) argue that the method cannot by itself guarantee you end up anywhere reasonable.
- **Which equilibrium?** Two people may reach different, internally coherent equilibria. Coherence alone does not decide between them.

The best replies point out that there is no alternative that avoids these problems. The utilitarian who discards intuitions in favor of a self-evident axiom is relying on an intuition that the axiom is self-evident; the radical who overturns received morality usually does it by appealing to judgments the received morality already contains (slaves are human beings; pain is pain wherever it occurs). Wide reflective equilibrium, with its demand to engage rival theories and empirical knowledge, is the best defense against parochialism we have. Disagreement after that point is real, and Chapter 2 asks what to do with it.

## The ring of Gyges

Now to the challenge that has haunted ethics for twenty-four centuries. In Book I of the *Republic* Thrasymachus has argued, loudly, that morality is a racket:

> Listen, then, he said; I proclaim that justice is nothing else than the interest of the stronger.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* I, 338c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Socrates ties him up in knots, but Plato's brothers, Glaucon and Adeimantus, are not satisfied. Glaucon thinks Socrates has won the argument without answering the worry. So at the start of Book II he does something every philosophy student should learn from: he restates his opponent's position more strongly than its own champion did, in order to give Socrates something worth refuting.

### Three kinds of good

Glaucon begins by sorting goods:

> How would you arrange goods—are there not some which we welcome for their own sakes, and independently of their consequences, as, for example, harmless pleasures and enjoyments, which delight us at the time, although nothing follows from them?
>
> — Plato, *Republic* II, 357b (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

There are, he continues, goods we want both for themselves and for their results (knowledge, sight, health), and goods we want only for their results (exercise, medical treatment, money-making). Where does justice belong? Socrates puts it in the highest class: good in itself *and* for its consequences. Glaucon replies that most people put it in the third class, as something unpleasant that you put up with for the sake of reputation and rewards. And he asks Socrates to show that justice is good in itself:

> Setting aside their rewards and results, I want to know what they are in themselves, and how they inwardly work in the soul.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* II, 358b (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

### The origin of justice: a social contract

Glaucon then gives "the common view" of where justice comes from. It is the first social contract theory in Western literature, two thousand years before Hobbes (Chapters 3 and 12):

> They say that to do injustice is, by nature, good; to suffer injustice, evil; but that the evil is greater than the good. And so when men have both done and suffered injustice and have had experience of both, not being able to avoid the one and obtain the other, they think that they had better agree among themselves to have neither; hence there arise laws and mutual covenants; and that which is ordained by law is termed by them lawful and just.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* II, 358e–359a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Justice, on this view, is a compromise between the best outcome (doing injustice with impunity) and the worst (suffering it without redress).

### The ring

To show that people are just only "of necessity," because they fear the consequences of injustice, Glaucon tells a story. A shepherd named Gyges, serving the king of Lydia, finds a chasm opened by an earthquake. Inside is a bronze horse; inside the horse, a giant corpse wearing a gold ring. Gyges takes the ring.

> … into their assembly he came having the ring on his finger, and as he was sitting among them he chanced to turn the collet of the ring inside his hand, when instantly he became invisible to the rest of the company and they began to speak of him as if he were no longer present.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* II, 359e–360a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

He tests it, it works every time, and he acts at once: he gets himself sent to court, seduces the queen, kills the king and takes the kingdom. Then comes the thought experiment proper:

> Suppose now that there were two such magic rings, and the just put on one of them and the unjust the other; no man can be imagined to be of such an iron nature that he would stand fast in justice. No man would keep his hands off what was not his own when he could safely take what he liked out of the market, or go into houses and lie with any one at his pleasure, or kill or release from prison whom he would, and in all respects be like a God among men.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* II, 360b–c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

And the sting:

> If you could imagine any one obtaining this power of becoming invisible, and never doing any wrong or touching what was another's, he would be thought by the lookers-on to be a most wretched idiot, although they would praise him to one another's faces, and keep up appearances with one another from a fear that they too might suffer injustice.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* II, 360d (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

### The other Gyges

Herodotus, writing a generation before Plato, tells a different story of how Gyges became king of Lydia, with no ring at all. King Candaules is so obsessed with his wife's beauty that he insists his bodyguard Gyges hide in the bedroom to see her undress. Gyges protests, quoting an old saying:

> Moreover of old time those fair sayings have been found out by men, from which we ought to learn wisdom; and of these one is this,—that each man should look on his own …
>
> — Herodotus, *Histories* I.8 (tr. G. C. Macaulay)

He obeys anyway, and the queen sees him slip out. The next morning she summons him and offers a choice: kill Candaules and take the throne, or die. He chooses to live. The contrast with Plato's version is instructive. In Herodotus the man who thinks he is unseen *is* seen, and the consequences of a hidden wrong come out. Plato's Glaucon removes exactly that possibility. The ring guarantees that you will never be caught, so that the only remaining question is whether justice is worth having for its own sake.

### The perfectly just and the perfectly unjust

Glaucon sharpens the test further. Imagine, he says, the perfectly unjust man, who commits the greatest crimes while keeping a perfect reputation for justice, "for the highest reach of injustice is, to be deemed just when you are not" (361a). Next to him, imagine the perfectly just man, who is thought to be the worst of men:

> Let him be the best of men, and let him be thought the worst; then he will have been put to the proof; and we shall see whether he will be affected by the fear of infamy and its consequences.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* II, 361c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

The just man is to be whipped, tortured and finally impaled (361e; Christian readers from the early church onward have seen an eerie anticipation of the crucifixion here). Which of the two lives is better? Adeimantus then adds that even the defenders of justice, parents and poets, praise it only for its rewards and reputation, never for what it does in the soul. Socrates has to show that justice is better *for its possessor* even under the worst conditions.

### The challenge as an argument

Set out formally, Glaucon's challenge is something like this:

1. A person has reason to be just only if being just is good for that person.
2. Justice is good for a person either (a) in itself or (b) because of its consequences, such as reputation, rewards, and avoiding punishment.
3. With the ring (or with a perfect reputation for injustice), all the consequences in (b) can be had without being just, and all the penalties of injustice can be avoided.
4. So if justice is not good *in itself* for its possessor, the ring-wearer has no reason to be just.
5. Most people believe, "in their hearts," that justice is not good in itself.
6. So, by their own lights, most people have no reason to be just when they can safely be unjust.

Premise 1 is the crucial one, and it is the one many modern philosophers deny. But notice how much depends on it. If you accept it, you have to show that morality pays, at least in the deepest sense of "pays." If you reject it, you owe an account of what other kind of reason there could be.

## Answers to "Why be moral?"

### Plato's answer: justice is the health of the soul

The rest of the *Republic* is Socrates's answer. In outline (Chapter 5 gives more detail): the soul has three parts, reason, spirit (*thumos*) and appetite, and justice is the condition in which each part does its own work, with reason ruling. Injustice is civil war inside you. By Book IV Socrates can draw the conclusion:

> Then virtue is the health and beauty and well-being of the soul, and vice the disease and weakness and deformity of the same?
>
> — Plato, *Republic* IV, 444d–e (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Glaucon now finds his own question absurd. If nobody would want to live with a ruined body, however rich and powerful, why would anyone want to live with a ruined soul?

> … and shall we be told that when the very essence of the vital principle is undermined and corrupted, life is still worth having to a man, if only he be allowed to do whatever he likes with the single exception that he is not to acquire justice and virtue …
>
> — Plato, *Republic* IV, 445a–b (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Book IX adds a portrait of the tyrant, the ring-wearer in real life, as the most enslaved and fearful of men, and Book X finally returns to the ring:

> … but justice in her own nature has been shown to be best for the soul in her own nature. Let a man do what is just, whether he have the ring of Gyges or not, and even if in addition to the ring of Gyges he put on the helmet of Hades.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* X, 612b (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

**The classic objection.** In "A Fallacy in Plato's Republic" (*Philosophical Review*, 1963) David Sachs pointed out that Socrates seems to change the subject. Glaucon asked about "vulgar" justice: not stealing, not lying, not killing. Socrates proves that "Platonic" justice, inner psychic harmony, is good for you. But why should a well-ordered soul refrain from stealing? Couldn't a harmonious, reason-governed person also be a calm and efficient embezzler? Defenders of Plato (Richard Kraut, Gregory Vlastos, Julia Annas among them) answer that Plato's reason is not mere calculation: it loves and knows the good, and a soul ruled by such a reason will not be moved by the appetites that drive theft and fraud. Whether that answer works depends on accepting Plato's demanding picture of reason, which Chapter 5 explores.

### Hume's sensible knave

David Hume gave the challenge an eighteenth-century face. At the end of his *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751) he imagines a person who agrees that honesty is generally a good policy but wants to make an exception whenever cheating is safe:

> … a sensible knave, in particular incidents, may think that an act of iniquity or infidelity will make a considerable addition to his fortune, without causing any considerable breach in the social union and confederacy. That HONESTY IS THE BEST POLICY, may be a good general rule, but is liable to many exceptions; and he, it may perhaps be thought, conducts himself with most wisdom, who observes the general rule, and takes advantage of all the exceptions.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* IX.ii (1751)

Hume's reply is remarkably candid:

> I must confess that, if a man think that this reasoning much requires an answer, it would be a little difficult to find any which will to him appear satisfactory and convincing. If his heart rebel not against such pernicious maxims, if he feel no reluctance to the thoughts of villainy or baseness, he has indeed lost a considerable motive to virtue …
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* IX.ii (1751)

For the rest of us, he says, "inward peace of mind, consciousness of integrity, a satisfactory review of our own conduct" are worth more than any gain from cheating, and knaves tend to get caught by their own cleverness anyway. Notice the shape of the answer. Hume does not claim to *argue* the knave into virtue. He says that the reasons for virtue speak to people with certain feelings, and most of us have those feelings. Chapter 8 shows how this fits Hume's whole theory of morals.

### Other answers

The question "Why be moral?" has received at least five other kinds of answer, which this course examines in turn:

- **Morality is in your interest after all** (enlightened self-interest). Hobbes's reply to "the Fool" who says there is no such thing as justice, and David Gauthier's contractarianism, argue that a disposition to keep agreements pays better than a disposition to break them when convenient, because others can detect and exclude cheaters (Chapters 3 and 12).
- **Virtue is part of your flourishing.** Aristotle and the Stoics, like Plato, argue that the good life for a human being simply *is* the virtuous life (Chapters 5–6).
- **Reason itself requires it.** Kant argues that the moral law is a requirement of practical reason as such; the knave is not merely nasty but irrational (Chapter 9).
- **The question is confused.** H. A. Prichard argued in 1912 that "Why should I do what I ought?" asks for a non-moral reason (usually self-interest) for doing what you already have conclusive moral reason to do. Philippa Foot ("Morality as a System of Hypothetical Imperatives," 1972) took the opposite tack: moral "oughts" bind only those who care about morality's ends, which is why the knave escapes. She later abandoned this view.
- **There is no answer, and that is fine or not fine.** Bernard Williams (*Morality*, 1972, chapter 1) concludes that no argument can force the "amoralist," though such a person is harder to imagine consistently than he first appears, since he relies on others' moral responses. The nihilism course follows the more radical denials of moral reasons ([Chapter 1 of that course](../learn-nihilism/01-what-is-nihilism.md)).

### What the ring reveals about you

Glaucon claimed that "all men believe in their hearts that injustice is far more profitable to the individual than justice" (360c). Is he right? Social psychology offers partial evidence. In a well-known experiment by C. Daniel Batson and colleagues ("In a Very Different Voice: Unmasking Moral Hypocrisy," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1997), participants had to assign themselves and another person to a pleasant or a dull task. Most agreed that flipping a coin was the fair way to decide. About half of them flipped the coin privately, and among those who did, the vast majority still assigned themselves the pleasant task, far more than chance allows. Batson called this "moral hypocrisy": wanting to *appear* fair while avoiding the cost of *being* fair. That is exactly the gap Glaucon pried open.

But the same literature also finds people who do not cheat when they could, and the trait that best predicts it is not fear but self-image: they care about being the kind of person who does not cheat. That is closer to Plato's and Hume's answer than to Glaucon's. The ring tests not your theory of morality but your character.

## Living it

Two exercises tie this chapter to your own life.

**The ring inventory.** For one week, at the end of each day, write down every choice you made where you would have acted differently if nobody could ever find out. Be honest; nobody will see the list, which is the point. Then, for each item, ask Glaucon's question: was what you did good *in itself*, or only for its consequences? You will probably find three kinds of entries: cases where only fear of discovery held you back (Glaucon was right about you there), cases where you acted well though discovery was impossible (what moved you?), and cases where you are not sure. The third kind is where ethical thinking starts.

**Your first equilibrium.** Write down five moral judgments you are confident about, at least two about particular cases and at least two about general principles. Then look for a conflict between any two of them. (There almost always is one: for instance, between "people should keep their promises" and "I was right to break my promise to help my sister move when my friend was in the hospital.") Try to state a principle that explains both. If you cannot, decide which to revise, and write down why. Keep this page; you will return to it in Chapter 22.

Socrates's claim in the *Apology* was that this kind of self-examination is not a burden added to life but part of what makes a life worth living. The exercises are a small test of that claim.

## Summary

- Ethics asks how you should live; you answer its questions by acting, whether or not you reflect.
- "Ethics" (Greek *ēthos*, character) and "morality" (Latin *mores*, customs) began as synonyms; some philosophers now distinguish the broad question of how to live from a narrower system of obligation.
- The three levels: **applied** ethics (concrete issues), **normative** ethics (what makes things right, wrong, good or bad: consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, contractualism and others), **metaethics** (what moral claims are and whether they can be true or known). Descriptive ethics is a separate, empirical study.
- Proposed marks of the moral: normativity, universalizability, impartiality, overridingness, content. Each is contested.
- Considered judgments ("intuitions") are the main data of ethics. Sidgwick's tests: clarity, careful reflection, consistency, consensus.
- Aristotle: ethics is precise only "roughly and in outline," and its purpose is to become good, not just to know.
- **Reflective equilibrium** (Rawls, after Goodman; anticipated by Aristotle's method of *endoxa*) adjusts principles and judgments against each other; **wide** equilibrium (Daniels) also brings in background theories. Objections: garbage in, garbage out; conservatism; multiple equilibria.
- **The ring of Gyges** (*Republic* II): if you could be unjust undetected, would you have any reason to be just? Glaucon's challenge asks for justice to be shown good *in itself* for its possessor.
- Answers: Plato (justice is the soul's health; objection: Sachs's "fallacy"), Hume (the knave cannot be argued round, but most of us value integrity), Hobbes and Gauthier (morality pays), Aristotle and the Stoics (virtue is flourishing), Kant (reason demands it), Prichard (the question is confused), Williams (philosophy cannot compel the amoralist).

### Dialogue 1 — On whether you would turn the ring

**Glaucon:** Suppose you found the ring of Gyges this morning, tested it, and it works. No one could ever see you or trace anything to you. What would you do with it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Glaucon in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Honestly, I'd use it for small things at first, maybe to skip a few queues or listen in on what people really think of me. But I wouldn't steal or hurt anyone.

**Glaucon:** Why not? You have just admitted that the small things are tempting. What is the difference between skipping a queue invisibly and taking a loaf of bread invisibly from a rich baker who will never miss it?

**You:** Stealing takes something that belongs to someone else. Skipping a queue doesn't really harm anyone.

**Glaucon:** The person behind you waits longer by exactly the time you saved. You have taken something of hers. But leave that aside. You say you would not steal. Is that because stealing is bad for the person robbed, or because it would be bad for *you*?

**You:** Because it's bad for the person robbed. It's wrong.

**Glaucon:** Then my question to Socrates applies to you. I granted that injustice harms its victims; everyone grants that. I asked why the *unjust person* should care, when he is safe. You have told me you would not steal. You have not told me why that is good for you rather than a sacrifice you make.

**You:** Maybe it isn't good for me. Maybe I just think I shouldn't do it.

**Socrates:** That is Prichard's answer, Glaucon, though he was not born for another two thousand years: that "I ought not" needs no further "because it pays." Are you satisfied with it?

**Glaucon:** Not entirely. If our friend's "I shouldn't" has no root in what is good for him, what holds it in place when temptation is strong? People abandon principles they cannot explain.

**Socrates:** Let us ask a different question, then. Would you want to *become* the person who uses the ring for everything he can get away with? Picture yourself ten years on.

**You:** No. That person would be lonely. He'd never be able to trust anyone, because he'd know what he himself does when no one is looking. And he'd stop being able to enjoy anything honestly earned.

**Socrates:** So the ring-wearer loses something, even if he is never caught.

**You:** Yes, though I'm not sure it's the same thing as justice. It's more like losing the ability to have certain relationships and certain kinds of pride.

**Glaucon:** You see, Socrates? He describes a cost, but a cost in the currency of consequences: loneliness, mistrust. I asked for justice good in itself.

**Socrates:** Perhaps the line between "in itself" and "in its consequences" is less sharp than your three classes suggested. Health is good in itself and for its results; and the "results" of health include the capacity to do and enjoy many things. If justice is a condition of the soul that makes trust, friendship and honest pride possible, are those mere consequences, or are they part of what it is to have a just soul?

**You:** I think they're part of it. Being just isn't a separate thing that then causes trust; being a person others can trust is part of what being just is.

**Socrates:** Then we have made some progress. We have not yet shown that justice is better than injustice in every possible circumstance; Glaucon's impaled just man still waits for an answer. But we have seen that the ring tests what kind of person you are, and that the answer is not neutral between lives you would and would not want to live. Hold on to that when you read Book IV.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether intuitions are evidence

**Sidgwick:** You say you are "certain" that it is wrong to break a promise to a dying man, even when no one will ever know and the money could do more good elsewhere. What makes you certain?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Sidgwick in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It just seems obviously wrong. When I imagine doing it, everything in me says no.

**Sidgwick:** Everything in you says no. I believe you. But in my *Methods* I warned that "any strong sentiment, however purely subjective, is apt to transform itself into the semblance of an intuition." How do you know this is an intuition rather than a strong sentiment?

**You:** I suppose I don't know for sure. But how else would I find out what's right, if not by what seems right to me after thinking?

**Sidgwick:** A fair question. I proposed some tests. First, are your terms clear? What exactly is the principle: "never break a promise"? "Never break a promise to the dying"? "Never break a promise when no one will know"?

**You:** Something like: you should keep serious promises, especially to people who can no longer hold you to them.

**Sidgwick:** Good; that is clearer, and more interesting, because it says the promisee's helplessness *increases* the obligation. Second test: does this survive careful reflection? Suppose the promise was to give his fortune to his racehorse-loving son, and you know the son will gamble it away, while a hospital could save dozens of lives with it.

**You:** That makes me less sure. But I still think something would be lost if I broke the promise, even if breaking it is the right thing to do.

**Sidgwick:** Then perhaps your intuition was not "this must never be done" but "this has real weight." That is a weaker claim, and much more likely to be true. W. D. Ross, who came after me, called such things *prima facie* duties. Third test: is it consistent with your other intuitions? You also believe, I imagine, that suffering matters and that one should prevent it when one can at small cost.

**You:** Yes.

**Sidgwick:** Then you have two intuitions that conflict in this case, and by my third test at least one of them, as you formulated it, is wrong. My own view was that the deepest consistent principle is impartial benevolence, and that the rules of common-sense morality, like keeping promises, are mostly justified by their usefulness.

**You:** But then the intuition about the promise is just being overruled by another intuition. Why should the benevolence one win?

**Sidgwick:** That is exactly the right question, and I could not answer it to my own satisfaction. I ended my book admitting a "dualism of the practical reason" that I could not resolve. What I can tell you is this: your seeming that the promise matters is evidence, not proof. It counts. It can be outweighed. And the only way to weigh it is the work you have just been doing, which Rawls later called reflective equilibrium.

**You:** So I shouldn't throw out the intuition, but I shouldn't treat it as the last word either.

**Sidgwick:** And you should notice the fourth test. Most careful thinkers in most cultures agree that promises bind; few agree that they bind absolutely. That pattern of agreement and disagreement is itself information. You have arrived at a modest and defensible position. In philosophy that is no small thing.

</details>
