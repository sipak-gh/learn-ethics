# Chapter 6 — Hellenistic Ethics

In 323 BCE Alexander the Great died in Babylon at thirty-two, and the world of the independent Greek city collapsed into a patchwork of vast, unstable kingdoms ruled by his generals. Aristotle, his former tutor, fled Athens the next year and died soon after. The philosophers who came next lived in a world where an ordinary citizen had little say over war, taxes, or exile, and they changed the question. Aristotle had asked what a flourishing life looks like, assuming a good city to live it in. The new schools asked how a person can live well *whatever happens*: under a tyrant, in poverty, in pain, facing death. Their answer was that philosophy is medicine for the soul. This chapter examines three of these "therapies": the Epicurean, which treats anxiety by teaching us to want less and fear nothing; the Stoic, which treats it by teaching that only virtue is good (covered briefly here, since the [Stoicism course](../learn-stoicism/01-why-stoicism.md) covers it in depth); and the Skeptic, which treats it by suspending belief altogether. The chapter closes by asking whether ethics *should* be therapy at all. Previous: [Chapter 5 — Ancient Virtue Ethics](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md). Next: [Chapter 7 — Confucian, Buddhist, and Other Traditions](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md).

## Philosophy as medicine

The Hellenistic schools agreed on a picture of the human condition. Most people are sick. They are not sick in body. They are sick in what they believe and want. They chase money, fame, and pleasure, fear death and the gods, rage at insults, and grieve without limit, and all of this makes them miserable. The cause, in each case, is a mistaken belief: that wealth is necessary for happiness, that death is bad for the one who dies, that the gods punish, that an insult harms. The cure is to change the belief, and the doctor who can do that is the philosopher.

Epicurus put the point in a saying preserved by the later Platonist Porphyry. Paraphrased, it runs: empty is the argument of a philosopher that heals no human suffering, for just as medicine is useless if it does not drive out bodily disease, philosophy is useless if it does not drive out the suffering of the soul. The Stoic Chrysippus wrote a whole book called *Therapeutics*. Cicero opened the third book of his *Tusculan Disputations* by calling philosophy the medicine of the soul.

Martha Nussbaum's *The Therapy of Desire* takes this medical analogy seriously and lists what it implies. Medical arguments, unlike Platonic ones, aim at the patient's health rather than abstract truth. They are fitted to the particular patient, so a good philosopher speaks differently to different students. They attend to the patient's emotions and desires, not only their reasoning. And they may use any technique that works: memorization, repetition, vivid imagery, confession, daily review, even the authority of the teacher. Nussbaum admires much of this, but she also worries about it, and her worries will be the subject of the last section.

Pierre Hadot pressed the point further. Ancient philosophy, he argued, was not mainly a body of theory but a *way of life*, sustained by "spiritual exercises": meditating on death, viewing one's troubles from above, rehearsing misfortunes in advance, reviewing one's day each evening. A Hellenistic school was less like a modern philosophy department than like a monastery or a twelve-step program. You joined it to change your life.

### A note on the Cynics

The most dramatic version of philosophy as a way of life came first. Diogenes of Sinope (c. 412–323 BCE), called "the dog," lived, according to the anecdotes Diogenes Laertius collected, in a storage jar in the Athenian marketplace, ate and masturbated in public, and mocked every convention he could find. His point was that most of what people want is not natural but merely customary, and that a person who strips away convention can be free and self-sufficient. Diogenes Laertius records his most famous encounter:

> Once, while he was sitting in the sun in the Craneum, Alexander was standing by, and said to him, “Ask any favour you choose of me.” And he replied, “Cease to shade me from the sun.”
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* VI.38 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

Asked where he came from, he answered with a word that would outlive him:

> The question was put to him what countryman he was, and he replied, “A Citizen of the world.”
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* VI.63 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

The Cynics were more performance than system, but they gave the Stoics two ideas: that virtue suffices for happiness and that the wise person's city is the whole world. The [Stoicism course](../learn-stoicism/02-before-the-stoa.md) traces the line from Diogenes to Zeno, and the [philosophy course](../learn-philosophy/07-hellenistic-philosophy.md) places all these schools in their wider intellectual setting.

## Epicurus: pleasure, rightly understood

Epicurus (341–270 BCE) bought a house with a garden outside Athens around 306 BCE and founded a community there that admitted women and slaves, which scandalized his contemporaries. Over the gate, according to Seneca, was an inscription inviting the stranger to stay, since here the highest good was pleasure. His enemies took that at face value and spread stories of orgies. The word "epicure," meaning a gourmet, is their victory. The truth was almost the opposite. Diogenes Laertius reports that Epicurus said in his letters that he was content with water and plain bread, and asked a friend to send him a little pot of cheese so that he could have a feast when he wanted one (X.11).

Epicurus' ethics is hedonism. Pleasure is the only thing good in itself and pain the only thing bad in itself. His argument is that every animal, from birth and before it is corrupted by opinion, seeks pleasure and avoids pain. Nature itself shows us the good. In his *Letter to Menoeceus*, which is preserved in Diogenes Laertius and is the best short introduction to his ethics, he writes:

> and on this account, we affirm, that pleasure is the beginning and end of living happily; for we have recognized this as the first good, being connate with us; and with reference to it, it is that we begin every choice and avoidance...
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.128–129, Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus* (tr. C. D. Yonge)

But hedonism does not mean grabbing every pleasure. Some pleasures bring greater pains, and some pains bring greater pleasures. So the hedonist must calculate:

> Every pleasure is therefore a good on account of its own nature, but it does not follow that every pleasure is worthy of being chosen; just as every pain is an evil, and yet every pain must not be avoided.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.129, Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus* (tr. C. D. Yonge)

### The kinds of pleasure

The key to Epicurus' version of hedonism is a distinction between two kinds of pleasure. **Kinetic** pleasures are the pleasures of motion and change: the taste of food as you eat it, the feeling of scratching an itch. **Katastematic** ("static") pleasures are the pleasures of a settled state: the condition of being well fed, untroubled, healthy. Epicurus held that the katastematic pleasures are the highest, and that the highest of all is the complete absence of pain in the body (*aponia*) and of disturbance in the mind (*ataraxia*):

> When, therefore, we say that pleasure is a chief good, we are not speaking of the pleasures of the debauched man, or those which lie in sensual enjoyment, as some think who are ignorant, and who do not entertain our opinions, or else interpret them perversely; but we mean the freedom of the body from pain, and of the soul from confusion.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.131, Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus* (tr. C. D. Yonge)

This is a striking claim, and critics from Cicero onward have pounced on it. Isn't the absence of pain simply a neutral state? Is a dreamless sleep the best pleasure? Epicurus' answer seems to be that a healthy, untroubled creature *enjoys* its own condition. Being alive, free of pain and fear, is itself pleasant, and kinetic pleasures merely "vary" this pleasure without increasing it. Anyone who has felt the relief of a headache ending, or the quiet happiness of an ordinary morning with nothing wrong, has some sense of what he meant. Principal Doctrine 3 makes it explicit:

> The limit of the greatness of the pleasures is the removal of everything which can give pain.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.139, Epicurus, *Principal Doctrines* 3 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

This has a remarkable consequence: pleasure has a *ceiling*. Once you are free from pain and fear, nothing can make you happier. Luxury adds variety, not quantity. And since freedom from pain and fear is easy to achieve, happiness is within everyone's reach. That is the core of the Epicurean therapy.

### Three kinds of desire

To reach that ceiling you must manage your desires, and Epicurus offers a classification that is still useful. (Yonge's translation numbers the *Principal Doctrines* slightly differently from modern editions. This chapter uses the standard numbering.)

> “Of the desires, some are natural and necessary, some natural, but not necessary, and some are neither natural nor necessary, but owe their existence to vain opinions.”
>
> (Epicurus thinks that those are natural and necessary which put an end to pains, as drink when one is thirsty; and that those are natural but not necessary which only diversify pleasure, but do not remove pain, such as expensive food; and that these are neither natural nor necessary, which are such as crowns, or the erection of statues.)
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.149, Epicurus, *Principal Doctrines* 29 with scholium (tr. C. D. Yonge)

The three classes are these:

1. **Natural and necessary** desires, for food, drink, shelter, and the like, as well as for friendship and philosophy, which are necessary for happiness. Satisfy them. They are easy to satisfy.
2. **Natural but unnecessary** desires, for fine food, sex, and comfort. Enjoy them when they come, but do not depend on them.
3. **Empty** (vain) desires, for wealth, power, fame, and immortality. Eliminate them. They have no natural limit, so they can never be satisfied, and the effort to satisfy them is a source of endless anxiety. They are Socrates' leaky jars from [Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md).

Epicurus' practical advice follows:

> And simple flavours give as much pleasure as costly fare, when everything that can give pain, and every feeling of want, is removed; and corn and water give the most extreme pleasure when any one in need eats them. To accustom one’s self, therefore, to simple and inexpensive habits is a great ingredient in the perfecting of health, and makes a man free from hesitation with respect to the necessary uses of life.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.130–131, Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus* (tr. C. D. Yonge)

The argument is not that luxury is wicked. It is that dependence on luxury is a vulnerability. If you can be happy with bread and water, then losing your fortune cannot touch your happiness, and when a feast does come along you can enjoy it without anxiety. Modern psychology has rediscovered parts of this argument. Research on materialism consistently finds that people who place high value on wealth, image, and status report lower well-being, and the "hedonic treadmill" ensures that each new luxury soon becomes the baseline.

### Virtue, justice, and friendship

If pleasure is the only good, why be virtuous? Epicurus' answer is instrumental but firm. Virtue is the indispensable means to a pleasant life:

> teaching us that it is not possible to live pleasantly unless one also lives prudently, and honourably, and justly; and that one cannot live prudently, and honestly, and justly, without living pleasantly...
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.132, Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus* (tr. C. D. Yonge)

Justice, in particular, is a social contract, a mutual agreement not to harm or be harmed. It has no existence apart from such agreements:

> Natural justice is a covenant of what is suitable, leading men to avoid injuring one another, and being injured.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.150, Epicurus, *Principal Doctrines* 31 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

Why not break the agreement in secret, like Gyges? Because you can never be sure you will not be caught, and the fear of discovery would destroy your *ataraxia*:

> It is not possible for a man who secretly does anything in contravention of the agreement which men have made with one another, to guard against doing, or sustaining mutual injury, to believe that he shall always escape notice, even if he have escaped notice already ten thousand times...
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.151, Epicurus, *Principal Doctrines* 35 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

This is ingenious, but critics have found it thin. It seems to imply that a perfectly secure Gyges, who could be certain of never being caught, would have no reason to be just. The Epicurean answer is that no human being can have that certainty, and that the person who even entertains such plans has already lost peace of mind. Whether that is enough is the question of [Chapter 1](01-what-is-ethics.md) all over again. Note, too, that Epicurus anticipates the contract theories of Hobbes and Rawls ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)) by two thousand years.

On friendship, Epicurus is warmer, and the tension in his view is more interesting:

> Of all the things which wisdom provides for the happiness of the whole life, by far the most important is the acquisition of friendship.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.148, Epicurus, *Principal Doctrines* 27 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

The Garden was, above all, a community of friends. Yet if pleasure is the only intrinsic good, a friend can be valued only as a source of pleasure or security, which sounds like Aristotle's lowest kind of friendship. Cicero's Epicurean spokesman in *De Finibus* I admits that Epicureans disagreed among themselves about this. Some said friendship begins in utility but grows into a love of the friend for their own sake. Others said the wise make a kind of pact to love their friends as themselves. Epicurus himself reportedly said that the wise person will sometimes die for a friend. A hedonism that can say that has stretched itself very far.

## Death is nothing to us

The most famous Epicurean argument concerns death. Epicurus thought that fear of death, together with fear of the gods, was the greatest source of human misery. His physics, an atomism inherited from Democritus, taught that the soul is made of fine atoms that disperse when we die. There is no afterlife and no punishment. So:

> Death is nothing to us; for that which is dissolved is devoid of sensation, and that which is devoid of sensation is nothing to us.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.139, Epicurus, *Principal Doctrines* 2 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

The *Letter to Menoeceus* spells out the argument:

> Therefore, the most formidable of all evils, death, is nothing to us, since, when we exist, death is not present to us; and when death is present, then we have no existence. It is no concern then either of the living or of the dead; since to the one it has no existence, and the other class has no existence itself.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* X.125, Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus* (tr. C. D. Yonge)

Put formally:

1. Something can be bad for a person only if the person can experience it.
2. Once dead, a person cannot experience anything.
3. So being dead is not bad for the person who is dead.
4. It is irrational to fear what is not bad for you.
5. So it is irrational to fear being dead.

The Roman poet Lucretius, whose *On the Nature of Things* (c. 55 BCE) set Epicurean physics and ethics to Latin verse, added a second argument, the **symmetry argument**. We were not troubled by the eternity of time before we were born. The eternity after our death is exactly like it:

> Therefore death to us  
> Is nothing, nor concerns us in the least,  
> Since nature of mind is mortal evermore.  
> And just as in the ages gone before  
> We felt no touch of ill, when all sides round  
> To battle came the Carthaginian host...
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* III.830–834 (tr. William Ellery Leonard)

And later:

> Look back:  
> Nothing to us was all fore-passed eld  
> Of time the eternal, ere we had a birth.  
> And Nature holds this like a mirror up  
> Of time-to-be when we are dead and gone.  
> And what is there so horrible appears?
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* III.972–977 (tr. William Ellery Leonard)

### Is death bad for the one who dies?

These arguments have been debated with great energy in modern philosophy, and most philosophers think they fail, though there is no agreement about why.

The leading reply is Thomas Nagel's **deprivation account**, set out in his 1970 essay "Death." Death is bad for the one who dies not because it is experienced as unpleasant but because it *deprives* them of the goods that more life would have contained. Nagel supports premise 1's rejection with examples. A man who is betrayed by his friends behind his back, and never finds out, has suffered a misfortune, even though he experiences nothing. An intelligent adult reduced by brain injury to the mental condition of a contented infant has suffered a terrible loss, though the infant-like person is not unhappy. So something can be bad for you without being experienced.

The deprivation account faces two counter-objections. **When** is death bad for you? Not while you are alive, since you are not yet dead, and not after you are dead, since you do not exist. Defenders reply that it is bad for you timelessly, the way it is timelessly true that you would have been better off with more life. And what about **symmetry**? If death is bad because it deprives you of future life, why isn't prenatal nonexistence bad because it deprived you of earlier life? Nagel's answer is that you could not have been born much earlier and still been *you*, while you could have died later. Others, notably Frederik Kaufman, have questioned that reply. Still others, following Derek Parfit, suggest that our asymmetric attitude simply reflects a bias toward the future, which may or may not be rational.

Bernard Williams added a twist in "The Makropulos Case" (1973). He agreed with Nagel against Epicurus, but argued that *immortality* would be bad too. A person with a fixed character would eventually exhaust everything that could interest them and be overwhelmed by boredom. If so, the Epicurean is right to free us from the longing for endless life, even if wrong to say death is nothing.

Even if the Epicurean argument fails as a proof, its therapeutic purpose may succeed. Many people find, as Epicurus hoped, that rehearsing the symmetry argument does ease the dread. Epicurus would say that is what matters. An argument is a medicine, and a medicine is tested by whether it cures.

### Fleeing ourselves

Lucretius is also an acute observer of the restlessness that comes from unexamined fear. In a passage that sounds as if it were written about the modern commuter, he describes the rich Roman who cannot sit still:

> The man who sickens of his home goes out,  
> Forth from his splendid halls, and straight--returns,  
> Feeling i'faith no better off abroad.  
> He races, driving his Gallic ponies along,  
> Down to his villa, madly,--as in haste  
> To hurry help to a house afire.--At once  
> He yawns, as soon as foot has touched the threshold...
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* III.1060–1066 (tr. William Ellery Leonard)

His diagnosis follows a few lines later: "Each human flees himself." The cure, for Lucretius as for Epicurus, is understanding. Once we grasp the nature of things, including our own mortality, the restlessness stops.

## The Stoics, briefly

The Stoic school, founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BCE and systematized by Chrysippus, was the Epicureans' great rival and became the most influential philosophy of the Roman world. Because the [Stoicism course](../learn-stoicism/01-why-stoicism.md) covers it in depth, this section gives only an outline, chiefly to contrast it with the other two therapies.

**Virtue is the only good.** Where Epicurus said that pleasure is the only good and virtue its means, the Stoics said that virtue is the *only* good and vice the only evil. Health, wealth, reputation, and even life itself are "indifferents." Some are "preferred" and it is natural to select them, but they add nothing to happiness. This is a sharper version of the Socratic claim that a good person cannot be harmed. The details, including the doctrine of preferred indifferents and the Stoic paradoxes, are in [Virtue and the Good](../learn-stoicism/06-virtue-and-the-good.md).

**Oikeiōsis.** The Stoics, like the Epicureans, began from the behavior of newborn animals, but drew a different conclusion. Cicero's Stoic spokesman Cato explains:

> V. Those philosophers, said he, whose system I approve of, consider that as soon as an animal is born, (for this is where we must begin,) he is instinctively induced and excited to preserve himself and his existing condition, and to feel attachment to those things which have a tendency to preserve that condition...
>
> — Cicero, *De Finibus* III.16 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

The first impulse is not toward pleasure but toward *self-preservation*. This "appropriation" (*oikeiōsis*) of oneself develops, as reason matures, into an appropriation of reason itself, and then outward to family, city, and all humanity. The later Stoic Hierocles pictured it as a set of concentric circles, with the self at the center and the whole human race at the edge, and urged us to draw each circle inward. This is the root of Stoic cosmopolitanism, and through it of modern ideas of universal human rights.

**The passions are false judgments.** Anger, fear, grief, and craving are not blind forces but beliefs: that something bad has happened or is coming, or that something good is within reach, *and* that it is right to react. Since the beliefs are false (because externals are neither good nor bad), the wise person is free from passion (*apatheia*), though not from feeling altogether. See [The Passions](../learn-stoicism/07-the-passions.md).

**The dichotomy of control.** The most famous Stoic text opens with a distinction that has become a self-help staple:

> There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power. Within our power are opinion, aim, desire, aversion, and, in one word, whatever affairs are our own. Beyond our power are body, property, reputation, office, and, in one word, whatever are not properly our own affairs.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1 (tr. T. W. Higginson)

And a few paragraphs later:

> Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things. Thus death is nothing terrible, else it would have appeared so to Socrates. But the terror consists in our notion of death, that it is terrible.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5 (tr. T. W. Higginson)

Epictetus, a former slave who taught in exile under the emperor Domitian, is covered in [Epictetus](../learn-stoicism/10-epictetus.md).

**Stoics versus Epicureans.** The two schools agreed that happiness is freedom from disturbance, that it is within everyone's power, and that it requires correcting false beliefs. They disagreed about whether pleasure or virtue is the good, whether the gods care for us, and whether the universe is a providential whole or a chance collision of atoms. They also disagreed about politics. The Stoics urged the wise to take part in public life, while Epicurus advised "live unnoticed." Yet they were not always at war. Seneca, a Stoic, quotes Epicurus approvingly again and again, and in *Of a Happy Life* he defends him against his own followers:

> I myself believe, though my Stoic comrades would be unwilling to hear me say so, that the teaching of Epicurus was upright and holy, and even, if you examine it narrowly, stern: for this much talked of pleasure is reduced to a very narrow compass, and he bids pleasure submit to the same law which we bid virtue do—I mean, to obey nature.
>
> — Seneca, *De Vita Beata* (*Of a Happy Life*) XIII (tr. Aubrey Stewart)

## The Skeptics: peace through suspension

The third therapy is the strangest. Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE) traveled with Alexander's army to India, where, according to ancient reports, he met the "naked philosophers" and returned convinced that nothing can be known. Like Socrates he wrote nothing. Diogenes Laertius preserves the legends:

> For he used to say that nothing was honourable, or disgraceful, or just, or unjust. And on the same principle he asserted that there was no such thing as downright truth; but that men did everything in consequence of custom and law. For that nothing was any more this than that. And his life corresponded to his principles; for he never shunned anything, and never guarded against anything; encountering everything, even waggons for instance, and precipices, and dogs, and everything of that sort...
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* IX.61–62 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

His friends, the story adds, had to follow him around to keep him from being killed. (The same passage reports another source who says Pyrrho was perfectly sensible in practice.) The tradition he founded was revived around the first century BCE by Aenesidemus and is known to us chiefly through the *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* of Sextus Empiricus, a physician who wrote around 200 CE.

### Ataraxia by accident

Sextus defines skepticism as an *ability*: the ability to set appearances and judgments against one another so that, because the opposing arguments are equally strong, we suspend judgment (*epochē*), and then reach tranquility. Skepticism is not a doctrine that nothing can be known (that would be a dogma too) but a practice of continuing to inquire without committing.

Why would suspension bring peace? Sextus tells the story of how it happened to the first skeptics. They began, like everyone, by trying to find the truth in order to be at peace, and they failed:

> He met, however, with contradictions of equal weight, and, being unable to judge, he withheld his opinion; and while his judgment was in suspension [ataraxia] followed, as if by chance, in regard to matters of opinion. For he who is of the opinion that anything is either good or bad by nature is always troubled...
>
> — Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* I.26–27 (tr. Mary Mills Patrick)

The person who believes that wealth is *really* good is tormented when they lack it and anxious when they have it, fearing to lose it. The person who has no such belief is spared both torments. Sextus illustrates with a famous story:

> For that which is related of Apelles the painter happened to the Sceptic. It is said that as he was once painting a horse he wished to represent the foam of his mouth in the picture, but he could not succeed in doing so, and he gave it up and threw the sponge at the picture with which he had wiped the colors from the painting. As soon, however, as it touched the picture it produced a good copy of the foam.
>
> — Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* I.28 (tr. Mary Mills Patrick)

Peace came when the skeptic gave up trying to get it, "as the shadow follows a body."

### Skeptical ethics

This has direct implications for ethics. Book III of the *Outlines* collects the endless disagreements among philosophers and among peoples about what is good, just, and pious. Some of them were quoted in [Chapter 3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md). Sextus concludes not that nothing is good (that would be a dogma) but that we should suspend judgment about whether anything is good *by nature*. Notice how this differs from relativism. The relativist believes that moral truth is relative. The skeptic believes nothing about it either way, and finds peace in not believing.

### Can a skeptic live?

The obvious objection, pressed by the Stoics, is called the **apraxia** ("inaction") objection. Action seems to require belief. If I don't believe the cart is coming, why step out of its way? If I don't believe that food will nourish me, why eat? A consistent skeptic would starve or be run over, as Pyrrho nearly was.

Sextus' answer is that the skeptic lives by *appearances*, without committing to whether they are true:

> Therefore, as we cannot be entirely inactive as regards the observances of daily life, we live by giving heed to phenomena, and in an unprejudiced way. But this observance of what pertains to the daily life, appears to be of four different kinds. Sometimes it is directed by the guidance of nature, sometimes by the necessity of the feelings, sometimes by the tradition of laws and of customs, and sometimes by the teaching of the arts.
>
> — Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* I.23 (tr. Mary Mills Patrick)

So the skeptic eats when hungry, follows the customs of their city (including its religion), and practices their trade, all without claiming that any of it is *really* right. It seems to work for perception: you can step out of the way of an apparent cart without a theory of whether appearances reveal reality. It is more troubling for ethics. Follow the customs of your city, even when the city's customs include slavery or persecution? The skeptic seems to have no ground for resistance, and a tyrant who demands something monstrous can only be met with the tranquil observation that it appears to be the custom. Critics from antiquity to Hume have found this the weakest point of the Pyrrhonist ethic. Defenders reply that nothing stops the skeptic from finding cruelty *appearing* abhorrent and acting on that appearance, and that dogmatists have hardly had a better record of resisting tyranny. The [nihilism course](../learn-nihilism/02-ancient-roots.md) considers what the skeptics share with later doubts about value.

## Should ethics be therapy?

The three schools disagree about the cure but share a diagnosis: human misery comes from false belief and runaway desire, and philosophy can fix it. That raises some questions about the whole therapeutic project.

**Is tranquility the right goal?** All three schools aim at *ataraxia*, an untroubled mind. But is an untroubled mind always good? A person whose child is in danger *should* be troubled. A citizen who sees injustice and feels nothing may have achieved tranquility at the cost of something important. Nussbaum argues that the Hellenistic schools, especially the Stoics, buy invulnerability by withdrawing concern from exactly the things that make human life valuable: friends, children, political community. Epictetus is notorious on this point. He advises that when you embrace your child, you should remind yourself that you are embracing something mortal, so that its death will not disturb you (*Enchiridion* 3). To many readers this is not wisdom but a refusal to love. Aristotle, who held that the good life is vulnerable to fortune ([Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md)), would have agreed.

**Does therapy respect the patient?** Medical argument aims at health, not truth. The Epicurean student memorizes maxims, defers to the founder ("Do everything as if Epicurus were watching"), and avoids arguments that might unsettle them. That seems to sit uneasily with the ideal of free rational inquiry that Socrates embodied. Nussbaum finds the Stoics better here than the Epicureans, since the Stoics insisted that each student must reason their own way to the truth, and the Skeptics oddly best of all, since they are committed to continuing inquiry. But in each school there is a tension between the authority of the doctor and the autonomy of the patient.

**Do the cures work?** In one sense this is now an empirical question, and the answer is partly yes. Cognitive behavioral therapy, the most widely tested form of psychotherapy, was developed by Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, who both credited the Stoics, especially Epictetus' "men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things." Its core technique is identifying and disputing distorted beliefs, which is the Hellenistic method. Mindfulness practices echo the skeptic's suspension of judgment. Research on materialism and well-being supports Epicurus' diagnosis of empty desires. Whether the *theories* behind the therapies are true is a separate question. A therapy can work even if its metaphysics is wrong.

**Is morality only for the self?** All three therapies are aimed at the patient's own peace of mind. Where are other people? The Stoics have the strongest answer, since their oikeiōsis extends concern to all humanity and their ethics demands justice and public service. The Epicureans offer friendship and a social contract. The Skeptics offer custom. A modern reader may feel that something has gone missing, namely the idea that other people make claims on us whatever our own state of mind. That idea is central to the Kantian and utilitarian ethics of [Chapters 9](09-kant.md) and [10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md).

Michel Foucault, late in his life, found in the Hellenistic schools a model of ethics as "care of the self," the deliberate shaping of one's own life as a work of art, which he contrasted favorably with rule-based modern morality. Hadot, whose work Foucault drew on, protested that this missed the point. The ancient exercises aimed not at self-cultivation as an aesthetic project, but at transcending the self, at seeing oneself as a small part of nature or the cosmos. The disagreement is worth pondering. Is a good life one you design, or one in which you stop putting yourself at the center?

## Living it

- **Sort your desires.** List ten things you want. Mark each as natural and necessary, natural but unnecessary, or empty. Which ones are making you anxious? Epicurus predicts it will be the empty ones.
- **Practice the bread-and-water test.** Once a week, eat the simplest meal you can. Notice whether it is less pleasant than you expected when you are actually hungry. The point is not austerity but freedom from dependence.
- **Rehearse the symmetry.** When fear of death or of a deadline comes, recall the billions of years before you were born. Note whether it helps. If it does not, ask whether that is because the argument fails or because the fear was never about being dead.
- **Draw the circle.** Before the next decision that affects strangers, imagine the Stoic circles and draw one of them a little nearer.
- **Suspend one judgment.** Pick a contested question on which you hold a strong view you have never examined. For a week, say "it appears to me that…" instead of "it is…" and notice whether you feel calmer, more anxious, or neither.

## Summary

- After Alexander, the **Hellenistic schools** treated philosophy as **medicine for the soul**: misery comes from false belief and unruly desire, and argument is the cure. Hadot calls this philosophy as a way of life. Nussbaum analyzes the "medical" model of argument.
- The **Cynics** (Diogenes of Sinope) lived against convention and called themselves citizens of the world.
- **Epicurus** was a hedonist, but held that the highest pleasure is **katastematic**: freedom from bodily pain (*aponia*) and mental disturbance (*ataraxia*). Pleasure has a ceiling, so happiness is easy to reach.
- Desires are **natural and necessary**, **natural but unnecessary**, or **empty**. Eliminate the empty ones. Virtue is the means to pleasure, justice is a contract, and friendship is the greatest good wisdom provides.
- **Death is nothing to us**, because what is not experienced cannot be bad. Lucretius adds the **symmetry argument**. Nagel's **deprivation account** replies that death is bad because it deprives us of goods. Williams argues that immortality would also be bad.
- The **Stoics** (in brief) held that virtue is the only good, that oikeiōsis extends concern to humanity, that passions are false judgments, and that we should attend to what is in our control. See the [Stoicism course](../learn-stoicism/README.md).
- The **Skeptics** (Pyrrho, Sextus) seek ataraxia by **suspending judgment**, and it follows "as if by chance." They live by appearances in four ways: nature, feelings, custom, and the arts. The **apraxia** objection and the problem of custom remain.
- The therapeutic model raises questions about whether tranquility is the right goal, whether therapy respects the patient's reason, and whether it leaves room for others' claims. Modern **CBT** descends from it.

## Dialogues

### Dialogue 1 — Epicurus on the promotion

**Epicurus:** Come into the garden. There is bread, and some cheese today. You look like someone who has not slept.

**You:** …

*Your turn: tell Epicurus what is keeping you up at night, and why it matters so much to you.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I'm up for a promotion. If I get it, I'll make thirty percent more and have a corner office. If I don't, my rival gets it, and I'll have to watch her be my boss. I keep running scenarios at 3 a.m.

**Epicurus:** Let us sort your desires. You want enough money to eat, to have a roof, to see your friends?

**You:** I have that already. I'm not poor.

**Epicurus:** Then the desires that are natural and necessary are satisfied. The thirty percent more, what will you buy with it?

**You:** A nicer apartment. Better vacations. Honestly, I'm not sure. It's more about being the one who got it.

**Epicurus:** So the money is natural but not necessary, and the being-the-one is something else. The corner office, the title, the defeat of your rival: are these natural, like hunger, or do they come from opinion?

**You:** Opinion, I guess. Nobody is born wanting a corner office.

**Epicurus:** Then mark this. The desires that come from opinion have no limit. If you get the office, will you stop wanting?

**You:** I'd probably want the next promotion.

**Epicurus:** Like a jar with a hole in it. And meanwhile, what has the desire given you this week?

**You:** Insomnia. An ulcer, maybe.

**Epicurus:** So you are paying with your peace of mind, the greatest pleasure there is, to buy a pleasure that will vanish the day you get it. A poor trade. But I am not telling you to refuse the promotion. If it comes, enjoy the better vacations. Only do not let your happiness hang on it.

**You:** How do I stop it hanging on it? I can't just decide not to care.

**Epicurus:** Not in one night. But try this. Tonight, before you sleep, imagine that your rival got the job. Picture your life the day after. Do you still have bread? Friends? This garden?

**You:** Yes.

**Epicurus:** Then you have rehearsed the worst, and found that it leaves you everything that matters. Do this each night. In a month you may find you sleep. And if you get the promotion after all, you will enjoy it more, because you will not be afraid of losing it.

### Dialogue 2 — Sextus and the argument online

**Sextus:** You are typing very fast, and frowning. Is someone ill?

**You:** …

*Your turn: describe an argument you are having online about a moral or political question, and say how sure you are that you are right.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Someone in a comment thread is defending a policy I think is cruel. I've written four replies. I'm completely sure I'm right, and it's driving me crazy that he won't admit it.

**Sextus:** You are sure. And he?

**You:** He seems sure too. That's the problem.

**Sextus:** Interesting. Two people, each certain, each with arguments. Have you found an argument he cannot answer?

**You:** Not yet. He keeps coming back with statistics. I come back with other statistics.

**Sextus:** So the arguments appear, at the moment, to be of roughly equal weight, at least between you two. That is a very common situation. I have spent my life collecting them.

**You:** Are you saying I should just give up and agree with him?

**Sextus:** Not at all. That would be a dogma too, only his instead of yours. I am saying something stranger. Notice how you feel.

**You:** Angry. Tense. My jaw hurts.

**Sextus:** You are troubled because you believe that his view is *really* bad and yours *really* good, and that it matters terribly that he admit it. What if you held both views loosely, saying only "it appears to me that the policy is cruel," and kept inquiring?

**You:** But it *is* cruel. People will be hurt.

**Sextus:** Perhaps. It certainly appears so to you, and I do not tell you to stop acting on that appearance. Vote as it appears right to vote. Help those who appear to need help. The custom of your city, and your own feelings, will guide you. What I question is the fifth reply in the thread.

**You:** You think it won't change his mind.

**Sextus:** Has the fourth? Here is what happened to the painter Apelles. He kept trying to paint the foam on a horse's mouth and failing. At last, in frustration, he threw the sponge, and the foam appeared. Sometimes peace comes when you stop grasping for it.

**You:** I'm not sure I can live like that about things that really matter. Isn't that how people end up going along with injustice?

**Sextus:** That is the strongest objection to my way of life, and I will not pretend it is answered. But consider: did your four replies make anyone less cruel? Or only make you less calm? Close the window. Go do something that appears good. Then, if you like, come back and we will inquire together whether that appearance is true.

</details>
