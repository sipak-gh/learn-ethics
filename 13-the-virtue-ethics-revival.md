# Chapter 13 — The Virtue Ethics Revival

In 1956 Oxford University proposed to give an honorary degree to Harry S. Truman, the president who had ordered the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. A thirty-seven-year-old philosopher named Elizabeth Anscombe stood up in Congregation to oppose it. To choose to kill the innocent as a means to your ends, she said, is always murder. She lost the vote. Two years later she published "Modern Moral Philosophy," an essay arguing that the entire field had lost its way: the dominant theories (Kantian and utilitarian alike) were built on concepts that no longer made sense, and their authors could not see why a man who judicially executes an innocent to avert a disaster has done something unthinkable. Her proposed cure was to go back to Aristotle. This chapter tells the story of that return: Anscombe's diagnosis, Philippa Foot's naturalism, Alasdair MacIntyre's history of moral decline, Rosalind Hursthouse's systematic virtue ethics, the empirical challenge from social psychology, and the problem of moral luck that Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel posed in 1976. Previous: [Chapter 12 — Contractualism and Justice](12-contractualism-and-justice.md). Next: [Chapter 14 — Care and Feminist Ethics](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md).

## Anscombe's indictment

Elizabeth Anscombe (1919–2001) was a student and close friend of Wittgenstein, one of his literary executors and the translator of his *Philosophical Investigations*. She was a Catholic convert, smoked cigars, and wore trousers when women at Oxford did not; a much-repeated story has her, refused entry to a restaurant for wearing them, simply taking them off. Her book *Intention* (1957) founded the modern philosophy of action. "Modern Moral Philosophy" opens with three theses.

**First**, moral philosophy should be put on hold:

> it is not profitable for us at present to do moral philosophy; that should be laid aside at any rate until we have an adequate philosophy of psychology, in which we are conspicuously lacking.
>
> — G. E. M. Anscombe, "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958)

By "philosophy of psychology" she meant an account of action, intention, wanting, pleasure, and, ultimately, of what a virtue is and what human flourishing consists in. Without that, ethical theory floats free of the creatures it is supposed to guide.

**Second**, the concepts of moral obligation and moral duty, and the special "moral" sense of *ought*, should be jettisoned. They are survivals of a *law conception of ethics* that made sense within a religious framework (where there is a divine lawgiver), but which has been kept after the lawgiver was dropped. A law without a lawgiver is like the word "criminal" surviving in a society that had abolished criminal law and criminal courts: it retains its emotional force but has lost its sense. Kant's idea of legislating for oneself she called absurd: as absurd as calling each of a man's reflective decisions a vote, carried by an overwhelming majority of one to nothing. ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md) presents the Kantian reply.)

**Third**, the differences among English moral philosophers from Sidgwick on are of little importance. All of them hold that it could sometimes be right to do anything whatever, if the consequences of not doing it were bad enough. She coined the word **consequentialism** for this view ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)). A person who thought it an open question whether it might be right to procure the judicial execution of an innocent man, she said, shows a corrupt mind.

What should replace "morally ought"? Anscombe suggested that we already have the words. Instead of asking whether an act is "morally wrong," ask whether it is *unjust*, *untruthful*, *unchaste*, *cowardly*, *cruel*. These concepts have descriptive content that "wrong" lacks, and they belong to a vocabulary of virtues and vices. Philosophers now call them **thick ethical concepts**: they describe and evaluate at once. Bernard Williams later argued that a culture's thick concepts carry ethical knowledge that thin theories (with only "good," "right" and "ought") cannot capture.

## The Aristotelian resources

Anscombe pointed to Aristotle. Recall the core ideas from [Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md):

> Virtue then is "a state apt to exercise deliberate choice, being in the relative mean, determined by reason, and as the man of practical wisdom would determine."
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, II.6, 1106b36–1107a2 (tr. Chase)

Virtue is a *settled state* (*hexis*) of character, involving both feeling and choice. It is acquired by practice:

> men come to be builders, for instance, by building; harp-players, by playing on the harp: exactly so, by doing just actions we come to be just; by doing the actions of self-mastery we come to be perfected in self-mastery; and by doing brave actions brave.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, II.1, 1103a33–b2

And its point is practical: "we are enquiring not merely that we may know what virtue is but that we may become virtuous, else it would have been useless" (II.2). The standard of right action is the *phronimos*, the person of practical wisdom (*phronesis*), who perceives what the situation calls for. Happiness (*eudaimonia*) is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life, for "it is not one swallow or one fine day that makes a spring" (I.7).

Notice what is distinctive here compared with Kant and the utilitarians. The primary question is not "What should I do?" but "What kind of person should I be?" or, as Socrates put it, "How should one live?" Right action is defined by reference to character, not the other way round. And moral education is not learning a principle; it is training perception and emotion until the right response becomes second nature.

## Foot: goodness as natural

Philippa Foot (1920–2010) was Anscombe's friend and sparring partner at Oxford. (She was also the granddaughter of President Grover Cleveland, and a founder of Oxfam.) Her work spans fifty years and changed direction more than once.

**"Moral Beliefs" (1958)** attacked the dominant view of R. M. Hare and the emotivists, on which moral judgments express attitudes or commitments that can attach to anything ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)). Foot argued that the concepts of morality are tied to facts about human good and harm. Could someone sincerely call it "good" simply to clasp and unclasp your hands, with no story about why it mattered? Only by speaking a private language. Likewise "rude" applies only to behavior that causes offense by indicating lack of respect; you cannot use the word while refusing its conditions. Facts, she argued, can settle evaluations: the gap between "is" and "ought" is not the unbridgeable logical chasm that Hare supposed.

**"Morality as a System of Hypothetical Imperatives" (1972)** took a surprising turn. Foot argued that moral "oughts" are not categorical in Kant's sense: they give reasons only to people who care about the things morality protects. Like the rules of etiquette, they apply to everyone, but their force depends on our caring. She ended with an image of morally serious people not as conscripts in the army of duty but as volunteers, banded together to fight for justice and against inhumanity. ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md) discusses the challenge this poses to Kant.)

**Natural Goodness (2001)** reversed course again. Foot now argued that moral goodness is a species of the same *natural goodness* we attribute to plants and animals. Following Michael Thompson, she noted that biology uses "Aristotelian categoricals": "the wolf hunts in a pack," "the oak has deep roots." These are not statistical claims; they describe the life form. And they ground evaluation: a free-riding wolf who does not join in the hunt is *defective*, as is an oak with shallow roots. Humans, too, have a life form. We are rational, social, language-using animals who depend on one another and who make and keep promises. Peter Geach had put the thought memorably: humans need virtues as bees need stings. Justice, courage, temperance and practical wisdom are what humans need to live well as humans. Vice is a natural defect, like poor eyesight in an owl.

The objections are pointed. Is the human life form really fixed enough to ground ethics? Evolution produces aggression and tribalism as reliably as cooperation; how do we pick out the "proper" human life? And why should I, a rational agent, care about being a good specimen of my species? Foot's answer, that practical rationality itself is part of the human form, satisfies some readers and seems circular to others.

## MacIntyre: after virtue

Alasdair MacIntyre (1929–2025) was a Scottish philosopher who moved from Marxism to Thomist Catholicism and spent his later career in the United States. *After Virtue* (1981) is a work of historical philosophy with a polemical edge.

### The disquieting suggestion

The book opens with a parable. Imagine that a catastrophe destroys natural science: laboratories burned, scientists lynched, books destroyed. Later, people try to revive science from fragments: half-chapters, torn pages, instruments whose use is forgotten. They argue about relativity and phlogiston using words whose meaning has been lost. MacIntyre's "disquieting suggestion" is that the language of morality in our culture is in the same state. We use fragments of older traditions (Aristotelian virtues, biblical law, Enlightenment rights, utilitarian welfare) without the context that once gave them sense. That is why moral debates about abortion, war and justice are interminable: the rival premises are *incommensurable*, and there is no shared standard to settle between them.

The result, he argues, is a culture of **emotivism** in practice, whatever philosophers say in theory. Moral claims function as expressions of preference dressed up as objective statements. Its representative characters are the *manager*, who claims technical expertise in achieving given ends; the *therapist*, who treats ends as given and helps people adjust; and the *rich aesthete*, who uses others for his own satisfaction.

### The failure of the Enlightenment project

MacIntyre's history goes like this. Aristotelian ethics had a three-part structure: human nature *as it happens to be*, human nature *as it could be if it realized its end* (*telos*), and the ethical precepts that guide the transition. The Enlightenment rejected teleology, the idea that humans have an essential purpose. What remained were the precepts and untutored human nature, with nothing to connect them. Hume tried to ground morality in the passions, Kant in reason, Kierkegaard in choice, the utilitarians in happiness. Each, MacIntyre argues, failed, and Nietzsche saw the failure most clearly ([Chapter 15](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md)). The final choice, the title of chapter 9, is "Nietzsche or Aristotle?"

### Practices, narratives, traditions

MacIntyre's positive account of virtue has three stages.

1. **Practices.** A *practice* is a cooperative activity (chess, farming, architecture, medicine, scientific research, the raising of a family) with *internal goods*: goods that can only be achieved by participating in it and recognized only by those who do. He gives the example of a child bribed with candy to play chess. At first she has every reason to cheat, since only the candy matters. If she comes to love the game's own goods (analytical skill, strategic imagination, competitive intensity), cheating defeats her own purpose. *External goods* (money, fame, power) can be gained in many ways and are objects of competition. Virtues are the qualities that enable us to achieve the internal goods of practices. Institutions, which are necessary to sustain practices, are always tempted to corrupt them by prioritizing external goods: think of a hospital managed only by its budget.
2. **The narrative unity of a life.** Practices alone cannot settle conflicts among them, or tell us what the good life as a whole is. For that we need the idea of a life as a unified story:

> I can only answer the question "What am I to do?" if I can answer the prior question "Of what story or stories do I find myself a part?"
>
> — Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981), ch. 15

3. **Traditions.** The stories we inherit are embedded in traditions: socially embodied arguments about the good, extended over time. A living tradition is one in which that argument continues.

The book ends darkly. MacIntyre compares our age to the end of the Roman Empire, when people of good will stopped trying to prop up the imperium and built local communities in which morality and civility could survive the coming dark ages. We are waiting, he said, not for a Godot but for a new St. Benedict, the founder of Western monasticism. (Rod Dreher's *The Benedict Option*, 2017, took this literally.)

Critics ask whether MacIntyre's view is relativist (if virtues are defined by practices and traditions, can a tradition be wrong?) and whether its nostalgia hides the exclusions of the communities it admires. MacIntyre's later books, *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988) and *Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry* (1990), argue that traditions can be rationally compared: one tradition can show its superiority by solving problems that its rival cannot solve by its own standards.

## Hursthouse: virtue ethics as a normative theory

By the 1990s, critics said virtue ethics was a mood, not a theory: inspiring about character, useless about action. Rosalind Hursthouse (b. 1943), a New Zealander trained at Oxford under Foot, set out to show otherwise. *On Virtue Ethics* (1999) offers a criterion of right action designed to stand alongside those of Kant and Mill:

> An action is right iff it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically (i.e. acting in character) do in the circumstances.
>
> — Rosalind Hursthouse, *On Virtue Ethics* (1999), ch. 1

A virtuous agent, in turn, is one who has and exercises the virtues; a virtue is a character trait a human being needs to flourish, or live well.

The obvious objection is that this gives no **action guidance**: if you are not virtuous, how can you know what a virtuous agent would do? Hursthouse replies with three points:

- **V-rules.** Every virtue generates a prescription and every vice a prohibition: do what is honest, charitable, just; do not do what is dishonest, uncharitable, unjust, cruel. The vocabulary of virtues and vices supplies far more rules than Kant or Mill do, and they are the rules children are actually taught.
- **Ask someone.** If you are unsure, you can consult people you consider wiser. This is not a weakness; it is how practical knowledge works in other fields.
- **Codifiability is overrated.** No theory delivers mechanical answers in hard cases. Kantians and utilitarians also need judgment to apply their principles.

Hursthouse's discussion of **dilemmas** is perhaps her most original contribution. Some dilemmas are *resolvable*: there is a right answer, but acting on it leaves a remainder of regret or a duty to make amends. Some are *irresolvable*: two choices are equally defensible. And some are *tragic*: whatever the agent does, a virtuous person would not emerge from it unmarked. The virtuous agent in a tragic dilemma does not act *well* (the action is not one a virtuous agent would be glad to have done), though she may act rightly. Kantian and utilitarian theories, which tend to say there is always one right answer and no remainder, cannot capture this.

Her "Virtue Theory and Abortion" (1991) showed the approach at work: rather than asking about the fetus's moral status or women's rights, it asks what a virtuous person, who understands the goods of parenthood and of a human life, would do in a given situation, and whether a decision is callous, light-minded, self-centered or courageous ([Chapter 19](19-bioethics.md)).

### Other voices

The revival was never one school:

- **Iris Murdoch** (*The Sovereignty of Good*, 1970) argued that moral life happens largely in *attention*, not in choice. Her example: a mother-in-law, M, thinks her son's wife, D, is vulgar and juvenile. M behaves impeccably. Over time, by deliberate effort of just and loving attention, M comes to see D as refreshingly simple and spontaneous. Nothing in M's outward behavior has changed, but something moral has happened.
- **Julia Annas** (*Intelligent Virtue*, 2011) models virtue on practical skill: like a pianist, the virtuous person learns by imitation, then develops the ability to explain and improve.
- **Michael Slote** (*Morals from Motives*, 2001) developed an *agent-based* virtue ethics, grounded in empathy, drawing on Hume and care ethics ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)).
- **Linda Zagzebski** (*Exemplarist Moral Theory*, 2017) builds ethics from admiration for moral exemplars.
- **Confucian** virtue ethics, studied in [Chapter 7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md), has become a major comparative partner.

## The situationist challenge

Virtue ethics makes an empirical assumption: that people have robust, stable character traits that explain their behavior across situations. Social psychology seems to deny it. Gilbert Harman ("Moral Philosophy Meets Social Psychology," 1999) and John Doris (*Lack of Character*, 2002) cited a run of famous experiments: Stanley Milgram's obedience studies (1963), in which most ordinary participants went on giving what they believed were dangerous shocks when an experimenter told them to; John Darley and Daniel Batson's "Good Samaritan" study (1973), in which hurried seminarians walked past a man slumped in a doorway; and Alice Isen and Paula Levin's report (1972) that finding a dime in a phone booth made people more helpful. [Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md) looks at these studies, and at how well they have held up, in detail.

The *situationist* conclusion: behavior is driven by small features of situations far more than by character, so "global" traits like honesty or compassion are largely fictional. If so, virtue ethics rests on a false psychology, the very thing Anscombe warned against.

Replies:

1. **Virtue is rare.** Aristotle never said most people are virtuous. The experiments show that most people lack virtue, which virtue ethics already predicts.
2. **Traits are more complex.** Christian Miller (*Character and Moral Psychology*, 2014) argues that most people have "mixed traits": real, stable dispositions that are neither virtues nor vices.
3. **Traits are not behavioral regularities.** Rachana Kamtekar ("Situationism and Virtue Ethics on the Content of Our Character," 2004) and Julia Annas (*Intelligent Virtue*, 2011) argue that an Aristotelian virtue is not a disposition to emit the same behavior everywhere but an integration of perception, emotion and practical wisdom. The hurried seminarian may have *perceived* a duty to his waiting audience.
4. **Replication.** Some classic studies (the dime study in particular) have failed to replicate, and the "replication crisis" in social psychology has weakened the evidence.
5. **Use the findings.** If situations shape behavior, the virtuous person will choose situations wisely, much as the Stoics trained themselves against impressions. The data become part of practical wisdom.

## Moral luck

In 1976, at a joint session of British philosophical societies, Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel each presented a paper titled "Moral Luck." The phrase was meant to sound like a contradiction.

### The Kantian picture

The idea it contradicts is Kant's, from the opening of the *Groundwork* ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)): even if, through "special disfavour of fortune," a good will achieved nothing at all,

> then, like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself. Its usefulness or fruitlessness can neither add nor take away anything from this value.
>
> — Kant, *Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals*, Ak. 4:394 (tr. Abbott)

On this view, morality is the one domain immune to luck. You are responsible only for what is under your control, and what is under your control is your will.

### Smith's irregularity

Adam Smith had already noticed that we do not judge this way. Everyone agrees, he says, that if two people's intentions were equally good or bad, their merit or demerit is the same, however the results turned out. But:

> when we come to particular cases, the actual consequences which happen to proceed from any action, have a very great effect upon our sentiments concerning its merit or demerit, and almost always either enhance or diminish our sense of both.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, II.iii, introduction

He called this an "irregularity of sentiment, which every body feels, which scarce any body is sufficiently aware of, and which no body is willing to acknowledge." He also noticed the agent's own response. A rider who could not manage a frightened horse and trampled his neighbor's slave feels something he would not have felt if the horse had calmed down:

> The person himself, who by an accident even of this kind has involuntarily hurt another, seems to have some sense of his own ill desert, with regard to him. He naturally runs up to the sufferer to express his concern for what has happened, and to make every acknowledgment in his power.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, II.iii.2

### Williams: Gauguin and the lorry driver

Williams's paper uses two cases.

**The lorry driver.** A truck driver, through no fault of his own, runs over a child. He will feel something that a bystander, however sympathetic, will not: Williams calls it **agent-regret**, regret about one's own agency, even where it was not culpable. We would think worse of a driver who did not feel it, and we would try to talk him out of an excess of it. This is Smith's rider, two centuries later.

**Gauguin.** Williams imagines a painter (loosely based on Paul Gauguin) who abandons his family to pursue his art in Tahiti. Whether he was *justified*, Williams argues, depends on something he could not know when he chose: whether he would succeed as a painter. And it depends on a particular kind of luck, *intrinsic* to the project (whether he really had the talent), not merely extrinsic (whether he got sick on the voyage). If he fails, he has no justification. If he succeeds, he does, though not one that will satisfy his abandoned family. Justification, here, comes only retrospectively.

Williams's conclusion is radical: if rational justification of a life can depend on luck, then morality's claim to supremacy, and to immunity from luck, is undermined. Morality is one value among others, and not always the overriding one.

### Nagel's four kinds

Nagel's paper generalized the problem. He distinguished four kinds of luck that affect moral assessment:

1. **Resultant luck**: luck in how things turn out. Two equally careless drivers run a red light; a child steps into the road in front of only one. One is a killer; the other merely reckless.
2. **Circumstantial luck**: luck in the situations you face. Ordinary Germans in the 1930s faced a test that people in other countries were never set; many failed it, and are judged for failing. Someone who would have behaved just as badly, but was born in Argentina, escapes blame.
3. **Constitutive luck**: luck in who you are, your temperament, inclinations and capacities. Being naturally generous or naturally cold is not something you chose.
4. **Causal luck**: luck in how you are determined by antecedent circumstances, which is the problem of free will ([Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md)).

The **control principle** says we are morally assessable only for what is under our control. But if we apply it consistently, Nagel argues, the area of genuine agency shrinks to an extensionless point. Everything we do is shaped by what we did not control. Yet we cannot give up moral judgment. Nagel saw no solution: the problem arises from the clash between the internal view of ourselves as agents and the external view of ourselves as parts of the world.

### Responses

- **Deny resultant luck.** Many Kantians and some consequentialists say the two drivers are equally blameworthy; we just *know* more about one. The difference in our reactions is epistemic or legal, not moral.
- **Accept it.** Some philosophers accept that luck affects moral standing, and that this is just how morality is.
- **Embrace fragility.** Martha Nussbaum's *The Fragility of Goodness* (1986) argued that Greek tragedy and Aristotle saw what Kant missed: a good human life is vulnerable to fortune. Aristotle's example was Priam, the king of Troy who saw his sons killed and his city burned in old age: "the man who has experienced such fortune and died in wretchedness, no man calls happy" (I.9). The good person, Aristotle said, "bears all fortunes … becomingly," as "a good shoemaker makes the handsomest shoe he can out of the leather which has been given him," but even he will not be called blessed if he falls into fortunes like those of Priam (I.10).
- **Make virtue invulnerable.** The Stoics held that virtue alone is good and is entirely in our power, so the sage is happy even on the rack. Virtue ethics thus contains both the most luck-exposed and the most luck-proof positions in the history of ethics ([learn-stoicism, Chapter 6](../learn-stoicism/06-virtue-and-the-good.md)).

## Living it

**The thick-concept diary.** For one week, each evening, describe one thing you did without using the words "good," "bad," "right," "wrong" or "should." Use only thick concepts: generous, petty, tactful, cowardly, patient, careless, fair. Notice what the thick vocabulary lets you say that the thin one does not, and where it makes you uncomfortable.

**The practice audit.** Choose one practice you take part in (a sport, an instrument, a craft, your field of study). List its internal goods and its external goods. When did you last pursue the external goods in a way that damaged the internal ones (cramming for a grade instead of learning, playing for the win instead of the game)? What virtue would have prevented it?

**The luck inventory.** Think of one thing you are proud of morally and one thing you are ashamed of. For each, sort the contributing factors into Nagel's four categories. How much is left once you subtract luck? Does the exercise make you more forgiving of others, or less proud of yourself, or both?

## Summary

- Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958): moral philosophy needs a philosophy of psychology; the moral "ought" is a survival of a divine-law conception; all English moral philosophers since Sidgwick are "consequentialists" (her coinage). Thick concepts (unjust, cruel, cowardly) are the alternative.
- Aristotle's resources: virtue as a settled state of choice in a mean, acquired by practice, determined by practical wisdom; ethics aims to make us good.
- Foot: evaluative concepts have factual conditions of application ("Moral Beliefs"); moral oughts as hypothetical (1972); then *Natural Goodness* (2001): virtue as natural goodness for the human life form.
- MacIntyre: our moral language is in fragments; the Enlightenment project failed; virtues are defined through practices (internal goods), the narrative unity of a life, and traditions.
- Hursthouse: right action is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do; v-rules give guidance; resolvable, irresolvable and tragic dilemmas.
- Murdoch's attention, Annas's skill model, Slote's agent-based ethics, Zagzebski's exemplarism.
- Situationism (Milgram, Good Samaritan, dime studies) challenges robust traits; replies stress rarity of virtue, mixed traits, replication failures and situational wisdom.
- Moral luck: Kant's jewel versus Smith's "irregularity of sentiment"; Williams's lorry driver (agent-regret) and Gauguin (retrospective justification); Nagel's resultant, circumstantial, constitutive and causal luck; responses from denial to Nussbaum's fragility and Stoic invulnerability.

### Dialogue 2 — On the two drivers

**Nagel:** Two drivers, equally tired, equally careless, run the same red light on the same evening in different towns. A child steps out in front of one of them and is killed. Are they equally to blame?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Nagel in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** In principle, yes. They did the same thing. The only difference is luck.

**Nagel:** Then should they receive the same punishment?

**You:** The law punishes the one who killed more. But maybe it shouldn't.

**Nagel:** Should the one who killed feel the same as the other one?

**You:** No. He'll feel terrible, and he should. If he felt the same as the other driver, there'd be something wrong with him.

**Kant (stepping in):** And it is a confusion. The good or bad will shines, or fails to shine, by its own light. The child's step is no part of the driver's will.

**You:** But Smith is right that we all feel it. Are we all just confused?

**Williams (stepping in):** The feeling is not confusion. I call it agent-regret. It is how a person relates to his own agency, which includes what he actually did in the world, not only what he willed.

**Nagel:** But then you must say what blame tracks. If it tracks outcomes, then most of what we are blamed for is luck. If it tracks only the will, then what about luck in temperament and circumstance? The inner will shrinks to nothing.

**You:** Maybe blame and regret come apart. Blame should track what the drivers controlled, which was the same. Regret can track what happened, which wasn't.

**Nagel:** That is a reasonable division. But do you believe it when you imagine meeting the child's mother?

**You:** No. I'd feel that the driver who killed her son owes her something the other driver doesn't.

**Nagel:** Then you share my problem. I do not think it has a solution. I think it shows something about our position: we are agents who see ourselves from inside, and objects in the world seen from outside.

**Williams:** And moral philosophy should stop pretending that the second view can be wished away.

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On the seminarian who was late

**Doris:** You admire your friend Sam. You call him compassionate. Suppose Sam were one of the seminary students in the Good Samaritan study, told he was late for his talk. Would he stop for the man in the doorway?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Doris in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I'd like to think so. Sam is the kind of person who notices people.

**Doris:** So were many of the seminarians, by their own account and their teachers'. Most of the hurried ones walked past. Some stepped over the man. What makes you confident Sam is different?

**You:** I've seen him help people many times.

**Doris:** In what situations? When he had time? When others were watching? When he had just had good news? The research suggests that what you have seen is compassion-when-unhurried, or helpfulness-after-a-good-day: narrow dispositions, not a global virtue.

**You:** Then is calling him compassionate just a mistake?

**Aristotle (stepping in):** Perhaps calling him *virtuous* is premature. I never said virtue was common. Most people are on the way, not at the end.

**Doris:** Then your theory has little to say about the people who actually exist.

**Hursthouse (stepping in):** It says a great deal to them. It tells them what to aim at, and the v-rules tell them what to do meanwhile: be kind, do not be callous. Your experiments show how far most of us fall short. That is not news to a virtue ethicist.

**You:** But it is useful. If being hurried makes me blind, then part of practical wisdom is not over-scheduling myself, or at least noticing when I am rushing.

**Doris:** I would accept that. I have argued that the lesson is to attend to situations, not to trust character.

**Aristotle:** And I would say that knowing which situations corrupt you, and avoiding them, is exactly what a person of practical wisdom does. We may be closer than you think.

**You:** Then the disagreement is about how much of the work character can do on its own.

**Doris:** Less than you hope.

**Hursthouse:** More than he fears.
