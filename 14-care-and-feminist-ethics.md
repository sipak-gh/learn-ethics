# Chapter 14 — Care and Feminist Ethics

Heinz's wife is dying of cancer. A druggist in town has discovered a drug that might save her, but he charges ten times what it costs him to make, and Heinz can raise only half the price. The druggist refuses to sell it cheaper or let Heinz pay later. Should Heinz steal the drug? Lawrence Kohlberg put this dilemma to boys and young men for decades and scored their answers on a six-stage scale of moral development. In the late 1970s his colleague Carol Gilligan put it to two eleven-year-olds, Jake and Amy. Jake said Heinz should steal it, because a life is worth more than money; he called the problem "sort of like a math problem with humans." Amy hesitated. Couldn't Heinz talk to the druggist, borrow the money, find another way? If he stole the drug and went to jail, who would look after his wife? On Kohlberg's scale, Jake scored a full stage higher. Gilligan thought the scale, not Amy, was what had gone wrong. This chapter follows two strands of feminist ethics. The older one, from Mary Wollstonecraft and John Stuart Mill, demands that women be held to the *same* moral standards and granted the same rights as men. The newer one, the ethics of care, argues that moral theory itself has ignored the moral knowledge found in relationships of dependence, and that what has been dismissed as "feminine" is part of what ethics is about. Previous: [Chapter 13 — The Virtue Ethics Revival](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md). Next: [Chapter 15 — Nietzsche and the Critique of Morality](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md).

## What makes an ethics feminist?

Feminist ethics is not one theory. Alison Jaggar's useful definition is functional: a feminist ethics is any approach that aims to understand, criticize and correct the ways gender has distorted moral thought and practice. That definition admits Kantian feminists, utilitarian feminists, virtue-ethical feminists and care ethicists. What they share is a set of suspicions:

- that the canonical theories were written by men who generalized from the lives of propertied men, men whose dependence on others (wives, servants, slaves) was invisible to them because someone else did the work;
- that the line drawn between the public sphere (politics, contracts, justice) and the private sphere (home, family, love) placed women's lives outside the reach of justice and their moral experience outside the reach of theory;
- that traits culturally coded as feminine (emotion, attention to particulars, responsiveness to others) were downgraded in ethics as they were in everything else.

Two programs follow, pulling in different directions. The first says: women are rational moral agents exactly as men are; apply the existing principles consistently and they will condemn women's subordination. The second says: the principles themselves are partial, and ethics must be rebuilt from the experiences of care that the tradition ignored. Call them the **equality** program and the **care** program. Wollstonecraft and Mill are the classics of the first.

## Wollstonecraft: virtue has no sex

Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797) was a self-educated governess, schoolteacher, translator and reviewer. In 1790 she wrote one of the first replies to Edmund Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France*, *A Vindication of the Rights of Men*. Two years later, in six weeks, she wrote *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, dedicated to Talleyrand, who had proposed to the French National Assembly a plan of public education that would keep girls at home. She died at thirty-eight, of an infection contracted in giving birth to her second daughter, the future Mary Shelley.

### The argument from reason

Wollstonecraft's argument is a direct application of Enlightenment moral philosophy. Virtue is the perfection of reason. Women are rational creatures. Therefore women's virtue must be the same in kind as men's:

> If women are by nature inferior to men, their virtues must be the same in quality, if not in degree, or virtue is a relative idea; consequently, their conduct should be founded on the same principles, and have the same aim.
>
> — Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, ch. 2

Notice the dilemma's structure. Wollstonecraft grants, for the sake of argument, that women might be weaker. It does not matter. Either virtue is one thing for all rational beings, or it is relative to sex, in which case it is not really virtue but conformity to someone else's preferences. A "female virtue" of obedience is no more a virtue than a slave's docility.

Her target was the moral literature of her day, which recommended to women a separate set of "virtues":

> Gentleness, docility, and a spaniel-like affection are, on this ground, consistently recommended as the cardinal virtues of the sex
>
> — Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, ch. 2

### Rousseau's Sophie

Her principal opponent was Rousseau, whom she admired. In *Emile* (1762), his treatise on education, Rousseau raises his imaginary pupil Emile to be independent, self-reliant and rational. Then, in Book V, he describes the education of Emile's future wife, Sophie, and it is a different education altogether:

> A woman's education must therefore be planned in relation to man. To be pleasing in his sight, to win his respect and love, to train him in childhood, to tend him in manhood, to counsel and console, to make his life pleasant and happy, these are the duties of woman for all time, and this is what she should be taught while she is young.
>
> — Rousseau, *Emile*, Book V (tr. Foxley)

Wollstonecraft found the portrait of Sophie "captivating" but "grossly unnatural," and attacked "the foundation of her character, the principles on which her education was built." Rousseau had argued that if women were educated like men, the more they resembled men, the less power they would have over them. Wollstonecraft quotes him and replies:

> This is the very point I aim at. I do not wish them to have power over men; but over themselves.
>
> — Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, ch. 4

The two kinds of power differ in kind. Power *over men*, through charm and cunning, is the power of a dependent; it corrupts both parties, as courtiers corrupt kings. Power *over oneself* is autonomy: the government of one's conduct by one's own reason, which is what virtue is. Wollstonecraft is arguing, in English and from her own premises, for something close to what Kant, a few years earlier in German, had called autonomy ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)).

### The gilt cage and the argument from consequences

Her second line of argument looks at what the existing education produces. Denied serious knowledge in the name of innocence, girls learn that their worth lies in their looks:

> Taught from their infancy, that beauty is woman's sceptre, the mind shapes itself to the body, and, roaming round its gilt cage, only seeks to adorn its prison.
>
> — Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, ch. 3

The result, she argues, is bad for everyone. Women kept ignorant become vain, cunning and frivolous, and then are condemned for being so. And they make poor mothers, which is the role their critics claim to value most. "To be a good mother," she writes, "a woman must have sense, and that independence of mind which few women possess who are taught to depend entirely on their husbands" (ch. 10). Her conclusion is pointed:

> make women rational creatures and free citizens, and they will quickly become good wives, and mothers; that is—if men do not neglect the duties of husbands and fathers.
>
> — Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, ch. 12

### Wollstonecraft against sensibility

One feature of the *Vindication* matters for what follows. Wollstonecraft is deeply suspicious of feeling. The culture of her time praised women's "sensibility": delicate, refined emotional responsiveness. She calls it a trap. Women are praised for "exquisite sensibility, and sweet docility of manners" in place of understanding (Introduction), but "sensibility is not reason" (ch. 4). She wants women to be governed by principle, not by the feelings men find attractive. This sets her against much of the later ethics of care, which praises precisely the emotional responsiveness she distrusted. Whether the two can be reconciled is the main question of this chapter.

## Mill: the subjection of women

John Stuart Mill wrote *The Subjection of Women* in 1861 and published it in 1869. He credited much of its thought to Harriet Taylor Mill, his close friend for two decades and then his wife for seven years, who had died in 1858 and whose essay "The Enfranchisement of Women" (1851) argued for full equality. In 1867, as a Member of Parliament, Mill moved an amendment to the Reform Bill to replace the word "man" with "person," which would have given women the vote. It was defeated, 196 to 73. The book opens with its thesis:

> That the principle which regulates the existing social relations between the two sexes—the legal subordination of one sex to the other—is wrong in itself, and now one of the chief hindrances to human improvement; and that it ought to be replaced by a principle of perfect equality, admitting no power or privilege on the one side, nor disability on the other.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *The Subjection of Women*, ch. 1

Note "wrong in itself." Mill is a utilitarian ([Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)), and much of the book argues from consequences: the waste of half of humanity's talent, the corruption of men by unearned power, the loss to marriage of real companionship. But the language of rights and justice is never far away.

### The willing slave

Mill's most original argument concerns why the subordination of women had lasted so long, longer than any other form of domination. Every other subject class was held down by force and fear. Women were held down by their own sentiments:

> Men do not want solely the obedience of women, they want their sentiments. All men, except the most brutish, desire to have, in the woman most nearly connected with them, not a forced slave but a willing one, not a slave merely, but a favourite.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *The Subjection of Women*, ch. 1

To achieve this, men "turned the whole force of education to effect their purpose." Women are taught that their ideal of character is the opposite of men's, "not self-will, and government by self-control, but submission, and yielding to the control of others":

> All the moralities tell them that it is the duty of women, and all the current sentimentalities that it is their nature, to live for others; to make complete abnegation of themselves, and to have no life but in their affections.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *The Subjection of Women*, ch. 1

This is an early theory of what feminists would later call **adaptive preferences** and internalized oppression. If women say they are content, that does not settle anything, because the contentment was manufactured. Hold this passage in mind: it will return when care ethicists praise living for others.

### The nature of women

The standard defense of subordination was that it followed women's nature. Mill's reply is epistemological. No one knows what women's nature is, because no one has ever observed women outside a system designed to shape them:

> What is now called the nature of women is an eminently artificial thing—the result of forced repression in some directions, unnatural stimulation in others.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *The Subjection of Women*, ch. 1

Other subjugated groups had at least been let alone in whatever was not crushed; but with women, he says, "a hot-house and stove cultivation has always been carried on of some of the capabilities of their nature, for the benefit and pleasure of their masters." Then the results of the hothouse are cited as nature. The only way to find out what women can do is to remove the restrictions and see.

### The family as a school

Mill's most far-reaching claim is that the family is where moral character is formed, and that the patriarchal family forms it badly:

> The family is a school of despotism, in which the virtues of despotism, but also its vices, are largely nourished.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *The Subjection of Women*, ch. 2

What it should be instead is "a school of sympathy in equality, of living together in love, without power on one side or obedience on the other." A boy who grows up watching his father rule his mother learns that half of humanity is beneath him, before he has done anything to earn it. Susan Moller Okin's *Justice, Gender, and the Family* (1989) revived exactly this argument against Rawls ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)): Rawls assumed that the parties in the original position were "heads of households" and that families were just, without asking whether they were. If the family is the first school of justice, Okin argued, an unjust family undermines the whole structure.

### Limits

Mill's feminism had limits that later feminists noted. He assumed that most married women, given a free choice, would choose to manage a household and raise children, and he did not ask men to share that work. Equality before the law left the division of labor at home untouched. That gap, between formal equality and the actual distribution of care work, is where the second program begins.

## Interlude: Addams and the charity visitor

Before the ethics of care had a name, Jane Addams (1860–1935) was practicing it. In 1889 she and Ellen Gates Starr founded Hull House, a settlement house in an immigrant neighborhood of Chicago; in 1931 she became the first American woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize. *Democracy and Social Ethics* (1902) is a work of moral philosophy written from that neighborhood.

Its second chapter studies the "charity visitor," a middle-class woman who visits poor families on behalf of a charitable society, investigates their worthiness, and lectures them on thrift and temperance. Addams describes how the neighborhood sees her. If a poor woman's neighbor has no shoes, she lends her own:

> The charity visitor has broken through the natural rule of giving, which, in a primitive society, is bounded only by the need of the recipient and the resources of the giver; and she gets herself into untold trouble when she is judged by the ethics of that primitive society.
>
> — Jane Addams, *Democracy and Social Ethics*, ch. 2

The visitor's ethic of thrift, Addams observes, "seems quite commendable in a comfortable part of town" but "appears almost criminal in a poorer quarter where the next-door neighbor needs food." Two moral codes meet, and the one with institutional power is not obviously the better one. The cure Addams proposes is democratic and relational: knowledge of others' lives that comes only from living among them.

> We are learning that a standard of social ethics is not attained by travelling a sequestered byway, but by mixing on the thronged and common road where all must turn out for one another, and at least see the size of one another's burdens.
>
> — Jane Addams, *Democracy and Social Ethics*, ch. 1

Maurice Hamington and others read Addams as a precursor of care ethics: attention to particular needs, distrust of rules applied from a distance, ethics learned in relationships.

## Beauvoir: the Other

Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) gave feminism its philosophical framework for the second half of the twentieth century. Its central claim is that woman has been constituted as the **Other**: man is defined as the subject, the absolute, the human as such; woman is defined relative to him, as what he is not. The concept comes from Hegel's master-slave dialectic, but Beauvoir notes an asymmetry: unlike slaves or the proletariat, women are not a minority with a separate history, and they live dispersed among men, attached by family and class more firmly than to other women. Volume II opens with the book's most famous line:

> One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman.
>
> — Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex* (1949), tr. H. M. Parshley (1953)

(The 2009 translation by Constance Borde and Sheila Malovany-Chevallier renders it "One is not born, but rather becomes, woman.") The claim is not that biology is irrelevant but that "woman," as a social meaning, is made: by education, by expectations, by the myths men tell about women and the myths women are taught to tell about themselves. This is Mill's "eminently artificial thing" given an existentialist foundation.

The ethical point is Beauvoir's concept of **complicity**. Women are oppressed, but they are also free, and some accept the role of Other because it offers comfort: protection, an escape from the anguish of freedom, a share in men's privileges. This is *bad faith*, a flight from freedom, and Beauvoir does not excuse it, though she places most of the blame on the situation that makes it tempting. [Chapter 16](16-existentialist-ethics.md) develops her *Ethics of Ambiguity* and the existentialist ethics of freedom. Beauvoir belongs firmly to the equality program: she wanted women to become free subjects, not to revalue the Other's role.

## Gilligan: a different voice

### Kohlberg's ladder

Lawrence Kohlberg (1927–1987), building on Piaget, held that moral reasoning develops through six stages in three levels:

| Level | Stage | Basis of judgment |
|---|---|---|
| Preconventional | 1. Obedience and punishment | avoid punishment |
| | 2. Instrumental exchange | serve one's own interests; fair deals |
| Conventional | 3. Interpersonal accord | be a good person in others' eyes; maintain relationships |
| | 4. Law and order | maintain the social system |
| Postconventional | 5. Social contract | rights and agreements that rational people would accept |
| | 6. Universal principles | self-chosen principles of justice and respect for persons |

Stage 6 is recognizably Kant and Rawls. Kohlberg's original longitudinal study followed 84 boys. When the scheme was applied to women, they tended to score at stage 3, where morality means helping and pleasing others. Kohlberg suggested that women would advance if their lives took them beyond the domestic sphere.

### Amy and Jake

Gilligan (b. 1936) argued that the scheme was biased. Return to Jake and Amy. Jake treats the dilemma as a conflict of rights (property against life) that can be resolved by ranking them; the right answer can be deduced by anyone. Amy sees a different problem: a breakdown in relationships. The druggist is failing to respond to need; Heinz should keep talking with him until he understands. She is not confused about the value of life. She is asking a different question: not "what are the rights at stake?" but "how can everyone's needs be met without anyone being abandoned?" Kohlberg's scoring could hear only the first question.

Gilligan supported this with a study of twenty-nine women deciding whether to have an abortion. She found a developmental sequence different from Kohlberg's: from concern with survival, to a conventional goodness defined as self-sacrifice, to a mature ethic in which care extends to oneself as well as others and in which the injunction is not to hurt anyone, including oneself.

She called the two orientations the **ethic of justice** and the **ethic of care**:

| | Justice | Care |
|---|---|---|
| Moral problem | conflicting rights | conflicting responsibilities |
| Self | separate, autonomous | connected, relational |
| Method | formal, abstract reasoning | contextual, narrative reasoning |
| Ideal | fairness, equal respect | no one left alone or hurt |
| Danger | indifference | self-abandonment |

Was this a claim about women? Gilligan denied it:

> The different voice I describe is characterized not by gender but theme.
>
> — Carol Gilligan, *In a Different Voice* (1982), Introduction

The association with women, she said, was empirical, not absolute, and she traced it to how girls and boys are raised, not to biology.

### The empirical critique

Lawrence Walker's review of the research (1984) found little evidence that women score lower than men on Kohlberg's scale once education and occupation are controlled for. A later meta-analysis (Jaffee and Hyde, 2000) found only small sex differences in care and justice orientations; the kind of dilemma people faced mattered more than their sex. On this evidence, the "different voice" is not a woman's voice but a human one that some theories had failed to hear. That reading strengthens rather than weakens the philosophical claim: if care reasoning is everyone's, a theory that ignores it is incomplete for everyone.

## Noddings: caring as the foundation

Nel Noddings (1929–2022), a philosopher of education, gave care ethics its first systematic statement in *Caring* (1984; the 2013 edition changed the subtitle from "A Feminine Approach" to "A Relational Approach").

Her starting point is the caring relation between a **one-caring** (later "carer") and a **cared-for**. Caring involves:

- **engrossment**: receptive attention to the other, taking in their reality as they experience it, not as the carer projects it;
- **motivational displacement**: the carer's energy flows toward the other's projects and needs;
- **completion** by the cared-for, whose response (recognition, growth, even simply flourishing) completes the relation. Caring that is not received is not fully caring.

Noddings distinguishes **natural caring** (a mother's care for her infant, which needs no ethical effort) from **ethical caring**, which draws on the memory of being cared for, and of caring, when natural care fails. The "ethical ideal" is one's best picture of oneself as one-caring; ethical caring is acting to preserve it. Natural caring is prior: ethics exists to restore the caring relation when it breaks down.

Two features are radical. First, Noddings rejects **universalizability** ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)): what one owes depends on who is actually present, and no principle can be extracted from one relation and applied to another situation that is "the same" in the abstract. Second, she held that obligation is limited by the possibility of completion. We are obliged to be prepared to care for anyone we encounter, but where no relation can be established or completed, as with distant strangers, there is no obligation to care in her full sense. She applied this to starving people far away and, more controversially, to animals.

Critics pounced: Peter Singer's drowning-child argument ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)) holds that distance is morally irrelevant, and Noddings seemed to make it decisive. Later care ethicists (Held, Tronto, Fiona Robinson, Daniel Engster) extend care to distant others through institutions: we care *about* them by supporting systems that care *for* them.

## Held, Kittay and Tronto: care beyond the home

### Held: care as practice and value

Virginia Held (b. 1929) defends care ethics as a full moral theory, distinct from Kantianism, utilitarianism and virtue ethics. In *The Ethics of Care* (2006), she identifies five features:

1. Its central focus is the compelling moral significance of attending to and meeting the needs of the particular others for whom we take responsibility.
2. It values emotions such as sympathy, empathy and responsiveness, not as feelings to be overridden by reason but as sources of moral understanding (though they need to be educated and checked).
3. It rejects the assumption that the more abstract a judgment, the better; it attends to particular situations and relationships.
4. It reconceptualizes the line between public and private: the family is not outside morality's reach.
5. It sees persons as **relational** and interdependent, not as the self-sufficient individuals of liberal theory.

Held treats care as both a **practice** (the labor of meeting needs) and a **value** (the relations of trust and mutual concern that such labor builds). Care, she argues, is more fundamental than justice: there can be care without justice (a loving but unequal family), but no justice without care, since without care no children would survive to become the rational agents that justice addresses. Justice should therefore operate within a framework of care, not the reverse.

Is care ethics just virtue ethics? Michael Slote (*The Ethics of Care and Empathy*, 2007) treats empathic caring as the master virtue ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)). Held replies that virtue ethics focuses on individuals' dispositions, care ethics on relations and the labor that sustains them.

### Kittay: some mother's child

Eva Feder Kittay (b. 1946), whose daughter Sesha has severe cognitive disabilities, argues in *Love's Labor* (1999) that liberal theories of justice are built on a fiction of equality among independent agents. Rawls models citizens as fully cooperating members of society throughout their lives. But every human being is helplessly dependent in infancy, many are in illness and old age, and some throughout life. Someone must care for them. Kittay calls this **dependency work**, and those who do it, often unpaid women, become dependent themselves: on whoever supports them while they care. Justice that ignores this "nested dependency" is unjust to both.

Her proposed principle, **doulia** (from *doula*, a woman who cares for a new mother), says that as we have been cared for, so we should ensure that those who care are themselves cared for. Its slogan is that everyone is "some mother's child": every person was cared for by someone, and each person's claim to care rests on that connection, not on rational capacity. This puts pressure on Kantian theories that ground moral status in rationality and on contract theories that count only those who can contribute ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)).

### Tronto: the politics of care

Joan Tronto's *Moral Boundaries* (1993) makes care a political concept. With Berenice Fisher, she defined caring very broadly, as everything we do to maintain, continue and repair our world so that we can live in it as well as possible. She distinguishes four phases, each with a matching moral quality:

| Phase | Moral quality |
|---|---|
| Caring about (noticing a need) | attentiveness |
| Taking care of (assuming responsibility) | responsibility |
| Care-giving (doing the work) | competence |
| Care-receiving (the response) | responsiveness |

Tronto's political point is that care work is distributed by power. The privileged enjoy what she calls "privileged irresponsibility": they receive care while assigning the work to others (women, servants, immigrants, low-wage workers) and need not notice that they depend on it. The sociologist Arlie Hochschild described **global care chains**, in which a woman from a poor country leaves her own children with relatives to care for the children of a richer family abroad. Care ethics here becomes a theory of justice in the distribution of care, close to what Mill was gesturing at, but now aimed at the work Mill left untouched.

## The hardest objection: is care a slave morality?

Return to Mill's willing slave and Wollstonecraft's spaniel. Both argued that the "feminine virtues" of self-abnegation, gentleness and living for others were products of subordination: hothouse growths, cultivated for the benefit of masters. Now care ethics praises attentiveness to others' needs, motivational displacement, and a self defined by its relationships. Has it taken the traits that oppression produced and renamed them virtues?

The worry has been pressed hard:

- **Claudia Card** ("Gender and Moral Luck," 1990) argued that the care perspective may reflect the moral luck of subordination ([Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)). People who must survive by pleasing others develop skills of attentiveness, but also damage: difficulty in saying no, in being angry, in recognizing one's own claims.
- **Sarah Lucia Hoagland** ("Some Thoughts about 'Caring'," 1991) argued that Noddings's model has no resources to tell a carer to leave an abusive relationship. If ethical caring means preserving the caring relation, it may counsel the battered wife to stay.

Nietzsche made a structurally similar charge against Christian morality as a whole: that it is a "slave morality" that turns weakness into virtue and the inability to retaliate into goodness ([Chapter 15](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md)). Feminist critics were not following Nietzsche, but they were asking his question: whose interests does this morality serve?

Care ethicists have several replies:

1. **Care includes self-care.** Gilligan's mature stage, and later theorists, insist that the self is one of the persons to whom care is owed. Care that destroys the carer is bad care.
2. **Justice is still needed.** Held and Kittay agree that care relations can be exploitative and that justice, rights and fair distribution are needed to correct them. The claim is that justice is not the whole of ethics, not that it is dispensable.
3. **Care is not gendered.** The values are human; the point is to distribute care work fairly, not to assign it to women. Mill and Wollstonecraft wanted women released from care work as a duty imposed by sex; care ethicists want it valued and shared.
4. **The critique cuts both ways.** If the "feminine" virtues were distorted by subordination, the "masculine" ideal of the independent, self-sufficient individual was distorted by privilege: it is the self-image of someone whose dependence is hidden.

The deepest reconciliation is perhaps that Wollstonecraft's target was not care but *ignorant* care, care without reason and without the power to refuse. Her good mother is one who has "sense, and that independence of mind." A care ethic grounded in free and reflective agency would satisfy her. A care ethic of uncomplaining self-sacrifice would not.

## Beyond care

Feminist ethics since the 1980s has moved in further directions. **Intersectionality** (Kimberlé Crenshaw, 1989) showed that categories of sex discrimination and race discrimination could both miss the situation of Black women; Patricia Hill Collins (*Black Feminist Thought*, 1990) described an ethic of caring and personal accountability with historical sources different from Gilligan's subjects. **Relational autonomy** (Catriona Mackenzie, Natalie Stoljar) treats autonomy as a capacity developed in relationships, which oppressive relationships can undermine, an answer to Mill's willing slave that does not abandon care. And **feminist Kantians and liberals** (Onora O'Neill, Barbara Herman, Martha Nussbaum) argue that the equality program's tools, properly applied, can do most of the work. [Chapter 19](19-bioethics.md) and [Chapter 21](21-technology-and-ai-ethics.md) carry these themes into relational consent and care robots.

## Living it

**The invisible-care audit.** For three days, list every act of care you receive: meals cooked, rooms cleaned, reminders given, emotional support, institutional services. For each, note who did it and whether they were paid. Then list the care you gave. What does the balance tell you about Tronto's "privileged irresponsibility"?

**Heinz, twice.** Write two responses to the Heinz dilemma: one in Jake's voice and one in Amy's. Then write a third that tries to combine them. Which was hardest to write? Which do you actually believe?

**The willing-slave test.** Think of one preference you hold about how you should behave because of your sex or gender (what you should look like, how you should express emotion, what work is yours). Apply Mill's test: if you had been raised without that expectation, would you have the preference? Can you tell?

## Summary

- Feminist ethics aims to correct gender distortions in moral thought and practice. It contains an **equality** program (same standards, same rights) and a **care** program (revalue relational, dependency-based moral experience).
- Wollstonecraft (1792): women's virtues must be the same as men's, "or virtue is a relative idea"; against Rousseau's Sophie, women need power "over themselves"; the "gilt cage" makes worse women and worse mothers; "sensibility is not reason."
- Mill (1869): subordination is "wrong in itself"; men want "a willing" slave and shape women's sentiments; women's known nature is "an eminently artificial thing"; the family should be "a school of sympathy in equality." Okin turned the argument on Rawls.
- Addams (1902): the charity visitor versus the neighborhood's "natural rule of giving."
- Beauvoir (1949): woman as the Other; one becomes a woman; complicity as bad faith.
- Gilligan (1982): Kohlberg's stages, Amy and Jake, the ethics of justice and of care; the different voice is defined by theme, not gender.
- Noddings (1984): engrossment, motivational displacement, completion; no universalizability; the problem of distant others.
- Held: care as practice and value, more fundamental than justice. Kittay: dependency work and doulia. Tronto: four phases of care and "privileged irresponsibility."
- The "slave morality" objection (Card, Hoagland) asks whether care ethics praises traits formed by subordination; replies stress self-care, the continuing need for justice, and ungendered care.

### Dialogue 1 — On Sophie's education

**Rousseau:** I have raised Emile to be free. Now he needs a companion. Should Sophie be educated exactly as he was, to be independent and to reason for herself?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Rousseau in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Yes. If independence and reason are good for Emile, they're good for her. They're both people.

**Rousseau:** They are both people, but they are not the same. Nature made them complementary. If you educate women like men, the more they resemble us, the less power they will have over us.

**Wollstonecraft (stepping in):** That is the very point I aim at. I do not wish them to have power over men, but over themselves.

**Rousseau:** Power over themselves? A woman's happiness depends on the man she lives with. Her education must be planned in relation to him.

**You:** But that's circular. Her happiness depends on him because you've designed her education that way. If she could support herself and think for herself, it wouldn't.

**Mill (stepping in):** Exactly. You say this is her nature, Jean-Jacques. How would you know? You have never seen a woman raised otherwise. What you call nature is a hot-house product.

**Rousseau:** Then tell me what she would become, if left to herself.

**Mill:** Nobody knows. That is my point. The only way to find out is to remove the restrictions and see.

**You:** And there's something else. Rousseau says Sophie should be pleasing, gentle, obliging. Those are only virtues if they're freely chosen. If she's trained into them because she has no other way to survive, they're just survival skills.

**Wollstonecraft:** Gentleness, docility and a spaniel-like affection: that is what they call the cardinal virtues of our sex. If virtue differs by sex, it is not virtue but a relative idea.

**Rousseau:** You will make women into men.

**Wollstonecraft:** I will make them into rational creatures. Make women rational creatures and free citizens, and they will quickly become good wives and mothers, if men do not neglect the duties of husbands and fathers.

**You:** That last clause is the one nobody quotes.

### Dialogue 2 — On Heinz and the druggist

**Kohlberg:** Heinz cannot pay. The druggist will not lower the price. Should Heinz steal the drug, and why?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Kohlberg in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Yes. The right to life outweighs the right to property. Any rational person would agree to that ranking if they didn't know whether they'd be Heinz's wife or the druggist.

**Kohlberg:** That is a stage 5 or 6 answer. You have appealed to universal principles and to impartial choice.

**Gilligan (stepping in):** And you have treated the people as positions in a calculation. Did you ask what will happen to Heinz's wife if he goes to jail?

**You:** That's a practical question. The moral question is whether stealing is permitted.

**Gilligan:** Why do you assume those are separate? Amy saw that the problem was that the druggist was failing to respond to a person in need. She wanted to repair that failure, not to rank rights.

**Kohlberg:** Her answer was confused. She did not see that the druggist had no right to let someone die for profit.

**Gilligan:** She saw it. She thought the druggist could be brought to see it, too, if Heinz explained the situation well enough. Is that confused, or is it a different picture of what people are: connected, so that a failure of response is the problem to solve?

**You:** But suppose the druggist won't listen. Amy's way runs out.

**Gilligan:** Then perhaps Heinz must steal. But notice what you have learned on the way: who depends on whom, what would be lost, whether there is any way to avoid harm. Your principle gave you the answer without any of that.

**Kohlberg:** Principles are meant to be impartial. The druggist and Heinz's wife count equally.

**You:** Maybe the two approaches answer different questions. Justice tells me what Heinz is permitted to do. Care tells me what he should notice and try first.

**Gilligan:** That is close to what I meant by two voices. The mature person can hear both.

**Kohlberg:** And who decides between them when they conflict?

**You:** I don't know yet. But I think a scoring system that heard only one of them was measuring the wrong thing.

### Dialogue 3 — On the stranger far away

**Singer:** You would wade into a pond to save a drowning child, even if it ruined your shoes. Children are dying of preventable disease in other countries, and a donation would save some of them. Is there a morally relevant difference?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Singer in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I can't see one. Distance doesn't make the child's life matter less.

**Noddings (stepping in):** It does not make it matter less. But caring is a relation. With the child in the pond, I can see her, respond to her, and she can respond to me. With a child I will never meet, there is no relation for my care to complete.

**Singer:** So she may die, because you cannot see her face?

**Noddings:** I did not say that. I said my obligation to care for her, in the full sense, depends on the possibility of a relation. I must be prepared to care for anyone I meet.

**You:** That sounds like it lets me off too easily. Most of the people I could help, I'll never meet.

**Held (stepping in):** Then care about them through the institutions that can care for them. Care ethics is not only about the people in your house. It asks who does the caring work in the world and whether the world is arranged so that needs are met.

**Singer:** I welcome the allies. But if you agree that the distant child's need generates an obligation, why not simply say so, impartially?

**Held:** Because impartiality is not how the obligation is felt or met. It is met by people, in relations, and by institutions built from those relations. A theory that starts from impartial calculation forgets the work.

**You:** So Singer tells me that I must help. Care ethics tells me how help actually happens, and what it costs the people who give it.

**Noddings:** And it reminds you that the child is not a unit of welfare but someone's child.

**Singer:** She is both.

</details>
