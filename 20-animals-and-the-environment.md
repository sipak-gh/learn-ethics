# Chapter 20 — Animals and the Environment

In 1789, in a footnote to a book on legislation, Jeremy Bentham asked a question that has not gone away: what is it that should "trace the insuperable line" between the beings whose suffering counts and those whose suffering does not? His answer, "Can they suffer?", started the modern debate about animals. Two centuries later, the debate has been joined by a second one, about the natural world as a whole: forests, rivers, species, the atmosphere, and the people not yet born who will live with what we do to them. Both debates ask the same question about the boundaries of morality. Most of the theories in this course were built by humans, for humans, about how humans should treat one another. This chapter asks who and what else is owed something, and what. The first half covers animals: the old view that they do not count, Bentham and the utilitarian case (Peter Singer), the rights case (Tom Regan), the Kantian case (Christine Korsgaard), and the skeptics. The second half covers the environment: the varieties of value in nature, Aldo Leopold's land ethic, deep ecology and environmental justice. It ends with climate change, which Stephen Gardiner has called a "perfect moral storm" because it combines nearly every feature that makes moral problems hard. Previous: [Chapter 19 — Bioethics](19-bioethics.md). Next: [Chapter 21 — Technology and AI Ethics](21-technology-and-ai-ethics.md).

## Who counts?

Every moral theory needs an answer to a question it rarely asks out loud: whose interests, rights or good does it cover? Philosophers call this the question of *moral status* or *moral considerability*. A being has moral status if it matters morally *for its own sake*, so that what happens to it can wrong it, not just its owner or the people who care about it. A car has no moral status: if you smash my car, you wrong me, not the car. A child has moral status: if you hurt my child, you wrong me, but you also wrong the child.

Three questions should be kept apart. First, *which* beings have moral status? Candidates include all humans, all sentient beings, all living things, species, ecosystems, and future people. Second, *on what basis*? Proposed criteria include rationality, membership in the human species, the capacity to suffer, being a subject of a life, being alive, and being part of a biotic community. Third, *how much*? Even if a mouse has moral status, its interests might count equally with similar human interests (the egalitarian view) or count for less (hierarchical views).

The history of ethics is in part a history of answers to the first question growing wider, from family and tribe to nation, to all humans regardless of race or sex, and perhaps further. Charles Darwin thought this widening was the natural course of moral development:

> This virtue, one of the noblest with which man is endowed, seems to arise incidentally from our sympathies becoming more tender and more widely diffused, until they are extended to all sentient beings.
>
> — Charles Darwin, *The Descent of Man*, chapter IV

The "virtue" is humanity toward animals, which Darwin called "one of the latest moral acquisitions." Whether its extension is moral progress or sentimental confusion is the question of the first half of this chapter. The Irish historian W. E. H. Lecky and, later, Peter Singer described the widening as an "expanding circle." Critics note that the image assumes the answer: a circle that expands to include ants has not obviously become wiser.

## The traditional view: animals as things

For most of Western philosophy, animals had at most indirect moral status. Aristotle held that plants exist for animals and animals for humans. Aquinas held that cruelty to animals is wrong mainly because it might train people in cruelty to humans. The strongest version came from René Descartes, who held that animals are complex machines without minds. Speech, he argued, is the sign of thought, and even the dullest human invents signs to communicate while the cleverest parrot only repeats:

> And this proves not only that the brutes have less reason than man, but that they have none at all
>
> — René Descartes, *Discourse on Method*, Part V, tr. John Veitch

If animals are automata, their cries are like the squeal of a machine, and nothing done to them is cruel. Some of Descartes's followers apparently drew that conclusion in the vivisection laboratories of the seventeenth century, though historians dispute how literally Descartes himself meant it.

Kant gave the most influential version of the indirect view. In his lectures on ethics he held that we have no duties *to* animals, since they are not rational beings, but we have duties *regarding* them. Cruelty to animals is wrong because a person who is cruel to animals becomes hard in his dealings with people; kindness to them cultivates humane feelings that serve our duties to humans ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)).

Not everyone agreed. Montaigne, in his essay "Of Cruelty," wrote of animals and even plants:

> there is nevertheless a certain respect, a general duty of humanity, not only to beasts that have life and sense, but even to trees, and plants. We owe justice to men, and graciousness and benignity to other creatures that are capable of it
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, "Of Cruelty," *Essays*, Book II, tr. Charles Cotton

Montaigne's distinction is worth noticing: justice for humans, kindness for animals. That is roughly the common-sense view today in most countries. Animal welfare laws forbid "unnecessary" cruelty, while the law treats animals as property and allows them to be bred, confined and killed for food, research and clothing. The philosophical debate since the 1970s is largely about whether that combination is coherent.

## Bentham: "Can they suffer?"

Bentham's footnote is short and worth reading whole. He compares the legal status of animals to that of slaves, whose treatment he expects to improve, and then asks what could justify the line between humans and animals:

> But a full-grown horse or dog is beyond comparison a more rational, as well as a more conversable animal, than an infant of a day, or a week, or even a month, old. But suppose the case were otherwise, what would it avail? the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?
>
> — Jeremy Bentham, *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, chapter XVII, note

The passage packs in two arguments. The first is that the usual criteria (reason, language) do not separate all humans from all animals: a horse reasons better than a newborn. This is the ancestor of what is now called the *argument from marginal cases* (or, less offensively, the *argument from species overlap*). The second is the positive claim: for a utilitarian, what makes a being's interests count is that it can experience pleasure and pain ([Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)). If the hedonist account of value is right, the capacity to suffer is the only relevant criterion, and the line between species is like the line of skin color, a biological fact that is not a moral one.

John Stuart Mill drew the same conclusion. In *Utilitarianism* he defined the standard of morality as rules by which a happy existence "might be, to the greatest extent possible, secured to all mankind; and not to them only, but, so far as the nature of things admits, to the whole sentient creation." Bentham himself, however, did not think his argument condemned killing animals for food. As Henry Salt reported, Bentham held that "their pains do not equal our enjoyments," since a quick death at our hands might be less painful than death in nature, and animals do not dread the future. Bentham opposed torment, not slaughter.

The nineteenth century turned some of this into law. Britain's first animal cruelty statute, Martin's Act of 1822, protected cattle, horses and other farm animals; the society that became the RSPCA was founded in 1824. In 1892 Henry Salt, a former Eton schoolmaster, vegetarian and socialist, published *Animals' Rights*, which argued that animals have rights in the same sense as humans (a "restricted freedom" to live their own lives) and that the obstacle was a picture of the world in which humans stood apart:

> If we are ever going to do justice to the lower races, we must get rid of the antiquated notion of a "great gulf" fixed between them and mankind, and must recognize the common bond of humanity that unites all living beings in one universal brotherhood.
>
> — Henry S. Salt, *Animals' Rights*, chapter I

Salt's writing on vegetarianism impressed the young Gandhi, who met him in London, and *Animals' Rights* was largely forgotten until Peter Singer revived interest in it. Before Salt, Thoreau had made a prediction from his cabin at Walden:

> Whatever my own practice may be, I have no doubt that it is a part of the destiny of the human race, in its gradual improvement, to leave off eating animals, as surely as the savage tribes have left off eating each other when they came in contact with the more civilized.
>
> — Henry David Thoreau, *Walden*, "Higher Laws"

"Whatever my own practice may be" is a confession: Thoreau fished and ate meat. Many people today stand where he did, persuaded in principle and divided in practice.

## Singer: equal consideration and speciesism

The modern debate began with Peter Singer's essay "All Animals Are Equal" (1974) and his book *Animal Liberation* (1975). Singer, a preference utilitarian ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)), argued from a principle almost everyone already accepts.

The **principle of equal consideration of interests** says that equal interests should be given equal weight, whoever has them. It does not say that all beings should be treated the same: a pig has no interest in voting, so equal consideration does not require giving pigs the vote. It says that where a pig and a human have the same interest (in not suffering, for instance), the pig's suffering counts as much as the human's. The principle explains what is wrong with racism and sexism: they give less weight to some people's interests because of a characteristic (race, sex) that has nothing to do with those interests. Singer's step was to point out that the same is true of species. Giving less weight to an animal's pain because it belongs to another species is **speciesism**, a term coined by the British psychologist Richard Ryder in 1970. It is, Singer argued, a prejudice of the same structure as racism.

The argument does not say animals and humans are equal in every way. Humans have interests animals lack, and some human pains (such as the dread of a planned death) are compounded by understanding. In some cases this makes human suffering greater, and equal consideration allows for that. But it rules out the reasoning behind most of our use of animals, which is that their suffering is trivial because it is theirs.

Singer's practical conclusions came from the facts of modern agriculture, which he documented at length. Most animals raised for food in rich countries now live in industrial systems: broiler chickens bred to grow so fast their legs often fail, laying hens in cages, pregnant sows in crates too small to turn around in, cattle in feedlots. Worldwide, by the UN Food and Agriculture Organization's figures, more than eighty billion land animals have been slaughtered for food each year in the 2020s, most of them chickens, along with a far larger number of fish. If the suffering of these animals counts equally with similar human suffering, Singer argued, the system is among the largest moral catastrophes in history, and the minimum response is to stop buying its products. He also attacked research on animals where the benefits were trivial or the same knowledge could be had another way.

Three features of Singer's view surprise readers. First, it is not a rights view. A utilitarian has no objection in principle to using animals, or humans, if the overall balance of interests favors it. Singer accepts that some animal experiments may be justified when the benefits are great enough, and he adds that consistency would then permit the same experiment on a human with similar capacities. Second, it treats killing differently from suffering. In *Practical Ethics* (1979), Singer argued that beings who are not self-conscious, with no sense of themselves as existing over time, have no preference to go on living, so painlessly killing one and replacing it with another equally happy being may involve no loss. This "replaceability" argument would permit humane farming of some animals in principle, though Singer thinks such farming is rare in practice. Third, the argument from marginal cases cuts both ways: if an infant or a severely cognitively disabled human has capacities no greater than a pig's, Singer's view implies that their interests in continued life are no greater than the pig's. That conclusion has made him one of the most protested philosophers alive, and critics have argued that it shows the starting point is wrong.

## Regan: the rights view

Tom Regan, in *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983), agreed with Singer's conclusions about farming and research but rejected his utilitarianism. Utilitarianism, Regan argued, treats individuals as mere containers of value: what matters is the total of pleasure and pain, and an individual can be sacrificed if the total rises. That is wrong for humans, as the standard objections to utilitarianism show ([Chapter 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md)). It is equally wrong for animals.

Instead, Regan argued that every *subject-of-a-life* has **inherent value**, equal in all who have it. A subject of a life is a being with beliefs and desires, perception, memory, a sense of its own future, an emotional life, preferences, the ability to act on its goals, and a welfare that can go well or badly for it, whatever its usefulness to others. Regan argued that normal mammals over about a year old clearly qualify, and that birds probably do; he left open where the line falls further down. Because inherent value is equal and not reducible to experiences, every subject of a life has an equal right to be treated with respect, which means never being treated merely as a resource for others' benefit.

The rights view is *abolitionist*. It does not ask for bigger cages but for empty ones: the end of animal agriculture, of animal research, and of commercial and sport hunting and trapping. Reforms that make exploitation more humane, Regan argued, are like making slavery more humane.

Critics raise two problems. The first is a famous case of Regan's own. Four humans and a dog are in a lifeboat that can hold only four. Regan said the dog should go overboard, because death is a greater harm to a human, whose life has more possibilities for satisfaction, than to a dog, and he added that this would hold even if the choice were between one human and a million dogs. Critics replied that this concedes that lives differ in value, which threatens the claim that inherent value is equal, and that it uses aggregative reasoning while rejecting it. The second problem concerns the boundary. Regan's criterion is demanding, and much of the strongest evidence of suffering involves animals (fish, crustaceans, perhaps insects) that may not qualify. A rights view built on rich mental lives may protect fewer animals than a utilitarian view built on sentience.

## Korsgaard: a Kantian case for animals

Kant's own view of animals seems to follow from his theory: only rational beings are ends in themselves, so animals are things. Christine Korsgaard, one of the leading Kantians of the late twentieth century (her constructivism appears in [Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)), argued in *Fellow Creatures* (2018) that Kant misapplied his own theory.

Her argument runs roughly as follows. When we act, we treat our ends as good, and so we treat ourselves as ends in themselves, beings whose good matters. But what exactly do we value in valuing ourselves? Not only our rational nature. Much of what we care about in our own good is not rational at all: not being in pain, being fed, being safe, being able to move freely. These are goods we share with other animals; they matter to us because we are creatures for whom things can go well or badly. So when we value ourselves as ends, we value ourselves partly *as animals*, and consistency commits us to valuing every being for whom things can go well or badly in the same way. Animals cannot legislate, so they are not ends in themselves in the sense of being moral agents who make claims. But they are ends in themselves in the sense that their good matters for its own sake.

Korsgaard's conclusions are strong: she argues that factory farming and most animal research are wrong, and that we should not eat animals in the world as it is. She also argues, against Singer, that animals are not replaceable, because each animal's good is good *for it*, and there is no impersonal standpoint from which one creature's good can be swapped for another's. Her view gives Kantians, who usually emphasize what separates persons from things, a way to join the animal debate on their own terms.

## The skeptics

Not everyone has been persuaded. Three kinds of objection are worth knowing.

**Contractualist exclusion.** If morality is a system of principles that rational agents could agree to ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)), animals are outside it, since they cannot make agreements. Peter Carruthers (*The Animals Issue*, 1992) argued that animals therefore have no direct moral status, though cruelty to them is wrong because it reveals a bad character and offends people who care about them. Critics pointed out that the same reasoning would exclude infants and some cognitively disabled humans, and that Scanlon's contractualism can include beings who cannot reason by having others speak for them as trustees. Mark Rowlands argued that if the parties behind Rawls's veil of ignorance did not know their species, they would choose strong protections for animals.

**The relational objection.** Cora Diamond, in "Eating Meat and Eating People" (1978), criticized both Singer and Regan for missing how morality actually works. We do not eat our dead or our pets, she argued, and the reason is not that they have interests or rights that would be violated; a dead person has neither. It is that a person, or a pet, is a certain kind of being for us, someone with whom we share a life. The right response to animals, she suggested, comes not from a theory of moral status but from seeing them as "fellow creatures" who share our mortality and vulnerability. Diamond was not defending meat-eating; she was arguing that the case against it rests on something deeper than interests.

**Human dignity.** Many religious and humanist thinkers hold that human beings have a dignity that animals lack, not because of any capacity a given human has but because of the kind of being humans are. On this view the argument from marginal cases fails because an infant is a member of a rational kind, even if it is not yet rational. Defenders of animals reply that this makes the line depend on species membership after all, and that it needs to explain why membership in a kind, rather than an individual's actual capacities, should determine how it may be treated.

### The science of sentience

Underneath the philosophical arguments lies an empirical question: which animals can suffer? The evidence for mammals and birds is strong, and in 2012 a group of neuroscientists issued the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness to say so. The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024), signed by hundreds of researchers, went further: it held that there is strong scientific support for conscious experience in mammals and birds and at least a realistic possibility of it in all vertebrates, including fish, and in many invertebrates, including octopuses, crabs and insects. In 2021 a review led by Jonathan Birch for the UK government concluded that decapod crustaceans and cephalopods are sentient, and the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act of 2022 covers them. Birch argues that where sentience is a realistic possibility, we should take proportionate precautions rather than waiting for certainty that may never come. The number of animals at stake is vast: estimates of the number of wild fish caught each year run to the hundreds of billions or more.

### Beyond the farm

Three further questions show how far these views reach. *Wild animal suffering*: if animal suffering matters, most of it happens in nature, through predation, parasitism, disease and starvation. Are we obliged to reduce it? Most people find the idea absurd or dangerous, but it follows from some versions of the utilitarian view, and philosophers have taken it seriously. *Domestication*: Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka's *Zoopolis* (2011) proposed a political theory in which domesticated animals are co-citizens, wild animals have sovereign communities, and animals who live among us without being domesticated (pigeons, rats) are something like residents. *Capabilities*: Martha Nussbaum (*Justice for Animals*, 2023) argued that each animal has a characteristic form of life and that justice requires protecting its capabilities to live that life, not only its freedom from pain.

## The environment: what has value?

The second half of this chapter moves from individual animals to nature as a whole. The questions change. A forest cannot suffer. A species is not a subject of a life. A river has no preferences. If they matter, it is for different reasons.

The modern environmental movement grew from an old sense that something was being lost. In 1864 the American diplomat George Perkins Marsh published *Man and Nature*, a study of how deforestation, drainage and overgrazing had ruined landscapes from the Mediterranean to New England:

> Man has too long forgotten that the earth was given to him for usufruct alone, not for consumption, still less for profligate waste.
>
> — George Perkins Marsh, *Man and Nature*, chapter I

"Usufruct" is a legal term for the right to use something and enjoy its fruits without damaging the thing itself, as a tenant may farm land but not strip its soil. Marsh's view is human-centered: the earth is for human use, but for the use of all generations, not just ours. He also put the problem in terms that ecologists would later make precise: "man is everywhere a disturbing agent. Wherever he plants his foot, the harmonies of nature are turned to discords."

### Three kinds of environmental ethics

Environmental ethics distinguishes views by where they locate value.

**Anthropocentric** views hold that only humans (and perhaps other sentient beings) have moral status, and nature matters because of what it does for them: clean air and water, food, medicines, beauty, recreation, the stability of the climate. This is not a weak position. Most arguments for environmental protection are anthropocentric, and they are often enough: destroying a forest that holds a watershed together harms the people downstream. An "enlightened" anthropocentrism, attending to future generations and to the non-material goods nature provides, supports a great deal of protection.

**Biocentric** views hold that every living thing has a good of its own, and that this good matters morally. Albert Schweitzer, physician, theologian and organist, came to this view on a river in Gabon in 1915, when the phrase "reverence for life" came to him as his boat passed through a herd of hippopotamuses:

> A man is truly ethical only when he obeys the compulsion to help all life which he is able to assist, and shrinks from injuring anything that lives.
>
> — Albert Schweitzer, *Civilization and Ethics*, tr. C. T. Campion

Schweitzer did not think this could be followed perfectly. A doctor kills bacteria to save patients, and a farmer mows a field. His point was that each such killing should be done knowingly, as a necessity, never thoughtlessly. Paul Taylor gave biocentrism a systematic form in *Respect for Nature* (1986): every organism is a "teleological center of life" pursuing its own good, and all have equal inherent worth. The obvious difficulty is that taken strictly, equal worth for every organism makes ordinary life (eating, building, walking on grass) impossible to justify, and Taylor's priority rules for resolving conflicts were criticized as either arbitrary or as quietly reintroducing human priority.

**Ecocentric** (or holist) views hold that wholes, such as species, ecosystems and the biotic community, have moral status, and not just the individual organisms in them. This is the most radical departure from traditional ethics, and its classic statement is Aldo Leopold's.

The difference between anthropocentric and non-anthropocentric views is often tested by a thought experiment that Richard Routley (later Sylvan) proposed in 1973. Suppose you are the last person on earth, and you will soon die. Before you go, you destroy every remaining living thing: every tree, every animal, every plant. No human will ever be harmed. Have you done anything wrong? Most people feel that you have. If so, nature has some value that does not depend on its use to humans. Anthropocentrists reply that the intuition comes from a lifetime of seeing nature as valuable for us and cannot be trusted in a world without us.

### Leopold's land ethic

Aldo Leopold was a forester and wildlife manager who began his career killing wolves for the US Forest Service, on the theory that fewer predators meant more deer for hunters. In "Thinking Like a Mountain" he describes shooting a wolf and reaching her in time to watch the fire go out of her eyes. Years later, he writes, he watched the deer populations explode in states that had exterminated their wolves, strip the mountains of vegetation, and starve. The mountain, he concluded, knew something the hunters did not: that wolves were part of what kept it whole.

"The Land Ethic," the last essay of *A Sand County Almanac* (1949), published after Leopold died fighting a grass fire on a neighbor's farm, proposes that ethics has evolved by enlarging the community whose members we treat as having claims on us, and that the next step is to include "the land": soils, waters, plants and animals together. Its summary maxim is among the most quoted sentences in environmental thought:

> A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. It is wrong when it tends otherwise.
>
> — Aldo Leopold, "The Land Ethic," *A Sand County Almanac* (1949)

Leopold did not oppose hunting, farming or logging. He was a lifelong hunter. His point was that these activities should be judged by their effects on the health of the whole system, not only by their returns to us, and that the citizen of a land community should feel toward it something like the loyalty a person feels toward a human community.

The philosopher J. Baird Callicott developed the land ethic into a full theory in the 1980s, grounding it in Hume's and Darwin's view that ethics rests on social sentiments ([Chapter 8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md)), which evolution has extended from kin to community and can extend further. But the land ethic faces a serious objection, which Tom Regan stated sharply. If what matters is the integrity of the biotic community, individuals can be sacrificed for the whole. Culling deer to protect a forest is then not merely permitted but required. And humans are members of the biotic community too, so the same reasoning would seem to require culling humans when there are too many of us. Regan called this "environmental fascism." Callicott replied that the land ethic adds to, rather than replaces, our older obligations to human beings, which still take priority in most cases. Critics replied that once you add ranking rules, the land ethic loses much of its distinctive force.

Animal ethics and environmental ethics therefore often conflict. An animal rights view objects to culling invasive species to protect native ones; an ecocentric view requires it. A utilitarian may worry about the suffering of a deer starved by overpopulation; an ecocentrist worries about the forest the deer is eating. Callicott called the relation between animal liberation and environmental ethics "a triangular affair," and there is still no agreed way to reconcile them.

### Wildness, preservation and deep ecology

The American preservation movement drew on a romantic idea of wildness. Thoreau wrote, in "Walking," that "in Wildness is the preservation of the World." John Muir, the Scottish-born naturalist who founded the Sierra Club, came to see nature not as a storehouse of resources but as a community of beings with their own claims. Walking a thousand miles from Kentucky to the Gulf of Mexico in 1867, he reflected on alligators and snakes:

> How narrow we selfish, conceited creatures are in our sympathies! how blind to the rights of all the rest of creation!
>
> — John Muir, *A Thousand-Mile Walk to the Gulf*, chapter V

Muir's most famous fight was over Hetch Hetchy, a valley in Yosemite National Park that San Francisco wanted to dam for a water supply. Muir saw it as sacrilege: "Dam Hetch Hetchy! As well dam for water-tanks the people's cathedrals and churches, for no holier temple has ever been consecrated by the heart of man." His opponent, Gifford Pinchot, the first head of the US Forest Service, stood for *conservation*: the wise use of natural resources for the greatest good of the greatest number over the longest time. Congress approved the dam in 1913, and Muir died the next year. The split between preservation (protecting nature from use) and conservation (managing it for use) still runs through environmental politics.

In 1973 the Norwegian philosopher Arne Naess distinguished "shallow" ecology, which opposes pollution and resource depletion for the sake of people in rich countries, from "deep" ecology, which holds that all living beings have equal intrinsic value and that human flourishing is compatible with a much smaller human population and a much lighter footprint. Deep ecology inspired activists but was criticized, notably by the Indian historian Ramachandra Guha, as a rich-world ideology: the creation of wilderness parks in poor countries had often meant evicting the people who lived there.

The historian Lynn White Jr. had earlier argued, in a famous 1967 article, that the roots of the ecological crisis lay in the Western Christian idea that humans were given "dominion" over nature. Critics replied that environmental destruction has occurred under every religion and none, and many Christian thinkers developed an ethic of "stewardship" in response, which interprets dominion as responsibility rather than license.

### Environmental justice

A different critique came from communities of color in the United States. In 1982 residents of Warren County, North Carolina, a mostly Black county, protested the siting of a landfill for soil contaminated with toxic chemicals. A 1987 study by the United Church of Christ found that race was the strongest predictor of where hazardous waste facilities were located in the country, and the sociologist Robert Bullard documented the pattern in *Dumping in Dixie* (1990). The *environmental justice* movement argued that environmental ethics had been too concerned with wilderness and not enough with where people live, work and breathe. Its central claims concern distribution (who bears the burdens of pollution) and participation (who has a say in decisions that affect them). These are questions of justice in the sense of [Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md), and they matter even on the most anthropocentric view of nature. They are also, as the next section shows, at the center of climate ethics.

## Climate change: a perfect moral storm

Burning fossil fuels and clearing forests adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere, where much of it stays for centuries. The result is warming. By the early 2020s, average global surface temperatures were about 1.2°C above pre-industrial levels, and 2024 was the first calendar year in which the average exceeded 1.5°C, the more ambitious target of the Paris Agreement of 2015. The consequences include heat waves, droughts, floods, sea-level rise, crop failures and the extinction of species, and they fall hardest on people in poor countries who have done least to cause them.

Stephen Gardiner argued that climate change is a "perfect moral storm," in which three separate storms converge.

The **global storm**: emissions anywhere affect people everywhere, the countries that emit most are not the ones most harmed, and there is no world government to make everyone act. This has the structure of what the ecologist Garrett Hardin called "the tragedy of the commons" (1968): each herder gains the full benefit of adding another animal to a shared pasture and bears only a fraction of the cost of overgrazing, so all add animals until the pasture is ruined.

The **intergenerational storm**: the costs of today's emissions fall mostly on people who are not yet born and cannot vote, bargain or retaliate. Each generation has an incentive to enjoy the benefits of emitting and pass on the costs. Unlike the tragedy of the commons, this problem cannot be solved by cooperation among those present, because the victims are not present.

The **theoretical storm**: our moral theories are poorly equipped to handle problems involving vast numbers of people, long time scales, scientific uncertainty and diffuse causation.

Gardiner added a fourth danger, *moral corruption*: because the situation offers so many reasons for delay, and so many convenient arguments (uncertainty, other countries' failures, the cost of action), we are tempted to believe whatever excuses inaction. The danger is not only that we will fail to act, but that we will convince ourselves we are acting well.

### What do we owe future generations?

The intergenerational question raises a puzzle that Derek Parfit made famous in *Reasons and Persons* (1984). Suppose a society can choose between two energy policies: Depletion, which uses resources freely and makes life worse in two centuries, and Conservation. Who will exist in two centuries depends on which policy is chosen, since different policies change who meets whom and when children are conceived. So the people who will live under Depletion would not have existed under Conservation. If their lives are still worth living, Depletion has not made them worse off than they would otherwise have been, since otherwise they would not exist. Has it harmed anyone? This is the **non-identity problem**. Most people are sure Depletion is wrong, but explaining why requires giving up the simple idea that wrongs must make particular people worse off. Parfit concluded that we need impersonal principles, about how good the outcome is, not only principles about harming individuals.

A second question is how to weigh future costs against present ones. Economists *discount* future costs, partly because people in the future will probably be richer, and partly because people prefer benefits sooner. The second reason, "pure time preference," is the controversial one. Philosophers since Sidgwick have held that the mere position of a person in time is no reason to count her well-being for less, and the *Stern Review* (2006) on the economics of climate change set pure time preference near zero, which made strong action look worthwhile. Economists such as William Nordhaus used higher discount rates based on observed market behavior, which made gradual action look better. The disagreement is not technical. It is a moral question about how much our descendants matter.

### Who should pay?

Even if action is required, who should bear its costs? Three principles compete.

The **polluter pays principle** says those who caused the problem should pay to fix it. Rich industrialized countries have produced most of the carbon dioxide emitted since 1850. The objection is that most past emissions were made by people now dead, many of whom did not know the harm they were causing.

The **beneficiary pays principle** answers that objection: whoever caused the emissions, people in rich countries today benefit from the wealth they created, and so should bear the costs.

The **ability to pay principle** says the costs should fall on those best able to bear them, whatever their history.

All three point, in practice, toward rich countries paying more. Henry Shue added a distinction between *subsistence emissions*, needed for people to meet basic needs, and *luxury emissions*. Justice, he argued, cannot require the poor to give up subsistence emissions so that the rich may keep their luxury ones.

### Does my driving matter?

The hardest climate question for individuals is whether personal choices matter. Your lifetime emissions are a vanishingly small fraction of the total. Walter Sinnott-Armstrong argued in "It's Not My Fault" (2005) that taking a Sunday drive for fun in a gas-guzzling car is not morally wrong, because no individual's emissions make a detectable difference to anyone's suffering. Our real obligation, he said, is political: to press governments to change the rules.

Others disagree. Parfit argued, in a chapter of *Reasons and Persons* called "Five Mistakes in Moral Mathematics," that it is a mistake to ignore effects that are individually imperceptible. In his example, a thousand torturers each turn a dial that increases the pain of a thousand victims by an imperceptible amount. Each one's act makes no perceptible difference, but together they inflict severe pain, and each is responsible for a share of it. John Broome (*Climate Matters*, 2012) argued that your emissions do cause harm, since they add to a total with harmful effects, and that a duty of justice not to harm others requires you to reduce your emissions to zero or offset them. Shelly Kagan (2011) argued that even where effects come at thresholds, an individual act has an expected impact equal to the average, since it has a small chance of being the act that tips a threshold with large consequences. John Nolt estimated, on some contested assumptions, that the lifetime emissions of an average American cause serious harm to one or two future people.

There is also a virtue-ethical answer: even if your emissions make no difference, living in a way that ignores a catastrophe you are part of says something about your character. And there is a care-ethical one ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)): our responsibilities arise from relationships, including relationships with the people and places that our way of life affects. These answers do not settle how much an individual must give up, but they explain why "it makes no difference" is not the end of the matter.

### Where the two halves meet

Animal ethics and climate ethics often point the same way. Livestock is responsible for a significant share of global greenhouse gas emissions (the UN Food and Agriculture Organization's estimate in 2013 was about 14.5 percent), mostly through methane from cattle and the clearing of forests for pasture and feed. A shift away from industrial animal agriculture would reduce both animal suffering and emissions. But they can diverge: a rights view may oppose culling invasive species that a climate or biodiversity policy requires, and a climate policy may favor intensive systems that use less land per kilogram but confine animals more. Any serious environmental ethics must decide how to weigh individual animals, species, ecosystems and human beings, present and future, when their interests conflict.

## Living it

These arguments have uncomfortable implications for daily life. Some practical steps:

1. **Find your line, and test it.** Write down which animals you think it is wrong to make suffer for food, and why. Then check whether what you eat matches. If it doesn't, decide whether to change your view or your diet. Bentham's question works as a test: of each animal, ask whether it can suffer, not whether it is cute, rare or familiar.
2. **Try a reducetarian experiment.** Cut out the products of the most intensive systems (caged eggs, factory-farmed chicken and pork) for a month, and notice what that costs you. Many people find the practical cost is lower than they expected and the moral clarity higher.
3. **Measure your emissions.** Use a carbon calculator to estimate your annual emissions. Identify the two largest sources (usually flying, driving, home heating or diet) and pick one to cut. If you fly, consider offsetting through a reputable program, and note Broome's argument that offsetting is a minimum, not a virtue.
4. **Act politically.** Sinnott-Armstrong is right that governments can do what individuals cannot. Find out what climate and animal-welfare measures are under debate where you live and do one thing to support the ones you think are right.
5. **Spend time in a place.** Leopold's ethic grew from years of watching one farm in Wisconsin. Choose a place near you (a park, a river, a patch of woods) and visit it regularly for a season. Notice what lives there and what changes. It is hard to care about the integrity of a community you have never looked at.

## Summary

- The question of *moral status* asks which beings matter for their own sake, on what basis, and how much. Darwin saw the extension of sympathy to animals as a late moral acquisition.
- The traditional Western view (Aristotle, Aquinas, Descartes, Kant) gave animals at most indirect moral status; Montaigne dissented. Most law today forbids "unnecessary" cruelty while treating animals as property.
- Bentham asked "Can they suffer?" and argued that reason and language do not separate all humans from all animals. Salt argued for animals' rights in 1892.
- Singer's principle of equal consideration of interests condemns speciesism and factory farming; as a utilitarian he allows some trade-offs and holds that non-self-conscious beings may be replaceable.
- Regan's rights view holds that every subject-of-a-life has equal inherent value and demands abolition of animal use; it faces the lifeboat case and a demanding boundary.
- Korsgaard argues that in valuing ourselves as ends we value our animal nature, and so must value all beings for whom things can go well or badly.
- Skeptics include contractualists (Carruthers), relational thinkers (Diamond, who argues from "fellow creatures" rather than interests) and defenders of human dignity. The science of sentience now extends realistic possibility to fish and many invertebrates.
- Environmental ethics divides into anthropocentric, biocentric (Schweitzer, Taylor) and ecocentric (Leopold, Callicott) views. Routley's last-man argument tests whether nature has non-instrumental value; Regan's charge of "environmental fascism" and the conflict between animal and environmental ethics remain unresolved.
- Preservation (Muir) and conservation (Pinchot), deep ecology (Naess) and its critics (Guha), and environmental justice (Warren County, Bullard) shape environmental politics.
- Climate change is a "perfect moral storm" (Gardiner): global, intergenerational and theoretical. It raises the non-identity problem, the ethics of discounting, the choice among polluter pays, beneficiary pays and ability to pay, and the question whether individual emissions matter (Sinnott-Armstrong against Parfit, Broome, Kagan and Nolt).

**You:** It matters a bit. I don't like thinking about it. But it's a chicken. It's not a person.

**You:** Yes, probably. Chickens feel pain.

**Jeremy Bentham:** Then its pain is a pain. Does it hurt less because it happens to a bird?

**You:** Maybe it does. Maybe a chicken's pain is dimmer than mine.

**Jeremy Bentham:** Possibly. That is a question of fact, and if it is true, you may count it. But you did not say "its pain is dimmer." You said "it's a chicken." Is the species itself a reason?

**You:** I suppose not. If an alien with feathers suffered the way I do, its suffering would count.

**Jeremy Bentham:** So what counts is the suffering, not the feathers. Now, how much suffering went into your sandwich?

**You:** Weeks of it, if what you describe is right. For a few minutes of lunch.

**Jeremy Bentham:** I will confess something. I did not condemn killing animals for food. I thought their deaths at our hands could be quicker than death in nature, and that they do not dread the future as we do.

**You:** So you'd let me eat the sandwich?

**Jeremy Bentham:** If the chicken had lived well and died quickly, perhaps. I opposed torment, not death. Is that the chicken you are eating?

**You:** No. The chicken I'm eating was tormented. So by your own standard, the problem isn't eating meat. It's this meat.

**Jeremy Bentham:** And what would it cost you to find meat that was not produced by torment, or to do without?

**You:** More money. Some inconvenience. Less chicken.

**Jeremy Bentham:** Set that against six weeks of a creature's misery, multiplied by every chicken you will eat in your life. Do the sum honestly.

**You:** The sum isn't close. I think I've been avoiding doing it because I knew that.

**Jeremy Bentham:** That is the usual reason people avoid arithmetic.

### Dialogue 2 — On the deer and the forest

**Aldo Leopold:** A forest near your town is overrun with deer. They have stripped the young trees, and nothing new is growing. Wildflowers and songbirds are disappearing. The wolves were killed a century ago. The park service proposes a cull. Do you support it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Leopold in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I don't like it. The deer haven't done anything wrong. Each one has a life it cares about. Shooting them to save some trees seems backwards.

**Aldo Leopold:** What happens to the deer if nobody shoots them?

**You:** I suppose they keep eating until there's nothing left, and then they starve.

**Aldo Leopold:** I have seen that. A starving deer herd is not a pretty thing. Is starvation kinder than a bullet?

**You:** No. But that's an argument for contraception, or bringing back wolves, not for shooting.

**Aldo Leopold:** Good. Bringing back the wolf is what I would prefer. Will your neighbors accept wolves?

**You:** Probably not. The farmers would fight it.

**Aldo Leopold:** Then you have three choices: the cull, the starvation, or a long fight for wolves while the forest dies. Which do you choose?

**You:** You're making it sound like the forest matters more than the deer.

**Aldo Leopold:** I am saying the deer is part of the forest. Its life depends on the plants, the soil and the predators. Can you care for the deer without caring for the thing that makes it a deer?

**You:** Maybe not. But if I follow you, individual lives don't matter, only the whole. That's where the "environmental fascism" charge comes from. If there are too many humans, do we cull them too?

**Aldo Leopold:** No. My land ethic adds to our duties to one another; it does not erase them. But I will ask you this: is it only for humans that individual lives matter?

**You:** No. That's why I didn't like the cull.

**Aldo Leopold:** Then you are caught between two goods: the deer's life and the community's health. I do not think a theory will dissolve that conflict for you.

**You:** So what do I do?

**Aldo Leopold:** Learn the place. Find out what the forest needs and what the deer need, and what can be done with the least killing. Then act, and keep watching. A land ethic is not a formula. It is a way of paying attention.

**You:** Support the cull for now, push for the wolves, and keep looking.

**Aldo Leopold:** That is thinking like a mountain.

### Dialogue 3 — On whether my emissions matter

**Derek Parfit:** You are deciding whether to fly to a friend's wedding on another continent. The flight adds about two tonnes of carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. Your friend says it makes no difference to the climate whether you go or not. Is she right?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Parfit in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** In a way she's right. The plane flies whether or not I'm on it, and even if it didn't, two tonnes is nothing against billions. No one will ever be worse off because I went.

**Derek Parfit:** Consider a case. A thousand torturers each have a dial attached to a thousand victims. Each turn of a dial increases each victim's pain by an amount too small to notice. Each torturer turns his dial once. At the end, every victim is in agony. Did any torturer do wrong?

**You:** All of them did. Together they caused agony.

**Derek Parfit:** But each made no perceptible difference to anyone.

**You:** So "no perceptible difference" doesn't mean "no difference." The pain was real, and it came from somewhere.

**Derek Parfit:** That is my claim. Imperceptible effects, summed over many people, can be very large. Now, does your flight differ from a torturer's turn?

**You:** The torturers intended harm. I just want to go to a wedding.

**Derek Parfit:** That affects how we judge you, not what you cause. If your emissions, summed with others', contribute to droughts and floods, is your share zero?

**You:** I suppose it's a very small share of something very large. Maybe a share of a death, somewhere, sometime.

**Derek Parfit:** Some have estimated it in those terms. And the people affected may not yet exist. Does that make it better?

**You:** It feels better. But if I left a bomb in a building that would go off in a century, the fact that its victims weren't born yet wouldn't excuse me.

**Derek Parfit:** Though you must be careful there. Which people will exist depends on what we all do, so you may not make any particular future person worse off than she would otherwise have been.

**You:** That's the non-identity problem. But it seems to prove too much. If it were right, nothing we did to future generations could wrong them.

**Derek Parfit:** I agree. So we need a principle about how good outcomes are, not only about whom we harm.

**You:** So what do I do about the wedding? Never fly?

**Derek Parfit:** I am not telling you that. I am telling you not to rely on the mistake that what makes no perceptible difference does not matter. Then weigh your friend's wedding honestly against your share of the harm.

**You:** I could go, offset the flight properly, and fly less for other things. And vote as if the total mattered, because it does.

**Derek Parfit:** That would be a reasonable conclusion. Whether it is enough, I leave to you.

</details>
