# Learn Ethics: From Socrates and Confucius to Building Your Own

Ethics is the part of philosophy you cannot opt out of. You already have answers to its questions: whether you may lie to spare a friend, how much you owe a stranger, whether a pig's suffering counts the same as yours, what you would do if you could never be caught. The only choice is whether to hold those answers by habit or by thought. This course is for people who want to think.

It is also a lot of fun. Ethics is full of good stories and better arguments. A condemned man refuses to escape from prison and explains why in one of the tightest arguments ever written. A shepherd finds a ring that makes him invisible. A runaway trolley sets off fifty years of philosophical car crashes. Confucius and Mencius argue about whether human nature is good; a seventeenth-century bishop demolishes egoism in a sermon to lawyers; Kant tells you not to lie even to a murderer at your door; Bentham asks of animals not "Can they reason?" but "Can they suffer?"; Nietzsche asks where your conscience came from, and does not like the answer. Along the way you meet every major way of answering "How should I live?": virtue, duty, consequences, contract, care, faith, and the suspicion that morality itself is a mistake. Each chapter asks you to try the idea on, test it against your own life, and argue back. By the last chapter you will write down your own ethical framework, know which theories it borrows from, and know its weakest points better than its critics do.

## Who it's for

Anyone who has wondered why they believe what they believe about right and wrong, and wants better answers than "it's obvious" or "who's to say?" No background in philosophy is assumed: the early chapters define every term and walk through every argument in premise and conclusion form. The course ramps up steadily. By the last third you will be reading the current scholarly debates (metaethics, moral responsibility, the ethics of AI) at roughly a graduate-seminar level, with the key papers, books and positions named so you can go further.

## What you need

- **No software** beyond an EPUB reader (Apple Books, Calibre, KOReader, Thorium, Foliate, or any e-reader).
- The **bundled readings**: 83 public-domain books from Project Gutenberg in [readings/](readings/README.md), from Plato, Aristotle, Confucius and the *Dhammapada* to Kant, Mill, Sidgwick, Nietzsche and Schweitzer.
- Some **procure-yourself books and articles**: most twentieth-century ethics is still in copyright, so you will need to buy, borrow or find these through a library. The essentials are Rawls's *A Theory of Justice*, Singer's "Famine, Affluence, and Morality," Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy," Mackie's *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, Thomson's "A Defense of Abortion" and Williams's *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*. Each chapter's Required reading block names the edition; [Appendix C](appendix-c-reading-list.md) collects them all.
- A notebook. Many exercises ask you to write, and the last chapter asks you to draft your own framework.

## How to use this course

1. **Read the chapters in order.** Each builds on the vocabulary and arguments of the ones before, and the course has an arc: from the tools of moral reasoning, through the great traditions East and West, to modern theories and their critics, then to the foundations (metaethics and moral psychology), the hard applied questions, and finally your own synthesis.
2. **Do the Required reading.** Each chapter opens with a short list: sections of the bundled EPUBs (linked directly) and, where needed, procure-yourself books and articles with full details. The chapter quotes and explains the key passages, but nothing replaces reading the originals. Optional "Further reading" follows.
3. **Try the Living it section.** Near the end of every chapter is a concrete practice: a way to test the idea against your own life, or to use it in building your own values. These are tied to the texts, not generic self-help.
4. **Use the Summary** to review before moving on.
5. **Work through the Dialogues.** Each chapter closes with two or three imagined conversations in the tradition of Plato's dialogues. A questioner probes you, or someone holding a view from the chapter; the questioner may be Socrates or another philosopher speaking in character (Aristotle, Confucius, Kant, Mill, Beauvoir, Nietzsche, Foot). The dialogues are invented teaching exercises, not quotations, though they stay true to each thinker's positions. Each one stops at a *Your turn* prompt: write your answer, then open **Continue the dialogue** to see how the conversation might go on.
