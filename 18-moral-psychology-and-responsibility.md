# Chapter 18 — Moral Psychology and Responsibility

In 1924 two wealthy Chicago students, Nathan Leopold and Richard Loeb, murdered a fourteen-year-old boy, Bobby Franks, to prove that they were superior beings who could commit a perfect crime. Leopold dropped his eyeglasses near the body. Their lawyer, Clarence Darrow, had them plead guilty and spent two days arguing that the boys had not made themselves: heredity, money, neglect and (in Leopold's case) a schoolboy diet of Nietzsche had made them, and it is cruel to hang someone for what the world has made of him. The judge spared their lives, though he said their youth, not Darrow's philosophy, decided it. Darrow's plea poses this chapter's questions. When is a person responsible for what they do? Does it matter whether their choices were caused? And what does the science of the moral mind (development, intuition and emotion, the power of situations) show about the agents we praise and blame? The first half covers the philosophy of responsibility: Aristotle on the voluntary, the free-will problem, P. F. Strawson's reactive attitudes and Harry Frankfurt's work on alternate possibilities and the will. The second covers empirical moral psychology: Piaget and Kohlberg, Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionism, Joshua Greene's dual-process theory, situationism and experimental philosophy. Moral luck gets its full treatment in [Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md), and the metaphysics of free will a broader survey in [the Philosophy course, Chapter 20](../learn-philosophy/20-philosophy-of-mind.md). Previous: [Chapter 17 — Metaethics](17-metaethics.md). Next: [Chapter 19 — Bioethics](19-bioethics.md).

## Two questions about the moral mind

"Moral psychology" names two projects that grew up apart and now overlap. The first is philosophical: what must an agent be like to be a fit target of praise and blame? It deals in the voluntary, intention, control, the will, character and excuse. The second is empirical: how do moral judgment and behavior actually work in human beings? Its tools are interviews, surveys, reaction times, brain scans and field experiments. Each needs the other. Theories of responsibility assume things about psychology (that people act from stable character, that their reasons cause their actions), and psychological findings get their interest from philosophical questions. A recurring lesson of this chapter is that each side has often been too quick to draw conclusions about the other.

## Aristotle: the voluntary and the involuntary

Aristotle begins with a practical need: we praise some acts and pardon others, and legislators must decide whom to punish. So we need a line between the voluntary (*hekousion*) and the involuntary (*akousion*):

> Now since Virtue is concerned with the regulation of feelings and actions, and praise and blame arise upon such as are voluntary, while for the involuntary allowance is made, and sometimes compassion is excited, it is perhaps a necessary task for those who are investigating the nature of Virtue to draw out the distinction between what is voluntary and what involuntary; and it is certainly useful for legislators, with respect to the assigning of honours and punishments.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* III.1, 1109b30–35 (tr. D. P. Chase)

There are two excusing conditions. The first is force:

> So then that seems to be compulsory "whose origination is from without, the party compelled contributing nothing."
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* III.1, 1110b15–17 (tr. D. P. Chase)

His example is a wind carrying you somewhere. The second is ignorance, but only of particulars (that the spear was sharp, that the man in the dark was your father). Ignorance of what is generally right excuses nothing; that is vice. Together they yield a definition:

> Now since all involuntary action is either upon compulsion or by reason of ignorance, Voluntary Action would seem to be "that whose origination is in the agent, he being aware of the particular details in which the action consists."
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* III.1, 1111a22–24 (tr. D. P. Chase)

The "origination" (*archē*, the moving principle) is the key word. A voluntary act is one whose source is in you. Two refinements make the account more than a definition.

**Mixed actions.** A captain throws his cargo overboard in a storm. No one does that willingly, "but with a view to his own and his shipmates' safety any one would who had any sense" (1110a9–11). Such acts are "of a mixed kind, but are most like voluntary actions," because at the moment of action the captain chooses them. Duress mitigates but does not turn an act into a mere event, and some acts (Aristotle mentions matricide) should not be done under any threat.

**Responsibility for character.** The deepest objection is Darrow's: "He is just like that." Aristotle answers that we built our characters by our actions:

> But perhaps a man is of such a character that he cannot attend to such things: still men are themselves the causes of having become such characters by living carelessly, and also of being unjust or destitute of self-control, the former by doing evil actions, the latter by spending their time in drinking and such-like; because the particular acts of working form corresponding characters...
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* III.5, 1114a3–8 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Law agreed: the drunk who does harm receives double penalties, "because the origination in such case lies in a man's own self: for he might have helped getting drunk" (1113b31–33). A formed character may be beyond change and still be yours, like a stone already thrown:

> There was a time then when he might have helped being ill, but now he has let himself go he cannot any longer; just as he who has let a stone out of his hand cannot recall it, and yet it rested with him to aim and throw it, because the origination was in his power.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* III.5, 1114a15–19 (tr. D. P. Chase)

This is a *tracing* account: responsibility for an act done from fixed character traces back to the choices that formed it. Contemporary theorists still use tracing to explain why a drunk driver is responsible for a crash he could not, at that moment, avoid. The weak point is the beginning. Aristotle himself says habituation from childhood "makes not a small but an important difference, or rather I would say it makes all the difference" (II.1, 1103b23–25). If character is shaped before reason matures, the tracing runs back to someone who was not yet a responsible agent. That gap is where the modern problem enters.

## The classical problem: determinism and its discontents

*Determinism* is the thesis that the state of the world at any time, together with the laws of nature, fixes a unique future. *Compatibilists* hold that freedom and responsibility are consistent with determinism; *incompatibilists* deny it, and divide into *libertarians* (we are free, so determinism is false) and *hard determinists* or, more broadly, *free will skeptics*.

### Hume's compatibilism

Hume thought the whole dispute "merely verbal." Everyone agrees that human actions follow regularly from motives, character and circumstances; that is why we can predict, trust and govern one another. And everyone agrees that we are sometimes free in the sense that matters:

> By liberty, then, we can only mean *a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will*; that is, if we choose to remain at rest, we may; if we choose to move, we also may. Now this hypothetical liberty is universally allowed to belong to every one who is not a prisoner and in chains.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, VIII.i, §73

Hume then turns the tables: far from threatening responsibility, necessity is required for it, because blame attaches to persons through their durable character:

> The actions themselves may be blameable; they may be contrary to all the rules of morality and religion: But the person is not answerable for them; and as they proceeded from nothing in him that is durable and constant, and leave nothing of that nature behind them, it is impossible he can, upon their account, become the object of punishment or vengeance.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, VIII.ii, §76

This is the *luck objection* to libertarianism in its first clear form: an uncaused choice seems to happen to me, like a random twitch. Contemporary libertarians (Robert Kane, *The Significance of Free Will*, 1996; Timothy O'Connor's agent-causal *Persons and Causes*, 2000) have worked hard to answer it, and it remains the main pressure on their position.

### James: the quagmire of evasion

William James found Hume's solution too cheap. In "The Dilemma of Determinism," an 1884 lecture to Harvard divinity students, he coined the labels still in use:

> Old-fashioned determinism was what we may call *hard* determinism. It did not shrink from such words as fatality, bondage of the will, necessitation, and the like. Nowadays, we have a *soft* determinism which abhors harsh words, and, repudiating fatality, necessity, and even predetermination, says that its real name is freedom...
>
> — William James, "The Dilemma of Determinism," in *The Will to Believe* (1897), p. 149

The compatibilist redefinition, he continues, is "a quagmire of evasion under which the real issue of fact has been entirely smothered." The real issue is whether the future contains genuine possibilities, and his argument for thinking it does is ethical. He takes a case from the newspapers, a murder in Brockton, Massachusetts:

> Hardly any one can remain *entirely* optimistic after reading the confession of the murderer at Brockton the other day: how, to get rid of the wife whose continued existence bored him, he inveigled her into a desert spot, shot her four times, and then, as she lay on the ground and said to him, "You didn't do it on purpose, did you, dear?" replied, "No, I didn't do it on purpose," as he raised a rock and smashed her skull.
>
> — William James, "The Dilemma of Determinism," in *The Will to Believe* (1897), pp. 160–161

We judge that something else should have happened; James calls these "judgments of regret." But if determinism is true, the murder was "called for by the rest of the universe." So either our regret is mistaken, or the universe is bad at its core. Determinism forces a choice between abandoning moral judgment and pessimism; a universe with real chance in it escapes the dilemma. James admits this is not a proof, and a compatibilist can reply that regret is the judgment that a different event *would have been better*, which is coherent even if it could not have happened. What James shows is that deterministic thinking can make moral attitudes feel weightless. P. F. Strawson would argue that this is a confusion.

### Two modern arguments for incompatibilism

**The Consequence Argument** (Peter van Inwagen, *An Essay on Free Will*, 1983; versions also in Carl Ginet and David Wiggins):

1. No one has any choice about the state of the world before they were born.
2. No one has any choice about the laws of nature.
3. If determinism is true, the state of the world before your birth and the laws together entail everything you do.
4. If you have no choice about *p*, and no choice about whether *p* entails *q*, then you have no choice about *q* (the "transfer principle").
5. So if determinism is true, no one has any choice about anything they do.

Most compatibilist replies attack premise 4, the transfer principle. David Lewis ("Are We Free to Break the Laws?", 1981) distinguished senses of "able to render a law false," and counterexamples by Thomas McKay and David Johnson (1996) showed the simplest form of the principle invalid. Reformulated versions remain the central case for incompatibilism.

**The Basic Argument** (Galen Strawson, "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility," *Philosophical Studies* 75, 1994) does not need determinism at all:

1. You do what you do because of the way you are (at least in mental respects).
2. To be truly responsible for what you do, you must be truly responsible for the way you are.
3. To be responsible for the way you are, you must have made yourself that way, and you must have done so in a way that was already shaped by the way you were.
4. This leads to a regress that cannot end; nobody can be *causa sui*, the cause of themselves.
5. So nobody is truly (ultimately) morally responsible.

The Basic Argument is Aristotle's tracing account with the gap made explicit. Its key word is "truly." Galen Strawson concedes that people are responsible in everyday senses; what he denies is the kind of responsibility that would make it fair, without qualification, for someone to suffer eternal punishment. Whether ordinary practices of blame require that kind of ultimate desert is precisely the question his father had already addressed.

## P. F. Strawson: the reactive attitudes

Peter Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962) changed the subject, and most work since has been written in its shadow. Strawson addressed two characters: the *pessimist*, who thinks that if determinism were true, blame and punishment would be unjustified, and the *optimist*, who thinks they would still be justified because of their social usefulness (deterrence, reform). Both, he argued, miss something. They talk about blame as if it were a policy that needs an external justification, and they forget what it actually is.

### What the reactive attitudes are

If someone treads on your hand deliberately, you resent it; if they tread on it accidentally while trying to help you, you do not, though the pain is the same. We care a great deal about the *quality of will* others show toward us. Our responses to it are the *reactive attitudes*: resentment, gratitude, hurt feelings, love, forgiveness; in their impersonal form, indignation and approbation; in their self-reactive form, guilt, shame and remorse. Responsibility, on this view, is not a metaphysical property to be found before blame is licensed. It is constituted by the practice of holding one another to a demand for reasonable regard.

### Excuses and exemptions

Strawson then sorts the pleas that lead us to modify reactive attitudes into two groups. *Excuses* ("He didn't mean to," "He didn't know," "He was pushed") show that this injury did not express ill will; the agent remains a full participant in moral relationships. *Exemptions* ("He wasn't himself," "He's only a child," "He's under hypnosis") invite us to suspend reactive attitudes toward the agent, not just the act. We take what Strawson calls the *objective attitude*, seeing the person as someone to be managed, treated, trained or avoided rather than resented or argued with.

### The argument

Now the question becomes: would the truth of determinism be a reason for taking the objective attitude toward everyone? Strawson's answer has three parts.

1. Determinism would not be an *excuse*. It does not show that anyone lacked ill will; the deliberate hand-treader still meant it.
2. Determinism would not be an *exemption* either. Exemptions apply to abnormal cases; they work by contrast with the normal adult. A general thesis about all events cannot show that everyone is abnormal.
3. Even if we asked whether to give up the reactive attitudes entirely, the question is idle. Our commitment to interpersonal relationships is too deep and too human to be overturned by a theoretical conviction. And if we could choose, we would judge the choice by what it gains and loses for human life, and a life of universal objectivity would be impoverished beyond recognition: no resentment, but also no love, gratitude or forgiveness as we know them.

The lesson is that justifications of blame belong *inside* the practice. We can criticize particular attitudes as unfair or excessive, extend exemptions, and argue about where the lines fall. What we cannot do is step outside the whole web of human attitudes and ask it to justify itself in terms of metaphysics.

### Objections and developments

**Is it a change of subject?** Skeptics reply that Strawson describes our practices but does not show they are *fitting*. If resentment presupposes basic desert and no one has it, resentment is systematically mistaken, however natural. Derk Pereboom (*Living Without Free Will*, 2001) argues that we could keep love, moral protest and a modified gratitude while dropping the desert-entailing attitudes.

**Robert Harris and the limits of evil.** Gary Watson's "Responsibility and the Limits of Evil" (1987) tested Strawson on a real case. In 1978 Robert Alton Harris murdered two teenage boys in San Diego, then calmly ate the hamburgers they had bought. The crime produces pure outrage. Then Watson presents Harris's childhood: born premature after his father kicked his pregnant mother, beaten by his father, rejected by his mother, brutalized in juvenile detention. Most readers find their outrage mixed with something else. That is a puzzle for Strawson, because the history changes nothing about Harris's quality of will at the time of the murder. Watson's suggestion is that the history makes us see that we might have become Harris, and that reactive attitudes presuppose a target who can be addressed as a fellow member of the moral community. Extreme evil can push someone outside that conversation.

**Two faces of responsibility.** Watson later separated *attributability* (an act reveals who you are and can be ascribed to you as your own) from *accountability* (you can fairly be held to account, with demands and sanctions) ("Two Faces of Responsibility," 1996). A psychopath's cruelty is attributable to him; whether he is accountable is doubtful. David Shoemaker (*Responsibility from the Margins*, 2015) added *answerability* and used the three to analyze cases at the edges: psychopathy, dementia, autism, depression.

The Strawsonian framework does not settle the free-will problem. It changes which questions look important (whom we can address, what we demand, how attitudes should respond to history and capacity), and those are questions empirical psychology can inform.

## Frankfurt: alternate possibilities and the structure of the will

Harry Frankfurt published two short papers that defined two research programs.

### Against the principle of alternate possibilities

The first targets an assumption shared by nearly everyone:

> This principle states that a person is morally responsible for what he has done only if he could have done otherwise.
>
> — Harry Frankfurt, "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility," *Journal of Philosophy* 66 (1969), p. 829

Call this PAP. If PAP is true and determinism rules out doing otherwise, determinism rules out responsibility. Frankfurt's counterexample is so famous that cases of its type are called *Frankfurt cases*. Black wants Jones to perform some action and can reliably predict Jones's decisions from some sign (in later versions, a neural signal). If Jones shows any sign of deciding otherwise, Black will intervene with a device in his brain and make him do it. As it happens, Jones decides on his own, for his own reasons, and Black never lifts a finger.

1. Jones acted for his own reasons, and Black's mechanism played no role in what happened.
2. So Jones is as responsible as he would have been if Black had not existed.
3. But Jones could not have done otherwise, because Black would have stopped him.
4. So a person can be morally responsible without being able to do otherwise; PAP is false.

What matters for responsibility, Frankfurt concludes, is the *actual sequence* that produces the action, not the existence of alternatives. A counterfactual intervener who never intervenes cannot reduce your responsibility.

**Replies.** The *flicker of freedom* strategy notes that Jones could still have shown the sign, but a twitch seems too thin to ground responsibility. A stronger reply is the *dilemma defense* (Robert Kane, 1985; David Widerker, 1995). Either the sign deterministically guarantees the decision or it does not. If it does, the case assumes determinism and begs the question against the libertarian. If it does not, Jones could still decide otherwise after the sign, and Black would be too late. Defenders have built more elaborate cases to escape the dilemma, such as Pereboom's "Tax Evasion" (2001) and the "buffer" cases of Alfred Mele and David Robb (1998), and many philosophers now accept that some version works.

**Semicompatibilism.** John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza (*Responsibility and Control*, 1998) concluded that responsibility requires *guidance control* (acting from your own, moderately reasons-responsive mechanism) rather than *regulative control* (the ability to choose among real alternatives). Since guidance control is compatible with determinism, responsibility is too, whether or not freedom to do otherwise is.

### Persons, wantons and the will you want

The second paper, "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971), asks what is distinctive about a person's will. Persons can have desires about their desires. Frankfurt distinguishes:

- *first-order desires*: desires to do or have something (to smoke, to work, to sleep);
- *second-order desires*: desires about first-order desires (to want to stop wanting to smoke);
- *second-order volitions*: second-order desires that a particular first-order desire be your *will*, the desire that actually moves you to act.

A creature with first-order desires but no second-order volitions is a *wanton*: it does not care which of its desires wins. Frankfurt contrasts two addicts. The *unwilling addict* hates his craving and experiences it as an alien force, but it moves him anyway. The *willing addict* would take the drug even if the addiction vanished. Freedom of the will is having the will you want, a match between your second-order volitions and the desire that moves you. The unwilling addict acts freely in Hume's sense but lacks a free will; the willing addict has both, though he could not do otherwise.

This hierarchical model explains well why compulsion and manipulation feel like loss of self. It faces three serious objections.

1. **The regress and authority problem.** Gary Watson ("Free Agency," 1975) asked what makes second-order desires authoritative. They are desires too, and endorsing them with third-order desires starts a regress. Watson located the self in its *values* (what one judges good) instead. Frankfurt answered with "identification" and "wholeheartedness": decisive commitment to a desire with no interest in changing it.

2. **The sane deep self.** Susan Wolf ("Sanity and the Metaphysics of Responsibility," 1987) imagined JoJo, favorite son of Jo the First, a sadistic dictator. Raised to admire his father, JoJo sends people to prison or death on a whim and endorses this at every level of reflection. On the hierarchical model his will is free; yet most people hesitate to blame him, because his values were installed by a world that never let him see what was wrong. Responsibility, Wolf concludes, requires a *sane* deep self, able to know and appreciate right and wrong. In *Freedom Within Reason* (1990) she defends an asymmetry: praise does not require that you could have done otherwise, but blame requires that you could have seen and done better.

3. **Manipulation.** Derk Pereboom's *four-case argument* (2001; refined in *Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life*, 2014) presses the point. Professor Plum kills Ms. White out of self-interest, satisfying every compatibilist condition (reasons-responsiveness, hierarchical endorsement, no compulsion).
   - Case 1: neuroscientists manipulate Plum moment by moment by radio, producing his reasoning process.
   - Case 2: they programmed him at the start of his life to reason this way.
   - Case 3: his community trained him from childhood, rigorously, so that he would reason this way.
   - Case 4: ordinary determinism produced him.

   In Case 1 he is clearly not responsible; no relevant difference separates adjacent cases; so he is not responsible in Case 4 either. Compatibilists take a *hard line* (Michael McKenna, 2008: once fully described, Plum is responsible even in Case 1) or a *soft line* that finds a difference in the agent's *history*, as Alfred Mele does with his cases of Beth and Ann (*Free Will and Luck*, 2006). Mele's own *zygote argument* shows how hard that line is to draw.

## Moral development: how morality grows

Philosophers of responsibility assume a normal adult with mature capacities. Developmental psychology asks how those capacities form. The question goes back to Darwin, who thought the moral sense arose from social instincts plus intellect, language and habit:

> The imperious word "ought" seems merely to imply the consciousness of the existence of a rule of conduct, however it may have originated. Formerly it must have been often vehemently urged that an insulted gentleman OUGHT to fight a duel. We even say that a pointer OUGHT to point, and a retriever to retrieve game.
>
> — Charles Darwin, *The Descent of Man* (2nd ed., 1874), ch. IV

### Piaget: from rules of the game to rules of cooperation

Jean Piaget's *The Moral Judgment of the Child* (1932) began with marbles. Piaget asked Swiss boys to teach him the game and found that young children treat the rules as sacred and unchangeable, handed down by adults, while older children (from about ten) see them as agreements that players can change by consent. He called the first stage *heteronomous morality* and the second *autonomous morality*, a morality of cooperation among equals.

His best-known probe compared two stories. John, called to dinner, opens a door and accidentally breaks fifteen cups; Henry, climbing for forbidden jam, breaks one. Younger children say John is naughtier; older children say Henry, because of his intention. Aristotle's distinction between the voluntary and the involuntary emerges in childhood, and later research showed that even three- and four-year-olds use intentions when they are made salient.

### Kohlberg's stages

Lawrence Kohlberg's 1958 Chicago dissertation extended Piaget into adolescence, following a group of boys for decades. His most famous dilemma: Heinz's wife is dying, and the druggist who invented a drug that might save her charges ten times its cost. Heinz can raise only half. Should he steal it? Kohlberg scored the *reasons*, not the verdict, in three levels of two stages each:

| Level | Stage | Orientation |
|---|---|---|
| Preconventional | 1 | Obedience and punishment: wrong is what gets punished. |
| | 2 | Instrumental exchange: you scratch my back, I scratch yours. |
| Conventional | 3 | Interpersonal conformity: being a good person in others' eyes. |
| | 4 | Law and social order: duty, rules, keeping the system going. |
| Postconventional | 5 | Social contract and individual rights: laws as agreements for mutual benefit, changeable when they fail. |
| | 6 | Universal ethical principles: justice, equal dignity, reversibility. |

The scheme is openly philosophical: Stage 6 looks like Kant and Rawls, and Kohlberg argued ("From Is to Ought," 1971) that higher stages are more adequate, not just later. Stage 6 almost never appeared in the data and was dropped from the scoring manual. Richard Shweder and others argued that the scheme builds liberal individualism into "development." And Carol Gilligan, Kohlberg's Harvard colleague, argued in *In a Different Voice* (1982) that it hears only a "justice" voice. Her eleven-year-old Jake solves the Heinz dilemma like a math problem; Amy looks for ways to keep the relationships intact (talk to the druggist, find a loan) and is scored lower for it. Later meta-analyses found little sex difference on Kohlberg's scale, but the "care" voice became the starting point of care ethics ([Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)).

### Domains and infants

Elliot Turiel and colleagues (*The Development of Social Knowledge*, 1983) found that children of three or four distinguish *moral* from *conventional* rules. Hitting would still be wrong if the teacher allowed it; talking without raising your hand would not. Moral violations are judged wrong independently of authority, everywhere, and because of harm, rights or fairness. Psychopaths, R. J. R. Blair found (1995), often fail to draw this distinction. Haidt, Koller and Dias (1993) complicated it: many people outside educated Western elites treat harmless but disgusting or disrespectful acts (eating the family dog after an accident) as authority-independent moral violations.

Infant research pushed the origins earlier. In a 2007 *Nature* study, J. Kiley Hamlin, Karen Wynn and Paul Bloom showed six- and ten-month-olds a puppet climbing a hill, helped by one character and hindered by another; most infants then reached for the helper. The finding has proved fragile: a large preregistered multi-lab replication (ManyBabies 4, reported in 2024–25) did not find the preference. Developmental moral psychology has been reshaped by the replication crisis, and its classic findings should be taught with their error bars.

## Intuition, emotion and reasoning

Hume claimed that reason by itself cannot move us to act:

> Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, 2.3.3

Hume's sentimentalism ([Chapter 8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md)) was a philosophical thesis about motivation and the source of moral distinctions. Since about 2000, experimental work has been read as vindicating something like it as a thesis about how moral judgments are produced.

### Haidt's social intuitionist model

Jonathan Haidt's 2001 paper "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail" argued against the rationalist tradition of Piaget and Kohlberg. On his *social intuitionist model*:

1. Moral judgments are usually caused by fast, automatic, affect-laden *intuitions*: a flash of approval or disapproval.
2. Moral *reasoning* usually comes afterward, and it works like a lawyer building a case for a verdict already reached, not like a judge weighing evidence.
3. Reasoning does change minds, but mainly *other people's*, by triggering new intuitions in them. Private reflection that overturns an initial intuition is possible but rare.

He later summed this up in a slogan:

> Intuitions come first, strategic reasoning second.
>
> — Jonathan Haidt, *The Righteous Mind* (Pantheon, 2012), Part I

The signature evidence is *moral dumbfounding*. Julie and Mark, adult siblings on vacation in France, decide to have sex once, with two forms of contraception. They enjoy it, never repeat it, keep it secret, and feel closer. Most participants say it was wrong, cite risks the story rules out, and when this is pointed out, keep their verdict anyway: "I can't explain it, I just know it's wrong." Other studies reported that inducing disgust (by hypnosis, a foul smell, a dirty desk) made moral judgments harsher.

Haidt and colleagues then developed *Moral Foundations Theory* (Graham, Haidt and Nosek, 2009): innate but culturally elaborated foundations of care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity and (later) liberty. American liberals, the surveys suggested, rely mainly on care and fairness; conservatives draw on all of them.

**Criticisms.**

- *Replication.* Several disgust-induction findings failed direct replication (Johnson, Cheung and Donnellan, 2014), and a meta-analysis (Landy and Goodwin, 2015) found at most a small effect of incidental disgust once publication bias is corrected for.
- *Dumbfounding is not irrationality.* Participants may be tracking reasons they cannot articulate (the risk-taking was itself reckless; norms against incest are valuable even when one case is harmless).
- *Reasoning does work.* Paxton, Ungar and Greene (2012) found that a strong argument *plus* time to reflect made people more willing to judge Julie and Mark's act permissible. Joshua May's *Regard for Reason in the Moral Mind* (2018) defends "optimistic rationalism": most moral judgment is inference, even when fast.
- *Foundations or biases?* Whether a widely felt intuition about sanctity is a *foundation* of morality or a bias it should correct is a normative question, which no survey can answer.

### Greene's dual-process theory

Joshua Greene came to psychology from philosophy. In a 2001 *Science* study, he and colleagues scanned brains during moral dilemmas. In the *switch* case, you can divert a runaway trolley from five people onto one; most say you may. In the *footbridge* case, you can stop it only by pushing a large man into its path; most say you may not. "Personal" dilemmas like the footbridge produced more activity in emotion-related regions; "impersonal" ones engaged areas linked to cognitive control.

Greene's *dual-process theory* holds that characteristically deontological judgments come from fast emotional alarms and characteristically consequentialist ones from slow, controlled reasoning (a camera's automatic settings versus its manual mode, in *Moral Tribes*, 2013). In "The Secret Joke of Kant's Soul" (2008) he concluded that deontology is largely rationalized emotion evolved for small-group life, to be trusted less on novel, large-scale problems.

**Criticisms.**

- *The normative premise does all the work.* Selim Berker ("The Normative Insignificance of Neuroscience," 2009) showed that every version of Greene's argument needs a premise like "judgments that respond to morally irrelevant factors (such as whether force is applied directly) are unreliable." But whether personal force is morally irrelevant is a first-order ethical question. Once you supply the normative premise, the brain data drop out. This is the same structure as the evolutionary debunking arguments discussed in [Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md): the empirical part tells you *why* you judge as you do; the philosophical part must tell you whether that cause undermines the judgment.
- *Is "utilitarian" the right label?* Guy Kahane, Jim Everett, Brian Earp and colleagues (2015) found that willingness to push correlates with psychopathic traits and not with impartial concern for the greater good, such as giving to distant strangers. Sacrificial dilemmas may measure willingness to cause harm more than utilitarian commitment.

Neuroscience is not irrelevant to ethics. It can reveal that a judgment is sensitive to factors you would reject on reflection; Eric Schwitzgebel and Fiery Cushman (2012) found professional philosophers' trolley judgments as susceptible to order effects as anyone's. What it cannot do by itself is tell you which factors are morally relevant.

### Sympathy and the impartial spectator

The sentimentalist tradition offered a richer picture than "emotion versus reason." Adam Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* opens with a claim about human nature:

> How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it, except the pleasure of seeing it.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, Part I, Section I, ch. 1

For Smith, moral judgment is sympathy (imaginatively entering another's situation) corrected by the standpoint of an *impartial spectator*, internalized as "the man within," "the inhabitant of the breast." Feeling is educated by imagination and social feedback, so that a mature moral sentiment is neither raw reflex nor calculation, a picture that both contemporary sentimentalists and critics of Haidt and Greene draw on.

## Character and situation

Virtue ethics (Chapters [5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md) and [13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)) assumes that people have robust character traits: an honest person tells the truth across a wide range of situations, and a compassionate person helps. In the 1990s, philosophers began to cite social psychology to argue that this assumption is false.

### The evidence

- *Hartshorne and May* (1928–30) tested thousands of schoolchildren for honesty in many settings; correlations across situations were low, around 0.2.
- *Milgram* (1963): 26 of 40 ordinary men continued to administer what they believed were dangerous electric shocks, up to the 450-volt maximum, when instructed by an experimenter in a gray coat.
- *Darley and Batson* (1973), "From Jerusalem to Jericho": Princeton seminarians on their way to give a talk (for some, on the parable of the Good Samaritan) passed a man slumped in a doorway. Of those told they had time to spare, 63 percent offered help; of those told they were late, 10 percent. The topic of the talk made no significant difference, and some hurried students stepped over the man.

Gilbert Harman ("Moral Philosophy Meets Social Psychology," 1999) and John Doris (*Lack of Character*, 2002) argued that we commit the *fundamental attribution error*, explaining behavior by character when situation does most of the work. Doris concluded that people have at most *local* traits, and that virtue ethics rests on a false empirical psychology.

### Replies

[Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md) gave the virtue ethicist's replies: virtue was always meant to be rare; Aristotelian virtues integrate perception, emotion and practical wisdom rather than emitting the same behavior everywhere (Kamtekar 2004; Annas 2011); and most people have robust but *mixed* traits that are neither virtues nor vices (Christian Miller, *Moral Character*, 2013). Two further points belong to moral psychology proper. First, the evidence is weaker than it looked in 2002: the dime study has failed to replicate; Gina Perry's study of Milgram's archives (*Behind the Shock Machine*, 2012) reports that the experimenter often departed from the script and that many participants doubted the shocks were real; and the personality psychologist Seymour Epstein (1979) showed that cross-situational correlations rise sharply when behavior is aggregated over many occasions, as everyday judgments of character implicitly do. Second, the practical upshot survives whichever side wins: if situations are powerful, ethical skill includes choosing and structuring them. Skip the dinner where you know you will be tempted; build friendships and institutions that support good conduct.

James had the practical side of this a century earlier. Character, he argued, is the residue of repeated action, laid down in the nervous system:

> Could the young but realize how soon they will become mere walking bundles of habits, they would give more heed to their conduct while in the plastic state. We are spinning our own fates, good or evil, and never to be undone. Every smallest stroke of virtue or of vice leaves its never so little scar.
>
> — William James, *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), vol. 1, ch. IV

His practical advice is Aristotelian: "*Never suffer an exception to occur till the new habit is securely rooted in your life*." Read with the situationist literature, this suggests that character is less a fixed possession than an ongoing project of habit-building, which includes managing the environments that trigger our habits.

## Moral luck, briefly

Two drivers run a red light with equal carelessness; a child steps in front of one of them. We blame the one who killed far more, though they differ only in luck. Adam Smith described this two centuries before Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel named it *moral luck* (1976):

> But how well soever we may seem to be persuaded of the truth of this equitable maxim, when we consider it after this manner, in abstract, yet when we come to particular cases, the actual consequences which happen to proceed from any action, have a very great effect upon our sentiments concerning its merit or demerit, and almost always either enhance or diminish our sense of both.
>
> — Adam Smith, *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, Part II, Section III, Introduction

Smith calls this an "irregularity of sentiment, which every body feels, which scarce any body is sufficiently aware of, and which no body is willing to acknowledge." He also noticed that someone who has *involuntarily* hurt another "seems to have some sense of his own ill desert" (II.iii.2), which is Williams's *agent-regret*. Nagel's four kinds of luck (resultant, circumstantial, constitutive and causal) connect it to this chapter: constitutive luck is Aristotle's gap and the Basic Argument; causal luck is the free will problem again. [Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md) takes up the responses.

## Experimental philosophy

In the early 2000s some philosophers began running their own studies. *Experimental philosophy* ("x-phi") tests the intuitions philosophical arguments rely on by asking non-philosophers about carefully varied cases.

### The Knobe effect

In Joshua Knobe's 2003 study, a chairman approves a profitable program, saying he doesn't care that it will harm the environment; it does. Did he harm the environment intentionally? About 82 percent said yes. In the matched version the program *helps* the environment, and he again doesn't care. Did he help it intentionally? About 23 percent said yes.

The asymmetry replicates widely; its interpretation is disputed. Knobe concludes that moral evaluation enters apparently descriptive concepts like *intentional* at a basic level; others see a blame-driven bias. Either way, if judgments of the voluntary are shaped by judgments of blame, Aristotle's order of explanation is not how the mind actually works.

### Folk intuitions about free will

Are ordinary people compatibilists? Shaun Nichols and Joshua Knobe (2007) found that it depends on the question. Asked abstractly whether people in a deterministic universe are fully morally responsible, most (86 percent) said no; asked about a man in that universe who burns down his house to kill his family, most (72 percent) said he *is* fully responsible. Nichols and Knobe suggested that affect drives the compatibilist verdict, perhaps as a performance error. Eddy Nahmias and Dylan Murray (2011) replied that many participants misread determinism as *bypassing* their beliefs and desires; correct the misreading and incompatibilist intuitions shrink.

### What experiments can and cannot show

The *negative program* of x-phi uses variation in intuitions (by culture, framing, order) to challenge armchair methods; the Schwitzgebel and Cushman results press against the reply that philosophers' intuitions are expert. The *positive program* uses experiments to understand the concepts at work, as Knobe's does.

Two cautions apply. First, x-phi was caught up in the replication crisis: an influential finding that intuitions about knowledge vary across cultures (Weinberg, Nichols and Stich, 2001) failed to replicate, and the XPhi Replicability Project (Cova and colleagues, 2021) replicated roughly 70 to 80 percent of 40 studies. Second, knowing what people *think* about responsibility does not tell you what it *is*. There is a nice irony in Schwitzgebel and Joshua Rust's finding that ethicists behave no better than other professors, and that ethics books go missing from libraries more often.

## Living without ultimate desert?

The two halves of the chapter meet in a practical question: if no one is ultimately responsible, or much of what we do is not up to us, what should happen to blame and punishment?

**Free will skeptics** (Pereboom; Gregg Caruso, *Rejecting Retributivism*, 2021) would give up *basic desert*, the idea that wrongdoers deserve to suffer simply because of what they did, while keeping forward-looking blame: moral protest, requests for apology, reconciliation and reform. Caruso's *public health–quarantine model* treats dangerous offenders like carriers of a disease: restrict them to protect others, minimally, with decent conditions and rehabilitation.

**Compatibilists** reply that the freedom worth wanting is the evolved capacity for self-control and reasons-responsiveness, which we have (Daniel Dennett, *Elbow Room*, 1984; *Just Deserts*, a debate with Caruso, 2021). Manuel Vargas (*Building Better Beings*, 2013) proposes *revisionism*: revise the concept, keep the practice, which is justified by its role in building agents who respond to moral reasons. Saul Smilansky (*Free Will and Illusion*, 2000) suggests that some illusion about desert may be morally necessary.

**Strawsonians** say the question is partly idle. We will not stop resenting; the work is making our reactive attitudes better informed about exemptions, circumstances and luck.

Darrow's argument was, in effect, a request for the objective attitude toward his clients. The judge's reply, that youth mattered and philosophy did not, was Strawsonian without knowing it: he extended a familiar exemption (immaturity) instead of accepting a general one (determinism).

## Living it

Try this for one week.

1. **Keep a reactive-attitude log.** Each time you feel resentment, indignation, guilt or gratitude, note it in a line: the person, the act, the attitude.
2. **Apply Strawson's sort.** For each episode of resentment, ask: is there an *excuse* (they didn't mean it, didn't know, had no decent alternative)? Is there a case for an *exemption* (they were not themselves, are a child, are ill)? If neither, your resentment may be fitting. Notice how often you resent before asking.
3. **Apply Smith's test for moral luck.** For each blame or self-blame episode, imagine that the outcome had been better or worse while the intention stayed the same. Would your attitude change? If it would, how much of your blame tracks the will, and how much tracks luck?
4. **Apply Haidt's test to yourself.** When you have made a quick moral judgment, write down your reason. Then ask whether the reason produced the judgment or was found to support it. Try Paxton, Ungar and Greene's method: sleep on a judgment you feel strongly about, after reading the best argument against it.
5. **Apply Frankfurt's test.** Pick one of your recurring failures (procrastination, a sharp tongue). Is the desire behind it one you endorse at the second order? If not, you are, in that domain, an unwilling addict. Then follow James and Doris: choose one habit, allow no exceptions for a month, and change the situations that trigger the old one.

At the end of the week, write a paragraph on what you learned. The aim is not to stop blaming but to blame more accurately: as Aristotle wanted, in the right way, at the right person, for the right thing.

## Summary

- Aristotle: the voluntary has its origin in an agent who knows the particulars; force and particular ignorance excuse; duress yields "mixed" acts; we answer for characters we formed, though this tracing account has a gap at childhood.
- Hume: liberty is acting as you will, and blame needs acts that flow from durable character. James: "soft determinism" is a quagmire of evasion; judgments of regret point to real possibilities.
- The Consequence Argument and Galen Strawson's Basic Argument are the main modern cases against responsibility.
- P. F. Strawson grounds responsibility in reactive attitudes to quality of will; determinism is neither an excuse nor an exemption. Watson's Robert Harris case and attributability/accountability refine the view.
- Frankfurt cases challenge the principle of alternate possibilities; semicompatibilism rests responsibility on guidance control. Frankfurt's hierarchical will faces Watson's authority problem, Wolf's JoJo and Pereboom's four-case argument.
- Piaget, Kohlberg, Gilligan and Turiel map moral development; infant findings have not survived large replications.
- Haidt and Greene make intuition and emotion central; replication failures, evidence that reasoning changes minds, and Berker's normative-premise point limit the conclusions.
- Situationism challenges robust traits; replies stress rare virtue, practical wisdom, mixed traits and the shaping of situations and habits.
- Experimental philosophy (the Knobe effect, folk judgments about determinism) can expose unreliable intuitions but cannot replace argument.
- Skeptics would replace retributive desert with forward-looking practices; compatibilists, revisionists and Strawsonians would reform blame instead.

### Dialogue 2 — On the will you want

**Frankfurt:** You say you are "not free" when you scroll on your phone at night instead of sleeping. But nobody forces you. You do what you want. In what sense are you unfree?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Frankfurt in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I do what I want in the moment, but I don't want to want it. Some part of me wishes the urge would just stop.

**Frankfurt:** So you have a first-order desire to scroll and a second-order desire not to be moved by it. Which of these is *you*?

**You:** The second, I think. The urge feels like it happens to me.

**Frankfurt:** Then you are, in this small domain, an unwilling addict. You act freely, in that you do what you want, but your will is not free, because it is not the will you want to have.

**Frankfurt:** Very well; call it valuing, or call it identification. What matters is that you are not neutral between your desires. You have taken a side.

**Wolf:** Let me press both of you. Suppose a young person raised in a cult has been taught that sleep is weakness and that the devout scroll scripture all night. She values it, she identifies with it, she has the will she wants. Is she free and responsible?

**Wolf:** And how do you know your own examination is not just another product of upbringing?
