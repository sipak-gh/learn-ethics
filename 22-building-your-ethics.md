# Chapter 22 — Building Your Ethics

This course began with a shepherd who found a ring that made him invisible, and with Glaucon's challenge: if you could do wrong without being caught, why would you do right? It also began with a method. Reflective equilibrium moves back and forth between principles and particular judgments, adjusting each in the light of the other, until they fit. Twenty-one chapters later you have met the main answers to Glaucon and the main materials the method works on: theories of right action and good character from four continents and twenty-five centuries; arguments about whether any of them can be true; findings about how moral minds actually work; and applied problems from abortion to artificial intelligence. This last chapter turns from studying ethics to doing it. It sets out what the theories share and where they part, what a personal ethical framework needs to contain, how to act when you are unsure which theory is right, and how to keep a framework alive once it is written. It ends with a reading path for those who want to continue at graduate level. Previous: [Chapter 21 — Technology and AI Ethics](21-technology-and-ai-ethics.md). Next: [Appendix A — Glossary](appendix-a-glossary.md).

## Looking back: the map

The course divided ethics into three levels ([Chapter 1](01-what-is-ethics.md)). *Normative ethics* asks what makes actions right and lives good. *Metaethics* asks what moral claims mean and whether any are true. *Applied ethics* takes particular problems. Between them sits *moral psychology*, which asks how moral minds actually work. The table below collects the normative theories. Each row compresses a chapter or more, and each is a lens, not a verdict.

| Theory | What matters most | Core test of an action | Classic objection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Divine command and natural law ([Ch. 4](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md)) | God's will; the goods of human nature | Does it accord with the law God commands or that reason finds in our nature? | Euthyphro's dilemma; whose God, whose nature? |
| Aristotelian virtue ethics ([Ch. 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md), [Ch. 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)) | Flourishing (*eudaimonia*), character, practical wisdom | Is it what a virtuous person would characteristically do? | Gives too little guidance; whose virtues? |
| Hellenistic ethics ([Ch. 6](06-hellenistic-ethics.md)) | Tranquility; virtue as the only good (Stoics); pleasure as absence of pain (Epicurus) | Does it express virtue and keep you free of disturbance? | Too detached from the goods and losses of ordinary life |
| Confucian, Buddhist and other traditions ([Ch. 7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md)) | Humaneness and ritual propriety; release from suffering; personhood through others | Does it cultivate *ren*, compassion, harmony? | Conservatism about roles; tension with individual rights |
| Sentimentalism ([Ch. 8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md)) | Sympathy and the approval of an impartial spectator | Would an informed, impartial observer approve? | Feelings vary; can sentiment be mistaken? |
| Kantian ethics ([Ch. 9](09-kant.md)) | Rational agency, dignity, autonomy | Can its maxim be universalized? Does it treat humanity as an end? | Rigid rules (the murderer at the door); empty formalism |
| Utilitarianism and consequentialism ([Ch. 10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md), [Ch. 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)) | Well-being, impartially counted | Does it produce the best outcome? | Demandingness, integrity, justice for individuals |
| Contractualism ([Ch. 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)) | Fair terms among equals | Could it be justified to each person affected? | Who is left out of the contract: animals, future people? |
| Care ethics ([Ch. 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)) | Relationships and responsiveness to need | Does it meet the needs of those in your care, and sustain the caring relation? | Parochialism; how to care for strangers |
| Nietzschean critique ([Ch. 15](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md)) | Self-overcoming; suspicion of moral motives | What does this value express, and does it enhance life? | Offers diagnosis more than guidance; elitism |
| Existentialist ethics ([Ch. 16](16-existentialist-ethics.md)) | Freedom, authenticity, responsibility | Do you own your choice, and will the freedom of others? | Seems to permit any authentically chosen project |

Behind every row stand the metaethical questions of [Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md). A realist reads each theory as an attempt to state moral truths. A constructivist reads it as an account of what rational agents would commit themselves to. An expressivist reads it as a systematic attitude. An error theorist, as [learn-nihilism, Chapter 12](../learn-nihilism/12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md) explains, reads it as a mistake. Beside them stand the findings of [Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md): moral judgment is often fast and intuitive, shaped by evolution and culture, and only sometimes corrected by reasoning. And under the applied chapters ([19](19-bioethics.md)–[21](21-technology-and-ai-ethics.md)) lie the same questions in practical form: who counts, what may be done to them, and who decides.

## Is there convergence?

On first meeting, the theories look like rival armies. On longer acquaintance, they share a great deal.

### Common ground

First, nearly all of them, the Nietzschean critique aside, demand some form of *impartiality* at some level: you may not count yourself as special simply because you are you. (Care ethics, Confucian graded love and much virtue ethics deny that impartiality is the whole of morality, as the list of residual disagreements below shows; but even they reject plain self-favoritism.) The utilitarian counts everyone's well-being equally; the Kantian universalizes; the contractualist seeks principles no one could reasonably reject; Smith's impartial spectator and Rawls's veil of ignorance strip away the information that would let you favor yourself; the Confucian Golden Rule (*shu*, "what you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others") is a reciprocity test; even the virtue ethicist counts justice among the virtues. Sidgwick took this to be one of the few self-evident principles of ethics:

> the good of any one individual is of no more importance, from the point of view (if I may say so) of the Universe, than the good of any other; unless, that is, there are special grounds for believing that more good is likely to be realised in the one case than in the other.
>
> — Henry Sidgwick, *The Methods of Ethics*, Book III, chapter XIII

Second, the theories agree about most cases. Gratuitous cruelty is wrong; promises generally bind; honesty, courage, gratitude and fairness are good; you should help others when it costs you little; torturing the innocent for fun is wrong on every theory anyone takes seriously. The disagreements are real, but they concern hard cases, edge cases and explanations, not the bulk of everyday moral life.

Third, sophisticated versions of the theories have moved toward each other. Rule consequentialists such as Brad Hooker justify something like common-sense duties by the good consequences of a code that includes them. Kantians such as Barbara Herman and Christine Korsgaard give a large place to the duty of beneficence and to judgment in applying it. Neo-Aristotelians such as Rosalind Hursthouse give virtue ethics principles of the form *do what is honest* and *do not do what is cruel*. R. M. Hare argued that Kantian universalizability, thought through, yields a form of utilitarianism at the critical level, and that at the intuitive level everyone should follow well-tested moral rules.

### Parfit's mountain

Derek Parfit went furthest. In *On What Matters* (2011), he argued that the best version of Kantian ethics, the best version of Scanlon's contractualism and the best version of rule consequentialism converge, if each is revised in the way he thought its own logic required, on a single principle: everyone ought to follow the principles whose universal acceptance would make things go best, which are also the only principles everyone could rationally will, and which no one could reasonably reject. He called this the *Triple Theory*. The three traditions, he wrote, are "climbing the same mountain on different sides." Parfit thought that if the major theories disagreed deeply, that would be evidence that no one had found moral truth, and that their convergence was evidence that there is something to be found.

Critics doubted the convergence. Kantians objected that Parfit had reshaped Kant's formula of universal law until it became consequentialist. Scanlon denied that his contractualism collapses into rule consequentialism, since it does not allow benefits to many to be added up against a serious burden to one. Others pointed out that virtue ethics, care ethics and the non-Western traditions are absent from Parfit's mountain. Still, his project changed the question. It is no longer only "which theory is right?" but also "what do the best theories agree on, and where exactly do they part?"

### The residue: where theories really disagree

Some disagreements survive every refinement. A personal framework has to take a position on each, or at least know that it has not.

1. **Aggregation.** May a large number of small benefits outweigh a grave harm to one person? Consequentialists say yes. Scanlon and many deontologists say no: you may not let one person die to spare millions from a mild headache ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md)).
2. **Constraints.** Are there things you may not do even to prevent more of the same thing? May you kill one to prevent five killings? Deontologists say no; consequentialists find the refusal puzzling ([Chapter 2](02-moral-reasoning.md), [Chapter 9](09-kant.md)).
3. **Partiality.** How much may you favor your family, friends, community and self? Care ethics and Confucianism make special relationships central; impartialist theories allow them only as means or within limits ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md), [Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)).
4. **Demandingness.** How much must you sacrifice for strangers? Singer's drowning child suggests a great deal. Most people's practice suggests very little ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)).
5. **The circle.** Who counts, and how much: animals, ecosystems, future people, possible artificial minds ([Chapters 20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md)–[21](21-technology-and-ai-ethics.md))?
6. **Self and others.** Is it ever rational to sacrifice your own good for others' greater good? Sidgwick called the conflict between egoism and utilitarianism the *dualism of practical reason*, and he could not resolve it. If self-interest and duty could not be shown to coincide, he concluded, there would be an "ultimate and fundamental contradiction in our apparent intuitions of what is Reasonable in conduct." In real conflicts, he wrote, practical reason would be "divided against itself."

Sidgwick ended his great book on that note of failure, and it is a fitting place for a student to start. A framework that pretends to have dissolved every one of these conflicts is probably hiding something.

### Williams's warning

Bernard Williams argued in *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* (1985) that the ambition of building an ethical theory, a systematic account that would tell us what to do and why, is misguided. Ethical life consists largely of *thick* concepts, such as cruel, brave, generous, treacherous and kind, which combine description and evaluation and are learned within a way of life. Theory tends to replace them with thin ones, such as good, right and ought, and in doing so loses knowledge. Reflection, Williams said, can destroy ethical knowledge as well as produce it. His example elsewhere ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)) was the man who, seeing his wife and a stranger drowning, consults a principle before saving his wife. He has "one thought too many."

Williams's point is not that you should stop thinking. It is that the aim is *confidence*, a practical security in living by your values, rather than *certainty* derived from a theory. You can build a framework in that spirit. Use theories as instruments for testing and organizing your judgments, not as machines that generate verdicts.

## The method, again

### Reflective equilibrium

Rawls named the method that most moral philosophers use, but Aristotle had used it first ([Chapter 1](01-what-is-ethics.md)). Begin with your *considered judgments*: moral beliefs you hold with confidence, formed when you are calm, informed and not personally at stake. Look for principles that explain them. Where a principle and a judgment conflict, revise one or the other, whichever you have less reason to trust. Then widen the circle. *Wide* reflective equilibrium also brings in background theories, such as theories of the person, of rationality, of human nature and of society, and the rival moral theories, so that your principles are tested against the strongest alternatives, not just against your own intuitions.

The method has critics. Some argue that it gives too much weight to intuitions, which [Chapter 18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md) showed are subject to framing effects, evolutionary distortion and cultural bias. If you start with prejudice, coherence will only organize it. Others argue that no better method exists. Every argument starts somewhere, and the alternative to testing principles against judgments is accepting principles without testing them. The fair conclusion is that the method is only as good as the inputs and the honesty of the person using it. That is why the wide version matters: it forces your intuitions to face the best debunking arguments and the best rival views.

### James: no ethics made up in advance

William James gave the method a democratic and historical twist. In "The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life" (1891) he argued that ethics is like science: no one can write it down once and for all, because it depends on experience that has not yet happened:

> The main purpose of this paper is to show that there is no such thing possible as an ethical philosophy dogmatically made up in advance. We all help to determine the content of ethical philosophy so far as we contribute to the race's moral life. In other words, there can be no final truth in ethics any more than in physics, until the last man has had his experience and said his say.
>
> — William James, "The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life," in *The Will to Believe*

James proposed that since every demand anyone makes is, by that fact, a claim to some good, the guiding principle should be to satisfy "as many demands as we can," and to favor the ideals that "prevail at the least cost," with the fewest other ideals destroyed. He meant this not as a formula but as an attitude: listen to the "cries of the wounded," and be ready to find that the established order has been crushing claims no one had noticed. Moral progress, on this view, often comes from people who were excluded insisting that their demands count, as the history of the expanding circle in [Chapter 20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md) illustrates. A personal ethics built in James's spirit is not a closed system but an experimental one.

### Middle-level principles

You do not need to settle the foundations before acting. Mill, a monist about value if anyone was, insisted on this:

> Whatever we adopt as the fundamental principle of morality, we require subordinate principles to apply it by: the impossibility of doing without them, being common to all systems, can afford no argument against any one in particular
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *Utilitarianism*, chapter II

Bioethics showed how far middle-level principles can go ([Chapter 19](19-bioethics.md)). Beauchamp and Childress's four principles, respect for autonomy, nonmaleficence, beneficence and justice, can be accepted by people who disagree about whether their ultimate ground is utility, rational agency, God or human flourishing. W. D. Ross's list of *prima facie* duties, namely fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement and non-maleficence, works the same way at the level of personal life. Each duty is binding unless outweighed, and no rule ranks them in advance; judgment decides in the case. Most people's working ethics is something like Ross's pluralism, whether they know it or not. The question is whether you can make yours explicit, consistent and answerable to criticism.

## What a framework contains

A personal ethical framework is not a theory in the textbook sense. It is a structured statement of what you value, what you will and will not do, whom you count, what kind of person you are trying to become, and how you will decide when these conflict. Seven components are worth working through.

### 1. What matters

List the things you take to be valuable for their own sake: well-being (and what you think it consists in, whether pleasure, desire satisfaction or objective goods such as knowledge, friendship and achievement, as in [Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)); the dignity and autonomy of persons; fairness; relationships; beauty; the flourishing of non-human life. Are these many values or expressions of one? A monist must explain why the others seem to matter independently. A pluralist must explain how to weigh them.

### 2. Constraints and options

Decide whether you accept *constraints*, things you will not do even for good ends, and what they are. Common candidates are killing or seriously harming the innocent, lying, breaking promises, coercing and manipulating ([Chapter 21](21-technology-and-ai-ethics.md)). Decide also whether you accept *options*: permissions to pursue your own projects even when you could do more good otherwise, which Samuel Scheffler called an *agent-centered prerogative*. A framework with no options is the moral saint's, and Susan Wolf argued in "Moral Saints" (1982) that such a life would be unattractive and perhaps not ideal at all: a person who devoted every moment to maximizing good would have no room for the cello, the novel, the joke or the friendship pursued for its own sake. A framework with no constraints will sometimes license atrocity.

### 3. Beneficence

Decide what you owe to strangers, and turn it into a practice. The drowning-child argument ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)) leaves room for many answers, from Singer's demanding conclusion to a modest fixed commitment, but it leaves little room for no answer at all. A concrete figure, such as a percentage of income, a number of hours, or a cause, is better than a good intention, because it survives a busy month.

### 4. The circle

State whom you count and on what basis: all humans equally? Sentient animals, and with what weight? Future generations? Possible artificial minds ([Chapters 20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md)–[21](21-technology-and-ai-ethics.md))? Then check your practices, what you eat, buy, vote for and build, against the answer. This is where most people find the largest gap between their stated principles and their lives.

### 5. Character

Choose the virtues you most need to cultivate. Aristotle's account of how ([Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md)) remains the most practical:

> men come to be builders, for instance, by building; harp-players, by playing on the harp: exactly so, by doing just actions we come to be just; by doing the actions of self-mastery we come to be perfected in self-mastery; and by doing brave actions brave.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book II, chapter I, tr. D. P. Chase

Character is built by practice, not by resolution. Benjamin Franklin learned this the hard way. In his twenties he set out to become morally perfect:

> It was about this time I conceiv'd the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection. I wish'd to live without committing any fault at any time; I would conquer all that either natural inclination, custom, or company might lead me into.
>
> — Benjamin Franklin, *Autobiography*

He soon found that "while my care was employ'd in guarding against one fault, I was often surprised by another; habit took the advantage of inattention." So he chose thirteen virtues, worked on one per week, and kept a little book with a grid in which he marked each day's lapses. He reported that he was "surpris'd to find myself so much fuller of faults than I had imagined," but saw them diminish. He never reached perfection and concluded, with characteristic irony, that a perfect character might be envied and hated. The method still works. It is the Aristotelian idea of habituation, made into a spreadsheet.

### 6. Decision procedures

A framework needs ways to decide in real time, which is different from a criterion of what makes acts right. Peter Railton's distinction ([Chapter 11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)) between a *criterion of rightness* and a *decision procedure* applies to every theory: a consequentialist may do best by not calculating, and a Kantian may need rules of thumb. Useful procedures drawn from the course include:

- **The publicity test.** Would you be comfortable if your reasons were published? It catches self-serving rationalizations ([Chapter 9](09-kant.md)).
- **The impartial spectator.** What would a well-informed, impartial observer feel about what you are doing? Smith's device works especially well in anger and self-pity ([Chapter 8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md)).
- **The veil.** Which rule would you choose if you did not know which person you would be ([Chapter 12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md))?
- **The friend test.** What would you advise a friend in your situation? It corrects for the biases of the first person.
- **The role test.** Whom do you owe things to in virtue of your roles as parent, teacher, doctor or citizen, and what do those roles require ([Chapter 7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md), [Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md))?
- **The theory panel.** Ask what a consequentialist, a Kantian, a virtue ethicist and a care ethicist would say. If they agree, act. If they disagree, you have found the morally interesting feature of the case.

### 7. Stance and uncertainty

Finally, decide how you hold the whole thing. Your metaethical view ([Chapter 17](17-metaethics.md)) affects how you understand disagreement and revision. A realist treats revision as discovery, a constructivist as better reasoning from commitments, and an expressivist as improving one's attitudes by one's own standards. It affects first-order ethics less than people fear. Quasi-realists and realists alike can say that cruelty is wrong, and would be wrong even if everyone approved of it. The nihilism course's capstone, [learn-nihilism, Chapter 15 — Beyond Nihilism](../learn-nihilism/15-beyond-nihilism.md), takes up what remains if no metaethical vindication is available, and argues that values can be built and lived even then.

What most people underrate is not metaethical but normative uncertainty. You may be confident that suffering matters and much less confident about whether killing is worse than letting die, or whether animals' interests count equally. The next section is about acting under that uncertainty.

## Acting under moral uncertainty

Suppose you think there is a 70 percent chance that eating factory-farmed meat is morally permissible and a 30 percent chance that it is seriously wrong. What should you do? The question is not settled by asking what the right theory says, because you do not know which theory is right. Philosophers working on *moral uncertainty* have developed several answers.

- **My favorite theory.** Act on the theory you think most likely to be true. This is simple but ignores stakes. If your favored view says an act is slightly better and your second view says it is a grave wrong, following the favorite seems reckless.
- **Maximize expected choiceworthiness.** William MacAskill, Krister Bykvist and Toby Ord (*Moral Uncertainty*, 2020) proposed treating moral uncertainty like empirical uncertainty: weight how good or bad each option is on each theory by your credence in that theory, and choose the option with the best expected value. On this approach, the 30 percent chance that factory farming is seriously wrong may outweigh the 70 percent chance that eating meat is mildly better, because the stakes are lopsided. Arguments of this shape also appear in debates about abortion, animals and existential risk.
- **The problem of comparison.** The approach requires comparing values across theories. How much worse is a rights violation, on a Kantian view, than a loss of utility, on a utilitarian one? The theories may use incommensurable scales. MacAskill and his co-authors propose ways of normalizing them; critics doubt that any is principled.
- **The moral parliament.** Nick Bostrom and Toby Ord suggested imagining your credences as seats in a parliament, with each theory represented in proportion to your confidence in it, and letting the delegates bargain. Minority views then get their way on the issues they care most about.
- **Robustness.** A simpler heuristic: prefer actions that are acceptable on every theory you take seriously, and take special care with actions that some credible view says are gravely wrong and none says are required. Many of the course's practical recommendations, such as giving some money effectively, reducing animal suffering, telling the truth and keeping promises, are robust in this sense.

A different school, represented by Brian Weatherson and Elizabeth Harman, argues that moral uncertainty should not be handled this way at all. What matters is doing what is actually right, not hedging among theories, and a person who cares about doing right as such, rather than about the things that make acts right, such as other people's suffering, displays a kind of moral fetishism. The debate remains open. What is not open is that every reflective person faces the problem, and that confidence in one's framework should be proportioned to the arguments for it.

## Living with your framework

### Integrity without rigidity

A written framework is a tool, not a cage. It is useful because it makes your commitments explicit so you can notice when you depart from them, explain yourself to others, and revise deliberately rather than by drift. It becomes harmful when it lets you stop noticing particular people and particular situations. Iris Murdoch, whom [Chapter 13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md) met, argued that most of moral life happens before choice, in how we see. A framework should sharpen your attention, not replace it.

Revision is part of the design. James's point holds for individuals as well as societies: you will meet cases your framework did not anticipate and people whose claims it ignored. The test of a good framework is not that it never changes but that it changes for reasons you can state.

### Practice

The Hellenistic schools ([Chapter 6](06-hellenistic-ethics.md)) treated ethics as something you practice daily, like an athlete's training, and the Stoic course in this series ([learn-stoicism, Chapter 16 — Living a Stoic Life](../learn-stoicism/16-living-a-stoic-life.md)) sets out their exercises: the morning preview of the day's difficulties, the evening review, the reminder of what is and is not in your control. Marcus Aurelius put the point in a sentence:

> Make it not any longer a matter of dispute or discourse, what are the signs and proprieties of a good man, but really and actually to be such.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, Book X, tr. Meric Casaubon

The Confucian tradition ([Chapter 7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md)) adds that cultivation takes a lifetime. Confucius described his own development decade by decade, ending with a state in which desire and rightness no longer conflict:

> At seventy, I could follow what my heart desired, without transgressing what was right.
>
> — Confucius, *The Analects*, Book II, chapter 4, tr. James Legge

That is the end point Aristotle also described, in which the virtuous person takes pleasure in acting well. Most of us are somewhere between Franklin's grid and Confucius at seventy.

### Community

No one builds an ethics alone. Moral views are tested in conversation, and Socrates' refusal to stop questioning, even at the price of his life, rested on the claim that "the unexamined life is not worth living" ([Chapter 5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md)). The examination he meant was done in public, with others. Find people who disagree with you and argue well: a reading group, a colleague in another field, a friend from a different tradition. Care ethics and the African ethics of *ubuntu* ([Chapter 7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md), [Chapter 14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)) add that the self who reasons about ethics is already formed by relationships, and that a framework which describes an isolated chooser has misdescribed its author.

The philosophy course's capstone, [learn-philosophy, Chapter 24 — Building a Philosophy of Life](../learn-philosophy/24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md), widens the frame from ethics to a whole view of life, including time, death, knowledge and meaning, and is the natural companion to this chapter.

## A sample framework

To make the exercise concrete, here is the outline of a framework a student might write after this course. It is one example among many and not a recommendation. Notice how it states commitments, marks uncertainties and names its sources.

- **Values.** Well-being, understood as a mix of happiness, relationships, knowledge and achievement; the equal dignity of persons; fairness; the welfare of sentient animals. Pluralist: these do not reduce to one value.
- **Constraints.** Do not deceive or manipulate people for advantage; keep promises unless keeping them would cause serious harm; never treat a person merely as a tool. These hold even when breaking them would produce slightly better results. Under extreme stakes, uncertain.
- **Options.** Personal projects, such as music and climbing, and special relationships may take priority over impartial good, within limits.
- **Beneficence.** Give 5 percent of income to effective charities, rising with income; volunteer two hours a month locally.
- **Circle.** All humans equally; sentient animals with substantial but not equal weight. In practice: no factory-farmed meat; reduce other animal products. Future people count; support climate policy.
- **Character.** Working on honesty in small things, patience, and courage to speak up. Method: Franklin's grid, one virtue at a time, reviewed weekly.
- **Procedures.** Publicity test for any act that causes unease; theory panel for hard cases; friend test when angry.
- **Uncertainty.** Credence split mainly between rule consequentialism and Scanlonian contractualism; prefer robust options; take seriously any credible view on which an act is gravely wrong.
- **Review.** Reread every New Year; record what changed and why.

The framework has gaps. It says nothing yet about politics, work, or end-of-life decisions, and its numbers are arbitrary. But it can be acted on, criticized and revised, which is more than most people's ethics can claim.

## Writing your own

Set aside a few hours and write answers to the following questions. Aim for one or two pages; brevity forces choices.

1. Which three moral judgments are you most confident of? What principle, if any, explains all three?
2. Which theory in this course did you find most persuasive? State the strongest objection to it and your answer.
3. Which theory did you find least persuasive? What does it get right that yours misses?
4. What do you value for its own sake? Is it one thing or many?
5. What will you not do, even for good ends?
6. What do you owe strangers? Translate the answer into a practice.
7. Whom do you count, and how does your daily life reflect it?
8. Which virtue do you most need, and how will you practice it?
9. How do you decide in hard cases? Name your procedures.
10. Where are you most uncertain, and how will you act given that uncertainty?
11. What would change your mind?
12. When will you review this, and with whom?

Then do the hardest part: show it to someone who disagrees with you, and listen.

## Living it

1. **Write the framework.** Answer the twelve questions above. Keep the result to two pages.
2. **Test it on a hard case.** Choose a decision you face this month, such as a job, a purchase, a conflict or a vote. Run it through your framework and the theory panel. Record whether the framework helped, and where it was silent.
3. **Start a Franklin grid.** Pick one virtue. For four weeks, mark each day whether you practiced it or lapsed. Review weekly.
4. **Find an interlocutor.** Share your framework with one person who disagrees with you on something important. Ask for the strongest objection. Revise or answer it in writing.
5. **Schedule the review.** Put a date in your calendar a year from now to reread the framework and the record of your hard cases. Note what changed and why.

## Summary

- The course's normative theories share more than they appear to: impartiality in some form, agreement on most cases, and refined versions that have moved toward each other (Hooker, Herman, Hursthouse, Hare). Parfit argued that Kantian, contractualist and rule-consequentialist ethics converge on a Triple Theory, "climbing the same mountain on different sides"; critics doubt it.
- Real disagreements remain over aggregation, constraints, partiality, demandingness, the moral circle, and Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason. A framework should take a position on each or admit that it has not.
- Williams warned that ethical theory can destroy knowledge carried by thick concepts and that the aim is confidence, not certainty. Theories are best used as instruments for testing judgments.
- Wide reflective equilibrium remains the working method, strengthened by facing debunking arguments and rival theories. James argued that ethics is never finished and must listen to the claims of the excluded. Mill and Ross show that middle-level principles let people act without settling foundations.
- A framework has seven components: what matters; constraints and options; beneficence; the circle; character (Aristotle's habituation, Franklin's grid); decision procedures (publicity, impartial spectator, veil, friend test, roles, theory panel); and metaethical stance and uncertainty.
- Under moral uncertainty, options include following your favorite theory, maximizing expected choiceworthiness (MacAskill, Bykvist and Ord), a moral parliament, and preferring robust actions. Critics (Weatherson, Harman) question the project itself.
- Frameworks live through practice, revision for stated reasons, and conversation. The companion capstones on Stoic practice, life after nihilism and a philosophy of life extend the same project.

### Dialogue 1 — The ring, again

**Glaucon:** You have studied ethics for twenty-one chapters. Now I hand you Gyges' ring. While you wear it, no one will ever know what you do. Do you put it on, and what do you do with it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Glaucon in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I'd put it on. I'd probably use it for some good, like exposing someone who's abusing people and getting away with it.

**Glaucon:** A just use. But think of the rest of the day. No one sees you take the larger slice, read a private letter, skip the payment you owe. Why not?

**You:** Because those things would still be wrong. Being seen isn't what makes them wrong.

**Glaucon:** That is what you say now, with no ring on your finger. When I first asked, in your first chapter, many people said the same, and many confessed they would not trust themselves. Has anything changed?

**You:** I think I understand better why I wouldn't. Every theory I studied gives a reason that doesn't depend on being seen. The Kantian says I'd be making an exception of myself. The utilitarian says the harm is the same. The contractualist says I couldn't justify it to the person I wronged.

**Glaucon:** Those are reasons why the act is wrong. I asked why you would not do it. Reasons do not move hands.

**You:** Fair. Then the answer is Aristotle's. I'm trying to become someone who doesn't want to. If I use the ring to cheat, I'm practicing cheating. In ten years I'd be that person.

**Glaucon:** And if you were certain of getting away with it forever, and the habit would not spread?

**You:** Then it's the virtue ethicist's point about who I'd be, and Plato's point that the unjust soul is at war with itself. I can't prove it to you. But I've watched how small dishonesties change the way I see people. I'd rather not see the world that way.

**Glaucon:** You no longer claim to refute me, then.

**You:** No. Sidgwick couldn't fully refute you either. But I can say what my framework is, why I hold it, and what I'd lose by giving it up. That's more than I could say at the start.

### Dialogue 2 — The dualism

**Henry Sidgwick:** You have been offered two jobs. One pays twice as much, in a city you love, doing work you enjoy. The other, at a charity, would let you prevent far more suffering than the money you could donate from the first. Your framework says impartial good matters. It also says you may pursue your own projects. Which does it tell you to take?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Sidgwick in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It doesn't settle it. That's the honest answer. Both parts of my framework apply, and they point in different directions.

**Henry Sidgwick:** That is my dualism of practical reason, which I could not resolve in a book of five hundred pages. Do not feel too bad. But you must still choose. How?

**You:** First, I'd check the facts. Would I really do more good at the charity? Some charity jobs could easily be filled by someone else, so my counterfactual impact might be small.

**Henry Sidgwick:** Suppose the facts are as stated: the difference is large.

**You:** Then I'd ask how large the sacrifice is. If it's twice as much money but the charity pays enough to live well, the sacrifice is mostly comfort and a city. That's real, but it's not my whole life.

**Henry Sidgwick:** You are weighing. On what scale?

**You:** My framework's options aren't unlimited. I said personal projects can take priority "within limits." A large difference in suffering prevented, against a moderate cost to me, seems to fall outside them.

**Henry Sidgwick:** And if you would be miserable at the charity, and do the work badly?

**You:** Then the good I'd do shrinks, and Wolf's warning applies. A life that is all duty may not even be the best life to want. So I'd want to know myself well enough to predict that.

**Henry Sidgwick:** So you resolve the dualism case by case, by judgment.

**You:** Yes. I don't think there's a formula. But I can use the uncertainty section of my framework: if one option is acceptable on all the views I take seriously and the other is gravely wrong on some, choose the first. Here neither is gravely wrong, so it really is my call. I'll take the charity job for two years and review.

**Henry Sidgwick:** A trial period. That is very English of you.

**You:** It's very James, actually. Ethics as an experiment.

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — The unfinished system

**William James:** You have written your framework, two pages, carefully argued. A friend reads it and says: "This is just your upbringing with footnotes. If you'd been raised elsewhere, you'd have written something else, and you'd find it just as convincing." What do you say?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer James in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** That's partly true. I can't step outside my upbringing to check. But that's true of every belief I have, including my scientific ones.

**William James:** Then you take the friend's point as a general skepticism, which proves too much. But suppose it is more specific. Suppose your friend points to one line, about animals perhaps, or about how much you owe strangers, and says, "You believe this because it is convenient."

**You:** Then I should take that seriously. Chapter 18 showed that people are very good at reasoning toward conclusions they already want. The question is whether I'd still hold the line if it were inconvenient.

**William James:** How would you find out?

**You:** Look for the strongest argument against it, from someone who doesn't share my interests. Ask what I'd think if I were in the position of the people it affects most. And notice whether I'm getting angry, because that's usually a sign.

**William James:** I said there can be no final truth in ethics until the last man has had his say. Your friend is one of the men, and so are the people your framework affects. Do you hear them?

**You:** Some of them. Not the animals, not future people, not people far away. I'll have to listen through other people who speak for them, and through argument.

**William James:** And if after all that you still disagree with your friend?

**You:** Then we disagree, and I keep the framework, marked as uncertain. That's not failure. It's what it means for ethics to be unfinished. I'd rather have a framework I can revise than none, or one I can't.

**William James:** Then you have understood me. Go and live it, and report back.

</details>
