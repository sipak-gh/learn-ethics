# Appendix A — Glossary

Key terms of the course in alphabetical order, with the original-language term where one matters and the chapter where the term is treated most fully. Use it to check a word you have forgotten, or as a review list: if you can explain each entry in two sentences and say who would disagree, you have the course's vocabulary. Previous: [Chapter 22 — Building Your Ethics](22-building-your-ethics.md). Next: [Appendix B — Timeline](appendix-b-timeline.md).

| Term | Original | Meaning | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Act consequentialism** | — | The view that an act is right if and only if its consequences are at least as good as those of any alternative open to the agent. Contrasted with rule consequentialism. | [10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md), [11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md) |
| **Akrasia** | Greek *akrasia*, "lack of mastery" | Weakness of will: knowingly acting against one's better judgment. Socrates denied it was possible; Aristotle explained it as knowledge that is held but not active. | [5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md) |
| **Anguish** | French *angoisse* | In Sartre, the awareness that in choosing you choose for all humanity and that nothing outside your freedom guarantees your choice. | [16](16-existentialist-ethics.md) |
| **Apatheia / ataraxia** | Greek, "freedom from passion" / "freedom from disturbance" | The Stoic ideal of a mind free of passions (false judgments of value); the Epicurean and Skeptic goal of tranquillity. | [6](06-hellenistic-ethics.md) |
| **Applied ethics** | — | The study of particular moral questions (abortion, animals, AI), as distinct from normative theory and metaethics. | [1](01-what-is-ethics.md), [19](19-bioethics.md) |
| **Authenticity** | French *authenticité* | Lucid recognition of one's own freedom and responsibility, the existentialist's one undeniable value; the opposite of bad faith. | [16](16-existentialist-ethics.md) |
| **Autonomy** | Greek *autos* + *nomos*, "self-law" | For Kant, the will's giving the moral law to itself; in bioethics, a patient's right to make informed decisions about her own care. | [9](09-kant.md), [19](19-bioethics.md) |
| **Bad faith** | French *mauvaise foi* | Sartre's term for lying to oneself about one's freedom, by treating oneself as a fixed thing ("that's just who I am") or as pure, unsituated freedom. | [16](16-existentialist-ethics.md) |
| **Care ethics** | — | The view, from Gilligan, Noddings, Held, Kittay and Tronto, that caring relations and responsiveness to particular others are central to morality, not a supplement to justice. | [14](14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md) |
| **Categorical imperative** | German *kategorischer Imperativ* | Kant's supreme principle of morality: a command that binds unconditionally, whatever you want. Its formulas include universal law, humanity as an end in itself, and the kingdom of ends. | [9](09-kant.md) |
| **Compatibilism** | — | The view that free will and moral responsibility are compatible with determinism. Defended in different ways by Hume, P. F. Strawson and Frankfurt. | [18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md) |
| **Considered judgment** | — | A moral judgment made calmly, with relevant information and without obvious bias: the "data" that reflective equilibrium starts from. | [1](01-what-is-ethics.md) |
| **Constructivism** | — | The metaethical view that moral truths are the output of an idealized procedure or standpoint (Rawls, Korsgaard, Street), not independent facts nor mere attitudes. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Contractarianism** | — | The Hobbesian strand of social-contract theory: morality is the set of rules that rational, self-interested people would agree to for mutual advantage (Hobbes, Gauthier). | [12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md) |
| **Contractualism** | — | The Kantian strand: morality is the set of principles that free and equal persons could justify to one another (Rousseau, Rawls, Scanlon). Scanlon: an act is wrong if any principle that permitted it could reasonably be rejected. | [12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md) |
| **Cultural relativism** | — | The view that moral claims are true or false only relative to a culture's code. Distinguished from the descriptive fact of cultural diversity. | [3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md) |
| **Demandingness objection** | — | The charge that consequentialism requires too much: that if you must always do the most good, almost every ordinary expenditure is wrong. | [11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md) |
| **Deontology** | Greek *deon*, "duty" | Any ethics that holds that some acts are required or forbidden independently of their consequences, through constraints, rights or duties. Kant is the classic case. | [9](09-kant.md) |
| **Difference principle** | — | Rawls's principle that social and economic inequalities are just only if they benefit the least advantaged. | [12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md) |
| **Divine command theory** | — | The view that what makes an act right or wrong is God's command or will. Faces the *Euthyphro* dilemma. | [4](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md) |
| **Doctrine of double effect** | — | The principle, derived from Aquinas, that it may be permissible to bring about a bad effect as a foreseen side effect of a good act when it would be wrong to intend it as an end or a means. | [2](02-moral-reasoning.md), [19](19-bioethics.md) |
| **Dualism of practical reason** | — | Sidgwick's name for the unresolved conflict between rational egoism and utilitarianism, both of which seemed to him self-evident. | [10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md), [22](22-building-your-ethics.md) |
| **Dukkha** | Pali *dukkha* | Suffering, unsatisfactoriness: the first of the Buddha's Four Noble Truths. Its cause is craving (*taṇhā*). | [7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md) |
| **Effective altruism** | — | A movement and research program, associated with Singer, Ord and MacAskill, that asks how to do the most good with given resources, using evidence and careful reasoning. | [11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md) |
| **Egoism, ethical** | — | The normative view that each person ought to pursue only his or her own interest. | [3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md) |
| **Egoism, psychological** | — | The descriptive view that people always do in fact act from self-interest. Butler's reply: self-love presupposes particular desires for things other than one's own happiness. | [3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md) |
| **Emotivism** | — | The metaethical view (Ayer, Stevenson) that moral judgments express emotions and seek to influence others, rather than stating facts. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Equipoise, clinical** | — | Freedman's condition for an ethical clinical trial: honest disagreement among expert clinicians about which treatment is better. | [19](19-bioethics.md) |
| **Error theory** | — | Mackie's view that moral judgments purport to state objective facts but that there are no such facts, so all positive moral claims are false. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Eudaimonia** | Greek, "having a good guardian spirit" | Happiness or flourishing: for Aristotle, the chief good, an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life. | [5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md) |
| **Euthyphro dilemma** | — | Plato's question: is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods? Restated for ethics: is an act right because God commands it, or does God command it because it is right? | [4](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md) |
| **Evolutionary debunking** | — | Arguments (notably Street's Darwinian dilemma) that because natural selection shaped our moral attitudes, realists cannot explain why those attitudes would track independent moral truths. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Expressivism** | — | The metaethical view that moral judgments express non-cognitive attitudes or plans, combined (in Blackburn and Gibbard) with a program to earn talk of moral truth and knowledge. See quasi-realism. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Felicific calculus** | Latin *felix*, "happy" | Bentham's method for measuring pleasures and pains by intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity and extent. | [10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md) |
| **Frankfurt case** | — | A scenario in which a person could not have done otherwise but acts on her own and seems responsible, used against the principle of alternate possibilities. | [18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md) |
| **Genealogy** | German *Genealogie* | Nietzsche's method of revealing the historical and psychological origins of moral values to call their value into question. | [15](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md) |
| **Golden mean** | Greek *mesotēs* | Aristotle's doctrine that each virtue of character is a disposition to feel and act in the intermediate way, relative to us, between excess and deficiency. Not a counsel of moderation in everything. | [5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md) |
| **Hedonism** | Greek *hēdonē*, "pleasure" | As a theory of value, the view that pleasure is the only intrinsic good and pain the only intrinsic evil. Epicurus, Bentham and (with qualitative distinctions) Mill. | [6](06-hellenistic-ethics.md), [10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md) |
| **Hypothetical imperative** | German *hypothetischer Imperativ* | A command that binds only given some end you have: "if you want X, do Y." Foot argued that moral oughts may be of this kind. | [9](09-kant.md), [13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md) |
| **Impartial spectator** | — | Adam Smith's imagined, well-informed and disinterested observer, whose sympathy is the standard of moral judgment and the voice of conscience. | [8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md) |
| **Integrity objection** | — | Williams's charge that utilitarianism alienates agents from their own projects and commitments, treating them as channels for the general good (George and Jim). | [11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md) |
| **Internalism (motivational)** | — | The view that sincerely judging an act right necessarily involves some motivation to do it. Contrasted with externalism. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Is–ought gap** | — | Hume's observation that writers move from "is" to "ought" without explaining how; often taken to show that no evaluative conclusion follows from purely factual premises. | [8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md), [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Jian ai** | Chinese 兼愛 | "Impartial caring" or "inclusive concern": Mozi's principle that one should care for others as for oneself and one's own. | [7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md) |
| **Junzi** | Chinese 君子 | Originally "son of a lord"; in Confucius, the exemplary person, noble by character rather than birth. | [7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md) |
| **Kingdom of ends** | German *Reich der Zwecke* | Kant's ideal of a community of rational beings, each both lawgiver and subject, all treated as ends in themselves. | [9](09-kant.md) |
| **Land ethic** | — | Leopold's ethic that enlarges the moral community to include soils, waters, plants and animals: "the land." | [20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md) |
| **Li** | Chinese 禮 | Ritual propriety: the rites, manners and customs through which Confucian virtue is expressed and cultivated. | [7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md) |
| **Longtermism** | — | The view that positively influencing the long-term future is a key moral priority of our time. | [11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md) |
| **Master and slave morality** | German *Herren-Moral*, *Sklaven-Moral* | Nietzsche's contrast between a morality of "good and bad" created by the noble out of self-affirmation and a morality of "good and evil" created by the weak out of *ressentiment*. | [15](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md) |
| **Metaethics** | — | The study of the nature, meaning, metaphysics, knowledge and psychology of morality itself: what, if anything, makes moral claims true. | [1](01-what-is-ethics.md), [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Moral luck** | — | The way factors outside an agent's control (outcomes, circumstances, constitution) seem to affect how we judge them (Williams, Nagel). | [13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md) |
| **Moral particularism** | — | Dancy's view that the moral relevance of a feature can change from case to case, so morality cannot be captured in principles. | [2](02-moral-reasoning.md) |
| **Moral sense** | — | In Shaftesbury and Hutcheson, an inner faculty by which we perceive and approve virtue, as the eye perceives color. | [8](08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md) |
| **Moral uncertainty** | — | The field asking how to act when you are unsure which moral theory is true (MacAskill, Bykvist and Ord). | [22](22-building-your-ethics.md) |
| **Natural law** | Latin *lex naturalis* | For Aquinas, the rational creature's participation in the eternal law, knowable by reason and grounded in basic human goods; revived by Grisez and Finnis. | [4](04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md) |
| **Naturalistic fallacy** | — | Moore's name for identifying goodness with any natural property, such as pleasure. See open question argument. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Nomos and physis** | Greek, "convention" and "nature" | The Sophists' contrast between what is right by human custom and what is right by nature. | [3](03-relativism-and-egoism.md) |
| **Non-identity problem** | — | Parfit's puzzle: a choice that makes future people worse off than others might have been may harm no one, since those people would otherwise not have existed. | [11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md) |
| **Normative ethics** | — | The study of what we ought to do and how we ought to live: the domain of the major moral theories. | [1](01-what-is-ethics.md) |
| **Oikeiōsis** | Greek, "appropriation," "making one's own" | The Stoic account of moral development, from an animal's concern for itself outward to family, city and all humanity. | [6](06-hellenistic-ethics.md) |
| **Open question argument** | — | Moore's argument that for any natural property N, "Is N good?" remains an open question, so "good" cannot mean N. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Original position** | — | Rawls's hypothetical choice situation, in which parties behind a veil of ignorance select principles of justice. | [12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md) |
| **Phronēsis** | Greek | Practical wisdom: Aristotle's intellectual virtue of deliberating well about what is good for human beings, without which no virtue of character is complete. | [5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md) |
| **Prescriptivism** | — | Hare's metaethics: moral judgments are universalizable prescriptions, a species of imperative. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Prima facie duty** | Latin, "at first appearance" | Ross's term for a duty that holds unless outweighed by a stronger one in the situation (fidelity, gratitude, non-maleficence and others). | [17](17-metaethics.md), [19](19-bioethics.md) |
| **Principlism** | — | Beauchamp and Childress's framework of four mid-level principles for bioethics: respect for autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence and justice. | [19](19-bioethics.md) |
| **Quasi-realism** | — | Blackburn's program of showing that an expressivist can legitimately say everything the realist says: that moral claims are true, known and mind-independent. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Reactive attitudes** | — | P. F. Strawson's term for resentment, indignation, gratitude and the like: the attitudes that make up our practice of holding one another responsible. | [18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md) |
| **Realism (moral)** | — | The view that some moral claims are true in virtue of facts that do not depend on anyone's attitudes. Naturalist (Railton, Boyd) and non-naturalist (Moore, Parfit, Enoch) versions. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Reflective equilibrium** | — | Rawls's method of adjusting principles and considered judgments against each other until they cohere; "wide" equilibrium also brings in background theories (Daniels). | [1](01-what-is-ethics.md) |
| **Ren** | Chinese 仁 | Humaneness, benevolence: the Confucian supreme virtue, the full realization of being human in relationships. | [7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md) |
| **Repugnant conclusion** | — | Parfit's result that total utilitarianism implies a huge population with lives barely worth living could be better than a smaller, very happy one. | [11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md) |
| **Responsibility gap** | — | The worry that when autonomous systems cause harm no human may be properly responsible (Matthias, Sparrow). | [21](21-technology-and-ai-ethics.md) |
| **Ressentiment** | French, used by Nietzsche | The rancor of the powerless, which, Nietzsche argued, became creative and gave birth to values in the slave revolt in morality. | [15](15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md) |
| **Rule consequentialism** | — | The view that an act is right if it is permitted by the code whose general acceptance would have the best consequences (Hooker). | [10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md), [22](22-building-your-ethics.md) |
| **Shu** | Chinese 恕 | Reciprocity or "putting oneself in the other's place": the Confucian negative Golden Rule, "what you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others." | [7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md) |
| **Situationism** | — | The challenge, from social psychology, that behavior depends more on situations than on robust character traits (Harman, Doris). | [13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md), [18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md) |
| **Social intuitionism** | — | Haidt's model on which moral judgments are caused by quick intuitions, with reasoning usually coming afterward to justify them. | [18](18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md) |
| **Speciesism** | — | Singer's term (borrowed from Richard Ryder) for giving less weight to the interests of beings because they are not of our species. | [20](20-animals-and-the-environment.md) |
| **Supererogation** | Latin *supererogare*, "to pay out beyond" | Acts that are good to do but beyond the call of duty. Consequentialism seems to leave little room for them. | [11](11-contemporary-consequentialism.md) |
| **Supervenience** | — | The claim that there can be no moral difference without some natural difference. Realists of every kind must explain why it holds. | [17](17-metaethics.md) |
| **Thick concepts** | — | Concepts such as cruel, brave and treacherous that combine description and evaluation, contrasted with thin ones such as good and right (Williams). | [13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md), [22](22-building-your-ethics.md) |
| **Ubuntu** | Nguni *ubuntu*, "humanness" | The southern African ethic summed up as "a person is a person through other persons"; Metz reconstructs it as a theory of harmonious relationships. | [7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md) |
| **Utilitarianism** | — | The consequentialist theory that the right act maximizes total well-being (classically, pleasure minus pain), each person counting for one (Bentham, Mill, Sidgwick). | [10](10-classical-utilitarianism.md) |
| **Veil of ignorance** | — | The feature of Rawls's original position that hides from the parties their own place in society, talents and conception of the good. | [12](12-contractualism-and-justice.md) |
| **Virtue** | Greek *aretē*; Latin *virtus* | Excellence. In ethics, a stable disposition of character, including feeling and perception, that makes its possessor good and lets her act well. | [5](05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md), [13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md) |
| **V-rules** | — | Hursthouse's rules derived from the virtues and vices ("do what is honest," "do not do what is cruel"), her reply to the charge that virtue ethics gives no guidance. | [13](13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md) |
| **Wu wei** | Chinese 無為 | "Non-action" or effortless action: the Daoist ideal of acting in accord with the Way without strain or contrivance. | [7](07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md) |
